# Chapter 11 - Never Rent a Getaway Car

**He rented the getaway car under a fake name, and the fake name is exactly
why the car's testimony could never be thrown out.**

At 4:38 on the afternoon of March 1, 2016, Aly Sunderani stepped out of his
Range Rover under the carport of the Comfort Inn in Guelph, Ontario. He was the
hotel's manager; this was his workplace; it was broad daylight.

A gunman walked up to him and opened fire with a submachine gun. Sunderani
suffered thirty-one gunshot wounds, some from bullets that passed through him
and struck again, three of them in his heart. The shooter dropped the gun
beside the body, climbed into a waiting black Infiniti QX60, and was gone.

No masked figure in the night. No hidden body. A public execution in front of a
hotel lobby, on camera. And yet the case that followed was entirely
circumstantial, because the one thing the cameras never captured was the
gunman's face.

What the Crown had instead, when Raja Dosanjh stood trial for first-degree
murder three years later, were four pillars. His DNA on the grip of the
submachine gun left at the scene; a mixed profile 3.7 million times more
likely to include him than not. The getaway QX60, rented from a Mississauga
agency just hours before the shooting by a man using the false name "Jaspinder
Nagra"; a man who was, undisputedly, Dosanjh. The rental agency's cameras,
which caught that man's pants, shirt, and shoes; the same clothing worn by the
gunman captured chest-down on the hotel's video.

And the fourth pillar: the car itself had been keeping a diary.

## The minute-by-minute confession

The QX60 came equipped with an infotainment system, the touchscreen console
that runs the maps and the music, which, like most modern vehicles, quietly
and automatically logged everywhere the car went. When Ontario Provincial
Police forensic analysts extracted the tracklog, they could read the day of the
murder like an itinerary:

**1:16 p.m.** The QX60 pulls out of the rental lot in Mississauga.
**1:28 p.m.** It arrives at the home of the parents of Dalvir Passi,
Dosanjh's brother-in-law. It stays eleven minutes.
**3:52 p.m.** It enters the Comfort Inn parking lot in Guelph and parks
behind the hotel, where it sits for the better part of an hour.
**4:38 p.m.** The exact minute Aly Sunderani is shot, the QX60 moves to the
front of the hotel, then immediately drives away.

From there the diary continues: back to the Passi parents' home, then on to the
home of Dosanjh's sister and brother-in-law. The hotel's surveillance video
confirmed the tracklog's account frame for frame.

One more entry completed the portrait. The next evening, Dosanjh returned the
QX60 to the rental agency, and asked his brother-in-law to pick him up not at
the agency, but waiting in a gas-station parking lot across a busy street. A
small, careful separation between himself and the car. The kind of detail a
man arranges when he knows exactly what the car has been part of.

## The two bald men

The defence answered with an alternate suspect story, delivered by the
brother-in-law himself. Passi testified that after the rental, two tall, white,
bald or balding strangers, men he had never seen before, arrived at his
parents' home, borrowed the QX60 from Dosanjh to complete a drug transaction,
and brought it back later that day. While the Infiniti was allegedly off
murdering a hotel manager, Passi and Dosanjh were, he said, playing video
games.

The story had one genuine asset: the Comfort Inn's front-desk manager, who
watched the shooting through the lobby window, described the gunman as fair,
white-complexioned, with "less hairs on his head." Dosanjh's own lawyer
described his client as a tall, brown-skinned young man with a full head of
dark hair. Two other eyewitnesses pointed the other way.

The jury weighed the bald strangers against the DNA on the gun grip, the
clothing on two videotapes, and a tracklog that put the car Dosanjh rented at
the murder scene at the killing minute. On August 1, 2019, it convicted him of
first-degree murder.

## The trap inside the fake name

On appeal, Dosanjh's lawyers went after the diary. The police, they argued,
had searched the infotainment system unlawfully, rummaging through GPS data
that revealed his movements, a "biographical core" of personal information
protected by section 8 of the Charter. If the search was unconstitutional, the
tracklog could be excluded, and the fourth pillar would fall.

The Court of Appeal for Ontario's answer, delivered by Associate Chief Justice
Fairburn in October 2022, is the dark joke at the heart of this chapter.
Privacy claims belong to people with a legitimate connection to the thing
searched; through ownership, control, a right to be where they were. Dosanjh's
connection to the QX60 was a rental contract signed in the name of a man who
did not exist. The GPS data, the court noted, came from a vehicle he had "no
right to be driving"; the synced contact list wasn't even his phone's. Tracking
data, the court added, sits far down the privacy spectrum to begin with; it
reveals where a car went, not the contents of a mind. He had no reasonable
expectation of privacy, and therefore no standing to complain; and even if the
search had breached the Charter, the evidence was reliable, the breach minimal,
and it would have been admitted anyway.

The fake name was supposed to make the car untraceable to him. Instead it made
the car's memory untouchable by him. He had disowned the Infiniti so
thoroughly that when it testified, he had no legal voice to object.

The appeal was dismissed.

## How'd he get caught?

He planned everything except the passenger seat. The false identity, the
borrowed-strangers story, the gas station across the street; all of it was
built to keep Raja Dosanjh's name off the getaway car. But the car didn't need
his name. It logged the rental lot, the family home, the hotel, and the murder
minute all on its own, his DNA rode the gun grip, and the same cameras that
never saw his face remembered his shoes. The modern getaway car is the worst
accomplice in crime: it goes everywhere you go, remembers everything, and owes
you nothing; especially if you were never really its driver.
