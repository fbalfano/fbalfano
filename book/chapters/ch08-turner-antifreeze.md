# Chapter 8 - The Sweet Taste of Antifreeze

**Her husband died of a sudden illness in 1995, and the doctors called it his
heart. Six years later her boyfriend died of the exact same sudden illness. It
was only the second death that made anyone test the first.**

Ethylene glycol, the toxic heart of ordinary automobile antifreeze, has one
property that makes it a poisoner's temptation: it tastes sweet. Sweet enough to
lace a food and go unnoticed, sweet enough that pets and children sometimes drink
it by accident and die. It attacks the body quietly, producing symptoms a harried
emergency room can easily mistake for a bad flu, and then it kills. Julia Lynn
Turner appears to have understood all of this, and she is in this chapter because
she made one mistake that a clever poisoner is uniquely prone to: she found a
method that worked, and she could not resist using it twice.

The first to die was her husband, Glenn Turner, a Cobb County, Georgia police
officer. In early 1995 the marriage was failing; Glenn had decided to move out,
and had set the weekend of March 3 to go. He never made it. A few days before his
planned departure he fell suddenly, violently ill, nauseated and flu-like, and
went to a hospital. The staff gave him fluids for dehydration and something for
the nausea and sent him home. The next morning, Julia served him green Jell-O, a
dish whose color and sweetness would hide the taste and appearance of anything
mixed into it. She fed him, left him alone in the house, and within a few hours
Glenn Turner was dead.

His death was attributed to natural causes, something heart-related, the kind of
sudden cardiac event that occasionally takes a man in his prime and surprises
everyone. Julia collected from his estate and, as the primary beneficiary of his
insurance policies, from his death benefits. Four days after the funeral, she
moved in with a man named Randy Thompson, with whom she had been having an affair
while Glenn was still alive.

The case, at that point, was closed. There was no case. A grieving widow, a
tragic heart attack, a fresh start. If Julia Lynn Turner had stopped there, she
would very likely have gotten away with murder.

## The same script, six years later

She did not stop there. Six years later, the debts had piled up again, and Randy
Thompson had become, like Glenn before him, more useful dead than alive.

What happened to Thompson is uncanny in its sameness, and the sameness is the
whole story. Three days before he died, he spent the evening with Julia. He fell
suddenly, violently ill, flu-like, and went to the emergency room, where he was
treated and discharged, just as Glenn had been. Julia fed him Jell-O. He was found
dead the next morning. And Julia Turner, carrying substantial debt, collected over
thirty thousand dollars as the beneficiary of Randy Thompson's life insurance.

A widow whose husband dies of a sudden mystery illness is a tragedy. A woman whose
husband *and* whose next partner both die of the identical sudden mystery illness,
both after a night with her, both after a bowl of Jell-O, both leaving her an
insurance check, is not a tragedy. She is a pattern. And a pattern is precisely
what forensic science is built to see.

## The poison that pointed backward

This time, someone tested. Samples taken from Randy Thompson's body were analyzed,
and they contained ethylene glycol. A Georgia Bureau of Investigation medical
examiner identified the cause of death as ethylene glycol toxicity, a rare form of
poisoning, the unmistakable signature of antifreeze in a human body.

And here is where the second murder reached back and exposed the first. A rare
poison in Randy Thompson was a scream loud enough to make investigators turn and
look again at the man who had died the same way six years earlier. Biological
samples had been preserved from Glenn Turner's original 1995 autopsy. They were
retested. Then Cobb County authorities exhumed Glenn's body, and additional tests
were performed, and they confirmed it: ethylene glycol. The sudden heart attack
that had closed the book on Glenn Turner in 1995 had never been a heart attack at
all. It had been antifreeze, served sweet, and it had gone undetected for six
years because no one had any reason to test a grieving widow's dead husband for
automotive coolant.

At trial for Glenn's murder, the prosecution was allowed to put Randy Thompson's
death before the jury as a similar transaction, evidence not that she should be
punished for Thompson directly, but that the near-perfect duplication of method,
symptoms, food, and financial motive made the poisoning of Glenn no accident and
no coincidence. Julia Lynn Turner was convicted of the malice murder of her
husband, and in 2007 the Supreme Court of Georgia affirmed. *(She would die in
prison in 2010, a detail from the case's later history rather than this opinion.)*

## How'd she get caught?

She was caught by her own encore. Julia Turner had, in Glenn's death, executed
something close to a perfect murder: a sweet, hard-to-detect poison that mimicked
natural illness, administered in a food built to hide it, resulting in a death no
one thought to question and an insurance payout no one begrudged a widow. The
poison did its job and then vanished from the story, because forensic toxicology
only finds what it is told to look for, and no one told it to look. Had she
retired on that success, the green Jell-O would have kept its secret. But a killer
who has found a method that works comes to trust it, and Julia ran the identical
play on Randy Thompson six years later, right down to the emergency room and the
dessert. The second antifreeze death was the one nobody could wave off as
coincidence, and the instant a lab named the poison in Thompson, it named the
poison in Glenn. She was not undone by a single fatal error. She was undone by
doing the same thing twice, and trusting that lightning would be mistaken for
nature a second time.
