# Chapter 14 - The Blind Photo From Inside a Pocket

**Somewhere on the drive to a robbery that would end in murder, a phone in
someone's pocket took a picture of the inside of that pocket. No one meant to
take it. It was pure darkness. And it was stamped with the time and the place.**

Modern phones are always half-awake, and every so often, jostled in a pocket or
a bag, one of them fires the camera at nothing and saves a black rectangle no
human eye will ever want to see. Usually these accidental photos are deleted with
a shrug. In the case of Melissa Zielinski, one of them helped place her near the
scene of a killing, because a photo of nothing is still a photo of *somewhere*,
at *some time*, and a phone records both whether you asked it to or not.

On August 27, 2020, Karl Henderson was robbed and fatally shot in the home he
shared with his father in Lino Lakes, Minnesota. Henderson's father came home to
find a man and a woman walking up from the basement, the man carrying a large
object. He told them to stop. They kept walking, and left. When he went down to
the basement, he found his son shot on the floor. The large object the man had
been carrying was a safe, the safe in which Karl Henderson was known to keep as
much as fifty thousand dollars.

## A plan with a paper trail

Melissa Zielinski needed money to buy a house, and the evidence showed she had
Henderson's name, his number, and a plan, worked out with a man named Nicholas,
to rob him. Like so many people in this book, she left that plan lying around in
the one place people forget is permanent: her phone.

The day before the robbery, she texted her own son that "we are going to collect
some money that's owed to me hopefully." Her son, reading the situation more
clearly than perhaps she intended, replied, "So you and Nick are going to muscle
some money." Zielinski's answer has the ring of a woman talking herself into
something: "Hopefully. I've waited long enough." In the days *after* the killing,
her phone kept confessing. Her internet history filled with searches about
SentrySafe brand safes, the very brand she and Nicholas had hauled out of the
basement and now needed to crack open. And she visited a Star Tribune news article
whose headline told her exactly how much trouble she was in: "Shooter left murder
weapon next to body in Lino Lakes home."

Against that backdrop came the accidental photograph. Investigators searching her
phone found an image taken, apparently by mistake, from inside a pocket, a
picture of nothing at all. But the phone had geotagged it, silently recording the
GPS coordinates where it was snapped, and those coordinates placed the phone about
a nine-minute drive from the crime scene, at around the time of the murder. Nobody
posed for it. Nobody knew it existed. It was the purest kind of evidence, a
witness that could not be coached because it did not know it had seen anything.

## The honest weight of it

It would make a tidier story to say the pocket photo cracked the case open, and
this book has told plenty of stories where a single stray thread did exactly that.
Zielinski's is not quite one of them, and the Minnesota Supreme Court was candid
about why, in a way worth borrowing.

The accidental photo was not the only thing placing Zielinski near the killing;
it was, if anything, the least of it. Cell-phone location data obtained by warrant
from her carrier showed her phone travelling with Nicholas's phone to the murder
scene at the time of the murder and returning together afterward, a far more
complete account than one blind snapshot. Her DNA was found on the very safe
stolen from Henderson's home. A friend testified to helping her break into it. The
"I've waited long enough" texts supplied motive and intent; the safe searches and
the news-article visit supplied consciousness of guilt. As the court put it, even
without the accidental pocket photo, the state's case
overwhelmingly proved her guilt.

That candor is the point, not a weakness. The accidental photo is memorable
precisely because it is so absurdly, mechanically honest, a phone betraying its
owner by taking a picture of the inside of her own coat, and the strength of the
case did not have to rest on it because everything else, the phone that travelled
with the killers and the DNA on the stolen safe, was already saying the same
thing. Melissa Zielinski was convicted of first-degree intentional murder. On
appeal, the Minnesota Supreme Court affirmed that conviction, correcting only a
technical problem, the entry of a duplicate second-degree murder conviction for
the same killing, which it reversed.

## How'd she get caught?

She was caught the way most people in the smartphone age are caught: by a device
that documented her whole day and had no loyalty to her at all. Melissa Zielinski
planned a robbery in texts, researched the safe in her search bar, read about the
murder in the news, and carried, through all of it, a phone that logged where she
went as faithfully as it logged what she typed. And at some point, unbidden, that
phone reached into its own pocket and took a photograph of the dark, stamping it
with a time and a place near a dying man. She never took that picture; her pocket
took it. But it did not matter who pressed the button, because the machine was
keeping the record either way, and the record did not care that no one was
looking.
