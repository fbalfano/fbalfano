# Chapter 31 - Mr. Big

**The police built a fake criminal empire, flew a lonely man across the country,
and spent four hundred thousand dollars, all to get him to confess to drowning
his two little girls. Then the Supreme Court asked whether any of it could be
believed.**

Some chapters in this book end with a wink at a criminal who outsmarted himself.
This one does not. This one ends with a question that has no comfortable answer,
and that is exactly why it belongs here.

On the morning of August 4, 2002, Nelson Hart drove his three-year-old twin
daughters, Karen and Krista, to a park near their home in Gander, Newfoundland,
to play on the swings. A lake lay beside the park. Somewhere between thirty and
forty-five minutes later, Hart came bursting back into his house alone, in a
panic, and told his wife that Krista had fallen into the water. His wife asked the
only question that mattered, the one that still hangs over the whole case: where
is Karen?

He had left her at the park.

By the time the first responders reached the lake, both little girls were in the
water, floating several hundred metres apart. There was nothing anyone could do.

Hart's account of that morning never held together. He told police he couldn't
swim, so rather than trying to reach his drowning daughter he ran to his car and
drove home to fetch his wife, leaving the other child alone on the dock beside the
water. There were two cell phones in his car; he called no one. He passed a
restaurant and a hospital; he stopped at neither. Two weeks later, after an
eight-hour interrogation led nowhere, he changed his story, now recalling an
epileptic seizure at the park, a foggy return to his senses, one daughter visible
in the water, and a single blind instinct to drive home. He had lied at first, he
said, because he was afraid of losing his driver's licence.

The police were certain he had drowned his daughters. Certainty, though, is not
evidence, and evidence was the one thing they did not have. The investigation went
cold.

What they did to revive it made Canadian legal history twice over. Once when it
appeared to work, and once when the Supreme Court of Canada looked hard at what
"working" had actually cost.

## Building a world around a lonely man

The technique is called a "Mr. Big" operation, a Canadian invention in which
undercover officers construct an entire fictitious criminal organization around a
suspect, draw him in, befriend him, pay him, and finally sit him down with the
crime boss, where the price of admission to the family is a full and honest
account of his past.

Nelson Hart was, from the police perspective, an ideal candidate, and it is
precisely that suitability that makes the case so uneasy. Surveillance in late
2004 found a man living on social assistance and almost entirely alone. He rarely
left his home, and when he did, his wife came with him. He had, in other words,
nothing, which is to say he had everything to be offered.

In February 2005 an undercover officer the judgment calls "Jim" approached Hart
outside a convenience store and asked for help finding his missing sister. Hart
obliged and was handed fifty dollars. Within days he had a job driving for Jim's
supposed trucking company. Within months the fictitious organization had become
the whole of his life. Over four months Hart took part in sixty-three staged
"scenarios," ferried mysterious packages across Newfoundland, and travelled to
Halifax, Montreal, Ottawa, Toronto, and Vancouver, staying in hotels and dining
in some of the finest restaurants in the country, so far outside his old world
that an officer had to quietly teach him how to conduct himself at the table. He
was paid $15,720. He came to regard the undercover officers as his closest
friends, his brothers. The final tab for the operation, borne by the public, came
to $413,268, a figure worth holding in mind when the question of reliability
arrives, because it is a great deal of money to spend building a stage.

The organization preached loyalty and honesty and stitched a thread of menace
through it all; at one point Hart watched Jim strike another "member" for
loose talk. Then came the lure. A big score was coming, worth twenty to
twenty-five thousand dollars to Hart himself. In Toronto they let him hold and
count $175,000 in cash, a taste of the future on offer. But there was a snag, Jim
confided in early June: the boss had run a background check and turned up a
problem, and Hart could not advance until it was resolved.

## The meeting with the boss

On June 9, 2005, Nelson Hart finally met Mr. Big. He opened by thanking him,
telling the boss his life had turned around since he'd joined. Mr. Big turned the
conversation to the deaths of Hart's daughters, warned that "heat" might be
coming, and asked him, directly, why he had killed them.

Hart gave the same answer he had given the real police three years earlier: a
seizure, an accident. Mr. Big waved it away and told him not to lie. And after
more prodding, Hart confessed. He had killed the girls, he said, because he feared
Child Welfare was going to take them and hand them to his brother. Asked how, he
said he had struck his daughters with his shoulder and knocked them from the wharf
into the water.

Two days later, Jim drove Hart back to the park where Karen and Krista had died
and asked him to walk through it. Jim knelt on the dock, and Hart demonstrated,
nudging Jim with his knee, how he had pushed his daughters into the lake.

Hart was arrested on June 13 and charged with two counts of first-degree murder.
Given a single phone call from the station, he used it to call for help. He called
Jim.

## What the Supreme Court saw

A jury convicted Hart on the strength of those confessions. But in 2014 the
Supreme Court of Canada used his case to put the Mr. Big technique itself on
trial, and to rebuild the law around it.

The Court's worry was not delicacy. It was reliability, and the arithmetic of the
operation lays the worry bare. The sting had lifted a destitute, isolated man out
of poverty, given him the first real friends of his life, dangled a
twenty-five-thousand-dollar payday, and then made one thing unmistakable: the road
to keeping all of it ran directly through telling Mr. Big what Mr. Big wanted to
hear. A confession purchased on those terms, the Court held, may be worth no more
than what was paid for it, and a jury that has watched months of a man playacting
as a gangster may end up convicting the character rather than weighing the proof.

So the Court laid down a new rule. Mr. Big confessions are now presumptively
inadmissible. The Crown must show the confession's probative value outweighs its
prejudicial effect, with reliability measured against the inducements offered and,
above all, against whether the confession produced anything that could actually be
checked, a body, a weapon, a fact only the killer could know. Behind that stands a
second guard, the abuse-of-process doctrine, for operations that overwhelm the
will through violence or threat.

Measured against its own new standard, Hart's confessions failed. They had been
bought with powerful inducements, they contradicted themselves in places, and,
unlike the confession in the companion case *R. v. Mack* (Chapter 32), where the
suspect led the fake gangsters to real human remains, they were confirmed by
nothing at all. The appeal was dismissed and the confessions were excluded. With
them gone, the Crown's case fell away; reporting at the time indicates the charges
were eventually withdrawn and Hart walked free. *(That final coda comes from news
coverage, not from the judgment itself.)*

## How'd he get caught, and should he have been?

He was caught the way a fish is caught, by the patient construction of an entire
ocean around him. The confession was real in the narrow sense that the words came
out of his own mouth, on the very dock where his daughters had died. What the
Supreme Court forces every reader to confront is whether the words were *true*, or
whether a poor, isolated, epileptic man simply said the only thing that would keep
the first family he had ever known from casting him out. This is the one chapter
in the book with no clean ending and no tidy lesson, and that is the point. Every
other story here asks how the killer got caught. Nelson Hart's asks what "caught"
is even worth when the state builds, at a cost of four hundred thousand dollars,
the entire world in which the confession is spoken. It is the reason every Mr. Big
confession in Canada must now pass through the gate this case built.
