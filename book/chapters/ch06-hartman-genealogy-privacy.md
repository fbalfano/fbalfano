# Chapter 6 - No Privacy in the DNA Your Cousins Share

**A man tried to throw out the evidence that convicted him of a child's murder by
arguing the police had violated his privacy. The court's answer draws the line
that makes genetic genealogy possible: the DNA you share with your relatives is
not yours alone to hide.**

The last chapter showed genetic genealogy catching a killer. This one shows the
courts deciding whether it is allowed to, because a technique that finds you
through your relatives' DNA raises a question the law had never quite faced: whose
privacy is it, anyway? The answer, worked out in the case of a Tacoma murder,
is the legal foundation the whole method rests on.

In 1986, a twelve-year-old girl the court identifies only as MW was raped and
murdered in a Tacoma, Washington park. Her killer left semen on her body. As with
so many of these cases, the biological evidence was complete and the killer was
nobody, an unmatched profile that sat untouched while the girl's family grew old
without an answer.

Then, in 2018, came the same quiet revolution that undid the killer in the last
chapter. Police enlisted the DNA technology company Parabon Nanolabs to take the
killer's genetic profile and upload it to GEDmatch, the consumer database, in
search of partial matches to his relatives. They did not get a warrant to do it.
Parabon found that several of the killer's cousins had DNA in the database, built
the family trees, and narrowed the search to a man named Hartman. Investigators
then collected a napkin bearing his discarded DNA, and it matched the semen left
on MW's body more than three decades earlier.

## The line the court drew

Hartman fought back with the tool the Constitution gives every defendant: a motion
to suppress. If the police had violated his rights in finding him, the DNA match,
and everything that flowed from it, could be thrown out as fruit of the poisonous
tree. His argument went to the heart of genetic genealogy. Comparing the
crime-scene DNA against the GEDmatch database, he said, had rummaged through his
private affairs without a warrant, in violation of the Washington Constitution's
robust privacy protections. He claimed a privacy interest in the DNA his relatives
had uploaded, and, eventually, in the very semen the killer had left at the scene.

The Court of Appeals affirmed his conviction, and in doing so it laid down the
principle that lets investigators do what they did to catch him. There is, the
court held, no privacy interest in the DNA a *relative* voluntarily uploads to a
public database. When your cousin spits in a tube and posts the results to find
long-lost family, the genetic information you happen to share with that cousin is
theirs to disclose, not yours to suppress. You have no standing to object to a
search of a database you never contributed to, conducted on data someone else
freely gave. And as for the DNA the killer left on his victim, that had been
abandoned at the scene, discarded in the act of the crime, and a person retains no
privacy interest in what he leaves behind on a murdered child.

Strip the legal language away and the ruling says something simple and, for
killers, terrifying. The genetic trail that leads to you does not run only through
your own choices. It runs through your entire extended family, every one of whom
has the right to explore their heritage, and none of whom needs your permission to
do it. Your DNA is not a private diary you can lock. It is a shared inheritance,
copies of it scattered across cousins you have never met, and any one of them can,
without knowing it, leave the door open.

## And it holds up

If Hartman set the constitutional floor, cases like it have shown the technique is
no fluke of one jurisdiction. In California, a former Marine named Horace Van
Vaultz Jr. was linked by the same method to two cold-case killings, the 1981
strangulation of Selena Keough and the murder of Mary Duggan. Sperm recovered from
Keough's body in the 1981 investigation, analyzed decades later, matched DNA found
on a gag in Duggan's mouth, tying the two crimes to one man, and investigative
genetic genealogy gave that man a name. It was Los Angeles County's first
prosecution built on the technique. A jury convicted Van Vaultz, and in 2024 the
California Court of Appeal affirmed, describing the DNA evidence as overwhelming.

Two states, the same story: a killer who evaded the science of his own era, found
decades later through the DNA of his relatives, and convicted, with the courts
signing off on the method that caught him. The cold cases that used to defeat
police, the ones with perfect DNA and no name to attach it to, are cold no longer.
The database caught up.

## How'd he get caught?

Hartman was caught by the same thing Talbott was, his family's DNA, and then he
was kept caught by a court that told him he had no right to hide behind it. This
is the chapter where the law blesses the technique, and the blessing rests on a
truth most people have never stopped to consider: you do not own your DNA in the
way you own your secrets, because you did not get it alone. Half of it came from
each parent, quarters from grandparents, fractions scattered outward to cousins in
an ever-widening circle, and every one of those people can place their share in a
database where a detective can find the family resemblance and follow it home to
you. The killers in these cases all made the same bet, that the decades had buried
them. What they never counted on was that the people who share their blood would
one day, cheerfully and voluntarily, dig them back up.
