# Introduction - The Smoking Gun Is Rarely a Gun

**Almost no one is caught the way the movies promise. There is seldom a witness who
points across the courtroom, seldom a confession that comes apart in a single
brilliant question. What catches a killer, almost every time, is smaller and quieter
than that: the one thing he forgot he left behind.**

We imagine murder as a locked-room puzzle solved by a genius in the final act. The
truth is closer to accounting. A person who takes a life has to move through the
world before and after doing it, and the world keeps records. It always has. A
century ago those records were a muddy bootprint, a torn button, a hair caught on a
nail. What has changed, and what this book is really about, is that the records have
multiplied beyond anything a killer can keep track of. We now carry devices that log
where we stood and when. We strap machines to our wrists that count our steps and
our heartbeats. Our cars remember our speed and our route. Our phones photograph the
inside of our own pockets. And we type our worst questions into a search bar that
never forgets we asked.

The smoking gun, in other words, is rarely a gun. It is a cell tower that puts a
phone on a lonely road at the wrong hour. It is a fitness tracker that keeps counting
steps for a woman her husband has already reported dead. It is a bullet that clears
the man everyone saw firing and points, silently, at the shooter no one was watching.
It is a search for "what happens when you die," typed minutes after a murder by
someone checking his work. This book is a collection of those quiet, decisive
things, and of the people undone by them.

## Every case here is real, and you can check us

That is the promise this book makes and keeps on every page. Each chapter is built on
an actual court decision, from the Supreme Court of Canada, the provincial Courts of
Appeal, the Supreme Court of the United States, and the American state high courts.
These are not composites, and they are not "inspired by true events." They are true
events, tried in real courtrooms, reviewed by real appellate judges, and written down
in opinions anyone can read.

The storytelling is ours. The facts are the courts'. At the end of every chapter you
will find a box headed "Read the case yourself," and in it the case name, the
citation, and a free link. A citation looks forbidding until you learn to read it,
and it takes about ten seconds to learn. Something like *R. v. Arp*, 1998 CanLII 769
(SCC) simply means: the case of the Crown against a man named Arp, decided in 1998 by
the Supreme Court of Canada, and free to read on a website called CanLII. The
American ones work the same way, through free sites like Justia and CourtListener. We
tell you the case every single time for one reason: so that you never have to take
our word for any of it. Go look. That is the point.

Two small conventions follow from telling the truth. First, when a court ordered that
a victim or a witness be shielded, this book keeps that person to initials, exactly
as the decision does. Second, when a fact comes from the news rather than from the
decision itself, we say so, because the difference matters.

## The book is honest about the cases that did not end cleanly

If this were only a parade of clever captures, it would be a lie by omission. Real
forensic evidence is powerful, and real forensic evidence is sometimes wrong, or
overstated, or gathered in a way the law will not allow. So some of these chapters do
not end with the cell door clanging shut.

You will read about a man whose blood-spatter conviction was later undone when the
analyst who read the spatter was discredited. You will read about a confession the
police worked years to obtain, thrown out because they went too far to get it. You
will read about a black box in a car that recorded the whole truth of a fatal crash
and was silenced anyway, because the police skipped the warrant. And near the end you
will meet the innocent: men sent toward death row by bite-mark "science" that turned
out to be no science at all, and a fourteen-year-old boy convicted on a pathologist's
guess about the contents of a dead girl's stomach, a guess it took forty-eight years
and a study of blow flies to overturn. Those chapters are here on purpose. A book
about how evidence catches the guilty owes you the cases where it caught the wrong
person instead, and where a later, better science set them free.

Where a case was reopened, reversed, or retried, you will find it labeled plainly, in
the chapter and in the citation box. Nothing is smoothed over to make a neater story.

## How to read this book

Think of it as a set of shelves rather than a staircase. The chapters are grouped by
the kind of evidence that did the catching: what the body kept, in DNA and trace and
poison; the phone in your pocket, and everything it logs; what you typed and what you
saved; the devices you strap on and invite inside; the gun and the residue it leaves;
the staged scene and the physics that exposes it; the confession, the sting, the
wiretap, and the informant; and, last, the times the science lied, and the times it
set an innocent person free.

But nothing here depends on anything before it. Every chapter is a self-contained
short story, and you can read them in any order you like: front to back, or by
whichever kind of evidence interests you most, or simply by opening to a title that
catches your eye. If one chapter sends you toward another, it is an invitation, never
homework.

A word on tone, since these are grave matters. There is, now and then, a dark
comedy in how these people got caught, in the sheer avoidable stupidity of calling an
ex-girlfriend on a murdered couple's phone, or of asking a police officer how long
gunshot residue lasts on the hands. Where that comedy is honest, this book lets
itself smile at it. But the smile is only ever aimed at the killer's blunder, never
at the person who died. The dead in these pages were real people, and they are
treated that way.

They were also, most of them, killed by someone who was sure he had thought of
everything. He had not. He never does. Turn the page, and meet the first man who
handed the police the very evidence that hanged him, because they asked him nicely,
and he could not think of a reason to say no.
