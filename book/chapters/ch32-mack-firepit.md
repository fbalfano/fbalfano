# Chapter 32 — The Firepit

**He told the fake crime boss there was "nothing left" of his roommate. Then
he drove an undercover cop to the exact spot where the nothing was.**

Robert Levoir went missing from Fort McMurray, Alberta, in November 2002. He
had been living with a man named Dax Mack, and if you had asked Mack about
him, you'd have gotten a review: a "crackhead," a "drug addict," a liar and a
thief who stole from Mack's young son's piggy bank.

About a month after Levoir vanished, the police got a phone call. Jay Love, a
friend of Mack's, told them that Mack had confessed to killing Levoir and
burning his body. The confession had come at a bar on December 21, 2002: Mack,
drunk, told Love he was his best friend, asked whether he could trust him,
aired his grievances about the roommate — and then said "Robbie" was dead.
When Love asked if he'd "outsourced" it, Mack answered: *"No, I did it
myself."* Another friend, Michael Argueta, would later testify Mack said much
the same to him on a drive back to Fort McMurray.

So the police knew almost from the beginning. Knowing and proving are
different things. There was no body, no scene, no weapon tied to anything.
What they had was a drunk man's barroom boast, relayed secondhand. They
opened a two-track investigation: a wiretap on Mack's phone — and a Mr. Big
operation.

## Third line

In January 2004, an undercover officer the judgment calls "Ben" was introduced
to Mack at the Fort McMurray nightclub where Mack worked as a DJ. A week
later, Ben paid him $200 to help repossess a vehicle. Within days, Mack was
volunteering about his missing roommate — as far as he was concerned, Levoir
was "pushing up daisies."

Through January and February, Mack did jobs for Ben's organization, run by a
boss named "Liam." He picked up a package at an Edmonton bus depot; back at
the hotel, Ben opened it — $30,000 in cash. Ben spun stories of the
organization's muscle, including a "weekend of reckoning" when Liam "looked
after" two men who had crossed him, and probed at Mack's past: had he ever
beaten anyone? There was one time, Mack said, that he couldn't talk about.
Ben said he hoped one day Mack would share his secret. Weeks later, Ben
suggested the person in question wasn't "walking anymore." Mack nodded:
"every man has a breaking point."

Then came the moment that separates this case from every other Mr. Big story
in this book. In mid-March, Mack was summoned to Vancouver to meet Liam
himself. The boss raised the missing roommate directly. And Mack — offered the
chance to buy his way up the organization with the truth — *declined*. He
asked if he could refuse to discuss it. Liam said that was his choice, but
refusing meant staying on the organization's "third line." Mack held firm
anyway: *"loose lips sink ships."*

Remember that refusal. The Supreme Court of Canada certainly did.

## The firepit

Three weeks later, on April 9, 2004, Ben asked Mack whether he wanted work,
and whether he'd be willing to sit down with Liam to talk about how his
roommate was killed. This time Mack agreed — and then told Ben everything.
Levoir was "a liar, a thief, and a piece of shit drug dealer." Mack had shot
him five times with a .223 rifle — four in the chest, one in the back. There
was "nothing left" of him, because Mack had burned the body.

He offered to show Ben where. Then, within moments, he lost his nerve and
yanked it all back: everything he'd just said was "bullshit," he'd been
"lying." Ben replied that he "really hoped" Mack had been telling the truth.

And Mack changed his mind again — and drove the undercover officer to a
firepit on his father's rural property. He'd cleared out the ashes, he said.
Nothing left.

On April 15, flown to Edmonton for a second meeting with Liam, Mack repeated
the confession in the same detail: the .223, five shots, four chest and one
back, the "big fire" at his dad's place. Six days later he was arrested.
Police searched the father's property and found, in the firepit Mack had
personally pointed out, fragments of bone and teeth — identified as Robert
Levoir — and shell casings. The casings had been fired by a rifle recovered
from Mack's own apartment.

He had said there was nothing left. He was wrong in every way that mattered.

## Why this confession survived

Mack was convicted of first-degree murder, and in September 2014 — the same
year it rewrote the rules for Mr. Big stings in *R. v. Hart* (Chapter 31) —
a unanimous Supreme Court of Canada upheld his conviction.

Set the two cases side by side and the new law explains itself. The problem
with a Mr. Big confession is reliability: a man sufficiently bribed or
frightened may say anything. So the Court asks what the sting *paid* and what
the confession *produced*. Mack's inducements were modest — about $5,000 over
30 scenarios, no threats, no staged violence directed at him; and the proof
that the fear was manageable is that he had already said *no* to Mr. Big once
and kept his job. Against that stood confirmation of the rarest kind: the
confession led police to remains no one had found, in a firepit only the
killer could point to, salted with casings from his own rifle — and it matched
what he'd told two friends in 2002, before any undercover officer existed in
his life.

There was one more wrinkle: the wiretap. The Crown conceded its authorization
was defective and the interception of Mack's calls had violated the Charter;
none of the calls were used at trial. Mack argued the illegal wiretap was so
entangled with the Mr. Big operation that his confessions should fall with it.
The Court disagreed — the confessions were made to people Mack chose to speak
to, not harvested off the tapped line — and the evidence stood.

## How'd he get caught?

Twice, by his own mouth — and finally, by his own feet. The drunk boast to a
friend put his name in a police file within a month. The sting merely offered
him a world in which saying it again seemed safe. But what convicted Dax Mack
wasn't words at all: it was the walk to the firepit, the one act no innocent
man performs. Anyone can confess falsely. Only the killer can take you to the
bones.
