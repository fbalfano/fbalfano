# Chapter 32 - The Firepit

**He told the fake crime boss there was "nothing left" of his roommate. Then, to
prove it, he drove an undercover cop straight to the spot where the nothing was.**

Robert Levoir went missing from Fort McMurray, Alberta, in November 2002, and if
you'd asked his roommate about him, you'd have gotten less an expression of
concern than a review. Levoir was, in Dax Mack's telling, a "crackhead," a "drug
addict," a liar and a thief who stole from Mack's young son's piggy bank. Not, in
other words, the sort of missing person Mack seemed inclined to lose sleep over.

The trouble for Mack was that he could not keep the news to himself. About a month
after Levoir vanished, a friend named Jay Love called the police. Mack, Love said,
had confessed to killing Levoir and burning his body. The confession had come the
way so many do, at a bar, drunk, in December 2002. Mack had told Love he was his
best friend, asked if he could be trusted, aired his grievances about the
roommate, and then announced that "Robbie" was dead. Love asked whether he'd
"outsourced" the job. "No," Mack said, "I did it myself," and added that he'd
burned the body. A second friend, Michael Argueta, later testified Mack told him
much the same thing on a drive home.

So the police knew, almost from the start, more or less exactly what had happened.
Knowing and proving, though, are two different sports. There was no body, no crime
scene, no weapon tied to anything, just a drunk man's barroom bragging relayed
secondhand. They opened a two-track investigation: a wiretap on Mack's phone, and
a Mr. Big operation, the elaborate undercover theater in which police build a fake
criminal empire around a suspect and wait for him to confess to the boss.

## Third line

In January 2004 an undercover officer the judgment calls "Ben" was introduced to
Mack at the Fort McMurray nightclub where Mack worked as a DJ. A week later Ben
paid him $200 to help repossess a car, and Mack, agreeable as ever, was soon
volunteering about his missing roommate. As far as he was concerned, he told Ben,
Levoir was "pushing up daisies."

Through the winter, Mack ran errands for Ben's organization, headed by a boss
named "Liam." He fetched a package from an Edmonton bus depot and watched Ben open
it back at the hotel to reveal $30,000 in cash. Ben told stories about the outfit's
muscle, a "weekend of reckoning" when Liam had "looked after" two men who crossed
him, and asked whether Mack had ever beaten anyone. There was one time, Mack said,
that he couldn't talk about. Ben said he hoped one day Mack would trust him with
the secret. A few weeks later, Ben floated that the person in question wasn't
"walking anymore," and Mack nodded along, offering that "every man has a breaking
point."

Now, here is the moment that makes Dax Mack different from almost every other
target in this book, and it matters enormously to how the case came out. In
mid-March, Mack was summoned to Vancouver to meet the great Liam himself. Liam
raised the missing roommate directly and pressed for the story. And Mack, offered
a golden ticket up the organization in exchange for the truth, *turned it down*.
He asked if he could decline to discuss it. Liam said that was his right, but it
meant staying on the organization's lowly "third line." Mack held firm anyway,
explaining, with the gravity of a man quoting scripture, that "loose lips sink
ships."

Hold onto that refusal. It is the reason his confession stuck.

## The firepit

Three weeks later, on April 9, 2004, Ben asked whether Mack wanted more work, and
whether he'd sit down with Liam and finally explain how his roommate died. This
time Mack agreed, and then told Ben the whole thing. Levoir was "a liar, a thief,
and a piece of shit drug dealer." Mack had shot him five times with a .223 rifle,
four in the chest, one in the back. There was "nothing left" of him, because Mack
had burned the body.

Then Mack offered to show Ben the spot. Then, a beat later, his nerve failed and
he snatched it all back, insisting everything he'd just said was "bullshit," that
he'd been "lying." Ben, playing it beautifully, replied only that he "really
hoped" Mack had been telling the truth. And that was all it took. Mack changed his
mind a third time and drove the undercover officer out to a firepit on his
father's rural property. He'd cleared out the ashes, he assured Ben. Nothing left.

On April 15, flown to Edmonton to meet Liam again, Mack repeated the confession
in the same detail: the .223, five shots, four in the chest and one in the back,
the "big fire" at his dad's place. Six days later, police arrested him and
searched the father's property. In the very firepit Mack had personally pointed
out, they found fragments of bone and teeth, later identified as Robert Levoir,
and shell casings. The casings had been fired by a rifle recovered from Mack's own
apartment.

He'd said there was nothing left. He was wrong about that in every particular that
counted, and the thing he was most wrong about was his own inability to keep from
showing off.

## Why this confession survived

A jury convicted Mack of first-degree murder, and in September 2014, the same year
its companion case *R. v. Hart* (Chapter 31) rewrote the rules for Mr. Big stings,
a unanimous Supreme Court of Canada upheld the conviction. Put the two cases side
by side and the new law almost explains itself.

The worry with a Mr. Big confession is reliability. A man bribed richly enough, or
frightened badly enough, will say whatever the boss wants to hear. So the Court
asks what the sting *paid* and what the confession *produced*. Mack's inducements
were modest, roughly $5,000 across thirty scenarios, no threats, no violence aimed
at him, and the surest sign he wasn't simply telling terrified lies is that he had
already told Mr. Big *no* once and kept his job. Against that stood confirmation of
the rarest and most damning kind: the confession led police to human remains no
one had found, in a firepit only the killer could have identified, seasoned with
casings from his own rifle, and it matched to the letter what he'd drunkenly told
two friends back in 2002, long before any undercover officer existed in his world.

There was one loose thread, the wiretap. The Crown conceded the authorization was
defective and had violated the Charter, so none of the intercepted calls were used
at trial. Mack argued the tainted wiretap was so tangled up with the Mr. Big
operation that his confessions should fall along with it. The Court disagreed. The
confessions were made to people Mack chose to talk to, not skimmed off a tapped
line, and they stood.

## How'd he get caught?

Twice by his mouth, and finally by his feet. The drunk boast at the bar put his
name in a police file within a month, which is the criminal-justice equivalent of
leaving your business card at the scene. The sting simply built him a comfortable
room in which saying it all again felt safe. But what actually convicted Dax Mack
wasn't a single word. It was the walk, the short, fatal stroll out to the firepit
to prove to a man he'd known for weeks that he really had gotten away with it.
Anyone can confess to a crime he didn't commit. Only the killer can lead you to
the bones, and Mack, unable to resist the applause, led them there himself.
