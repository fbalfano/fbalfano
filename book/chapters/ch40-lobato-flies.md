# Chapter 40 - The Flies That Never Came

**She was convicted of a murder that happened 170 miles from where she was, on
the word of a jailhouse informant who forged letters to con her own sentencing
judge. What finally freed her was the testimony of insects that never showed up.**

The last two chapters were about a science that pretended to work. This one is
about two different kinds of unreliable evidence that nearly cost a young woman
her life, and the humble, patient biology that eventually undid them both. It is
the story of Kirstin Blaise Lobato, and it is the case where the flies, of all
things, told the truth.

On July 8, 2001, Las Vegas police found the body of a man named Duran Bailey
behind a dumpster on West Flamingo Road. He had been savaged, beaten with
something like a bat, stabbed, and mutilated after death, his penis amputated at
the base. It was a brutal, sexually charged killing in the heart of the city.

Kirstin Lobato lived in Panaca, Nevada, a small town roughly 170 miles away. Some
weeks earlier she had told her former teacher, and later others, a frightening
story: that during a recent trip to Las Vegas an older man had tried to sexually
assault her, and that she had fought him off with her butterfly knife, cutting at
his groin before escaping. When a detective came to Panaca and suggested he
understood she'd been attacked and forced to defend herself, and then gently
referenced knowing she'd been "hurt in the past," Lobato began to cry and said, "I
didn't think anybody would miss him."

To the detective, that was a confession. In hindsight, it was something far more
ambiguous, the words of a traumatized young woman conflating her own real assault
with a crime she had heard about. Because the assault Lobato described, the one she
fought off, had happened weeks before Duran Bailey was killed, and in a different
part of the city. The state's theory required her to be the dumpster killer. Her
own account, and the geography of her life, put her a three-hour drive away.

## The informant

With the physical case thin, the state reached for a witness the criminal justice
system leans on far too often and trusts far too much: another prisoner.

Korinda Martin, an inmate at the Clark County Detention Center, testified that
Lobato had boasted of the murder, of amputating a man's penis and stuffing it "down
his throat," of stabbing him repeatedly, and of planning to "play the poor-me act"
and cry sexual assault. It was vivid, it was damning, and it came, as such
testimony so often does, from a woman with powerful reasons to say whatever the
prosecutors wanted. Martin had a robbery conviction, a coercion conviction, and a
history of trying every lever available to get herself out of jail.

Just how far Martin would go to work the system emerged in a hearing away from the
jury. Lobato's lawyers confronted her with two handwritten letters. One was a
forgery scheme: a "cover" letter asking a former fellow prisoner to copy an
attached recommendation in her own handwriting and mail it to Martin's sentencing
judge, a letter falsely claiming Martin held a steady job with a high-risk
pregnancy, all designed to con the court into leniency. Martin denied writing them.
But she conceded the letters were a fraud on her sentencing court, and there was
the small matter of the envelope they arrived in, which bore Martin's own return
address and prison identification number.

Here is where the trial went wrong in the way that would eventually undo it. When
Lobato's defense tried to actually prove that this letter-forging informant had
authored the fraud, offering a handwriting expert's opinion that Martin wrote the
letters, the trial court shut it down as a collateral matter and let none of it
reach the jury. The jurors who convicted Kirstin Lobato heard Korinda Martin's
devastating account. They never heard that Martin was, by the evidence, a
practiced forger willing to defraud a judge to shave time off her own sentence.
And after the verdict, a police document examiner confirmed it: Martin had written
at least one of the letters. The jury got the informant's story and was spared the
one fact that best explained why not to believe it.

The jury convicted. In 2004, the Supreme Court of Nevada reversed, holding that
barring the defense from impeaching Martin with the forged letters was an error
that required a new trial. It is that decision this chapter is anchored to, and its
lesson is old and unglamorous: a jailhouse informant trading testimony for freedom
is among the most dangerous witnesses a courtroom can host, and a jury that cannot
weigh her motives cannot weigh her word.

## The flies

The 2004 reversal was not the end. It was, agonizingly, the middle. Lobato was
tried again and convicted again, and the fight to prove her innocence stretched on
for years. And what carried it, in the end, was not a lawyer's argument or a
recanting witness. It was entomology.

Consider the one fact the original timeline had glided past. Duran Bailey's body
lay exposed outdoors, behind a dumpster, in Las Vegas, in July. Anyone who has
left a picnic unattended in high summer knows what happens next: within hours,
often within minutes, blow flies find exposed flesh and blood and begin to lay
eggs, and those eggs become larvae on a schedule so reliable that forensic
entomologists can read a body's insect colonists like a clock. In the desert heat
of a Las Vegas day, a corpse should have been swarming.

Bailey's body was not. The near-total absence of the insect activity that summer
daylight guarantees pointed to a single conclusion: he had been killed and left
after dark, in the cool of night, when the flies had gone quiet. And that timing
mattered enormously, because it placed the murder in a window when Kirstin Lobato,
by the evidence, was 170 miles north in Panaca. The bugs that never came to Duran
Bailey's body became the alibi that the state's timeline had denied her. On the
strength of that reading, and the mounting rot of the case against her, courts
eventually vacated Kirstin Lobato's conviction, and after more than sixteen years
in prison she walked free. *(The entomology evidence and the final exoneration
belong to Lobato's retrial and later habeas proceedings; the 2004 decision anchored
here reversed her first conviction on the informant issue and does not itself
address the insects.)*

## How'd she get caught? And how did she get free?

Kirstin Lobato was caught by two of the least reliable things a courtroom trades
in: a grief-stricken, ambiguous utterance mistaken for a confession, and a career
jailhouse informant who forged documents to game her own sentence and whose lies
the jury was never allowed to see for what they were. That is how an innocent woman
loses sixteen years. How she got them back is the quieter miracle at the center of
this book's final chapters. No one could shake the informant, no one could unspeak
the confession that wasn't one. But the flies could not be bribed, could not be
coached, could not be made to say what the prosecution needed. They simply followed
their nature, and their nature was to arrive at a fresh body within hours. When they
didn't arrive, they left behind the truth, which is that Duran Bailey died in the
dark, in a city where Kirstin Lobato was not.
