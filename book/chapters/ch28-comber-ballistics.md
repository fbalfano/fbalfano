# Chapter 28 - The Second Shooter the Bullet Found

**Witnesses saw one man standing in the road firing a rifle, and the police
charged him with murder. Then the bullet pulled from the victim's body said he
had not done it, and pointed into the dark at the side of the road, where a second
shooter no one was watching had been standing.**

On the evening of November 17, 2018, Hailey Dugay was twenty years old and sitting
in the rear passenger seat of a pickup truck as it travelled down Kuz Road, a
gravel road in the Interlake region of Manitoba. A single .30-calibre bullet came
through the back of the truck and struck her in the lower back. She died of the
wound. She had done nothing but ride in a truck down a country road.

What makes her case matter to this book is not a clever killer undone by his own
device; it is the quiet, patient way a single recovered bullet can overrule every
eyewitness on the scene, clear the man everyone was sure had done it, and finger
the one they had not even been looking at. This is a chapter about ballistics, the
forensic reading of what a fired gun leaves behind, doing the hardest thing that
evidence can do: telling the police they had the wrong man, and where to find the
right one.

## The man everyone saw

At first the case looked simple, because there was a shooter in plain view. Several
witnesses had seen Jesse Paluk standing on Kuz Road firing a rifle at the time the
truck carrying Hailey Dugay was shot. He was the obvious suspect, and the police
charged him with second-degree murder. If the story had ended with what people saw,
it would have ended on Paluk.

But there were two guns on that road that night, both firing .30-calibre rounds,
and the difference between them is the whole case. Paluk had a bolt-action Remington
that fired large Magnum cartridges, about seven centimetres long. Standing somewhere
off to the side, in the ditch, was a second man with a semi-automatic Winchester
rifle that fired a different, smaller .30-calibre round, a SAVFC cartridge about five
centimetres long. Two shooters, two guns, two distinct kinds of ammunition, and one
of them killed her.

The physical scene preserved that distinction perfectly. Police found five spent
Magnum casings in the middle of Kuz Road, and forensic analysis confirmed they had
been fired by Paluk's bolt-action Remington. About ten feet away, in the grass of
the ditch on the west side of the road, lay three spent SAVFC cartridge casings, a
wholly different round, and analysis confirmed those had not come from Paluk's rifle.
Two piles of brass, ten feet apart, in two different calibres of the same bore. The
road itself was telling anyone who could read it that there had been two shooters,
not one.

## What the recovered bullet said

Then came the piece of evidence that resettled everything. The .30-calibre bullet
that killed Hailey Dugay was recovered from her body, and it was examined. Forensic
analysis confirmed that the fatal shot had not been fired by Paluk's bolt-action
Remington, the rifle every witness had watched him shoot.

Sit with what that means. The man standing in the middle of the road, the man
several people saw firing, the man the police had already charged with murder, was
excluded as the killer by the bullet itself. Eyewitnesses are famously fallible;
they place people, they miss things in the dark, they assume the man they can see
firing must be the man who did the harm. The bullet is not fallible in that way. It
carries the marks of the barrel that fired it, and those marks said: not this rifle.
Paluk had fired, recklessly and dangerously, and he pleaded guilty to discharging a
firearm with recklessness for it. But he had not fired the shot that reached the
truck.

Paluk became a cooperating witness for the Crown, and he identified the second
shooter, the man in the ditch with the Winchester, as William Comber. On August 6,
2019, Comber was charged with the second-degree murder of Hailey Dugay, on the
theory that his was the gun that fired the fatal, distinct SAVFC round.

Here the book has to be honest about a limit in the science, because Comber's own
conduct created it. Comber had taken the Winchester rifle into his residence after
the shootings, and the police never recovered it. Without the gun, the firearms
examiners could not positively match the fatal bullet to Comber's rifle the way a
recovered weapon allows; the make of the firearm that fired both the fatal shot and
the three SAVFC casings could not be conclusively established. So the ballistics did
not fingerprint Comber's gun. What it did was more subtle and, at trial, enough: it
eliminated the only other shooter, and it proved that a second, different weapon
firing a second, different cartridge had been at the roadside, exactly where Comber
was seen standing. The defence tried to answer this by suggesting that Paluk had
somehow wielded two guns at once, firing both cartridge types himself, a theory the
courts did not accept. A jury convicted Comber of second-degree murder in December
2022.

## Murder, then manslaughter

This case does not end where the jury left it, and the reason is worth understanding,
because it is the difference between killing someone and meaning to.

On appeal in 2026, the Court of Appeal of Manitoba dismissed most of Comber's many
grounds, but it agreed with one thing that mattered: the second-degree murder verdict
was unreasonable, not because he did not fire the fatal shot, but because the evidence
did not support the finding that he fired it with the intent the law requires for
murder. Firing a rifle at a vehicle, the court reasoned, is not automatically the same
as intending to cause bodily harm to a person inside it. The fatal bullet had struck
the tailgate, passed through the empty truck bed, punched through the cab wall and the
rear seat, and only then reached Hailey Dugay; there was no evidence Comber had aimed
at a person. That is the stuff of a reckless, unlawful killing, which is manslaughter,
not the intentional killing that is murder. The court substituted a verdict of
manslaughter and sent the case back for sentencing on that basis.

So Comber stands convicted of the unlawful killing of Hailey Dugay, not of her murder.
It is a real conviction for a real death, told at the honest weight the law finally
gave it, and the thread that produced it never wavered through either verdict: it was
always the bullet, and the two kinds of brass in the road, that decided who fired the
shot that killed her.

## How'd he get caught?

He was caught by physics doing what witnesses could not. Everyone was looking at the
loud man in the middle of the road, and the recovered bullet calmly informed the
court that he was innocent of the killing, that his rifle had not fired the fatal
round, and that a second gun of a different calibre had spoken from the ditch. Comber
thought he had covered himself by carrying the Winchester home so it could never be
found, and in a narrow sense he succeeded, because the science could not put that
exact rifle in his hands. But he could not carry away the three spent casings in the
grass, or reload the fatal bullet back into a body, or make the marks on it match the
one gun the police already had. The ballistics cleared the wrong man and lit up the
right one. You can hide the gun. You cannot hide the fact that it was a different gun.
