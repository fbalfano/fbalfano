# Chapter 37 - The Teeth That Caught a Monster

**Bite-mark evidence helped convict Ted Bundy, and the experts swore it was a
science of "a high degree of reliability." They were right about the monster.
They were wrong about the science, and innocent men would pay for the
difference.**

Every chapter until now has ended with a guilty man caught. This one begins the
part of the book where the evidence itself goes on trial, and it opens with the
most famous mouth in American criminal history, because to understand how a
forensic technique sent innocent men to death row, you first have to understand
how persuasive it looked when it caught someone who truly deserved it.

In the small hours of January 15, 1978, an intruder slipped into the Chi Omega
sorority house at Florida State University and moved through it with a length of
tree branch in his hand. In minutes he bludgeoned four young women as they slept.
Margaret Bowman's skull was crushed. Lisa Levy was beaten and strangled. Kathy
Kleiner and Karen Chandler were left gravely injured but alive. Then, roughly an
hour later and a few blocks away, the same man broke into the apartment of a
fifth student, Cheryl Thomas, and beat her nearly to death in her bed. Five women,
all attacked in their sleep, most of them never able to describe the man who did
it.

The man was Theodore Bundy, though it would take a trial to prove it, and the
proof came down to two things above all the rest. One was an eyewitness. The
other was a set of teeth.

## The witness and the wound

The eyewitness was Nita Neary, a Chi Omega resident who came home from a date
around 3:00 a.m. and, moving through the darkened house, heard someone running
down the stairs. She reached the front hall in time to see a man at the door, a
club in one hand, caught in profile for a few seconds before he slipped out. She
described him that night, worked with a sketch artist, and later picked Bundy out
of a photo array and again from the witness stand. Good evidence, the kind that
puts a case in motion. But an eyewitness who saw a stranger's profile for a few
seconds in the dark is, on its own, a thin thread to hang two murders on.

The second piece was thicker, and stranger, and it is the reason this chapter
exists. When the attacker killed Lisa Levy, he bit her, hard enough to leave clear
indentations in her flesh that investigators could photograph and identify as
human bite marks. And human teeth, the theory went, are like fingerprints: so
wildly varied from person to person, so individually chipped and crowded and
turned, that a bite is a signature.

Acting on a judge's warrant, police made wax impressions of Bundy's teeth and
cast models of both rows. A forensic dentist compared the peaks and gaps of those
models to the enlarged, life-size photographs of the wound on Lisa Levy's body,
walked the jury through his technique in detail, laid his materials out before
them, and delivered his conclusion without hedging: the marks on the victim were
made by the teeth of Ted Bundy. A second forensic dentist, working from
computer-enhanced photographs, reached exactly the same verdict. Both men told the
jury the same comforting thing about their field, that because human teeth vary so
widely, bite-mark comparison could identify a person with, in the court's words,
"a high degree of reliability."

Around that centerpiece the state stacked its corroboration. Hairs recovered from
a knotted pair of pantyhose in Cheryl Thomas's apartment matched Bundy's under the
microscope. He had rented a room a stone's throw from the sorority house a week
before the murders. He was seen in the neighborhood before and after. He fled from
police twice in the weeks that followed. And there was the matter of Bundy's own
mouth, the one that didn't leave marks. Standing among fellow rooming-house
residents the day of the killings, listening to someone guess the culprit was
"some lunatic" now "hiding out real scared," Bundy corrected him. It was, he said
with the connoisseur's confidence of a man reviewing another's work, "a
professional job," committed by someone who had done this before and had probably
already left town. He was, of course, describing himself, and he could not resist
the review.

The Florida Supreme Court, in 1984, found the evidence more than sufficient and
affirmed his convictions. The bite marks had done their work. Bundy was, by any
measure the law recognizes and any measure history has since confirmed, guilty.
*(He would be executed in Florida in 1989, having confessed by then to a
sprawling list of murders across several states, a coda that comes from the
historical record rather than from this decision.)*

## The promise that couldn't be kept

Here is where the triumph curdles, and where this chapter hands off to the two
that follow it.

The dentists in Bundy's case did not merely say the marks matched. They made a
scientific promise to that jury and to every jury after it: that human teeth are
unique, that a bite is a reliable signature, that comparing indentations in skin
to a plaster cast of a mouth is a method with "a high degree of reliability." It
sounded authoritative. It came from credentialed experts. It helped convict a man
who richly deserved it, which is the most dangerous thing a shaky science can ever
do, because nothing launders a bad technique faster than using it on a guilty
celebrity.

That promise, it turned out, was one forensic odontology could not keep. In the
decades after Bundy, the science of bite-mark comparison came in for the kind of
scrutiny it had never faced in a Tallahassee courtroom, and it did not survive it.
Skin is not wax; it distorts, swells, and heals. The "wide variation" in human
teeth had never actually been measured against the claim that a bite could single
out one mouth among millions. When outside scientists finally tested the field's
central boast, they found something closer to educated guessing dressed in a lab
coat. Expert bodies would eventually warn that a perpetrator cannot be reliably
identified by bite-mark comparison at all. And by then the technique had done in
other courtrooms exactly what it did in Bundy's, delivered a confident expert, a
persuaded jury, and a verdict, except that in those other courtrooms the man in
the defendant's chair was innocent.

## How'd he get caught?

Ted Bundy was caught by a bite, a witness, and above all by his own bottomless
need to be admired for his work. The teeth marks he left in Lisa Levy became his
signature, the eyewitness put him at the door, and his own mouth supplied the
rest, calling his massacre "a professional job" to a room of people who would
carry the line into a courtroom. On the facts of this case, the science got the
right man. But the lesson of this chapter is not that bite marks work. It is that
they looked like they worked, spectacularly, on a monster everyone was desperate
to convict, and that the applause drowned out the quieter question of whether the
method was real at all. The next two men you will meet were convicted by the very
same "high degree of reliability." Neither of them was guilty.
