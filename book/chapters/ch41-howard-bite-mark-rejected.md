# Chapter 41 - The Bite Marks Only One Man Could See

**The autopsy found no bite marks. Then the body was exhumed, a forensic dentist
declared he'd found three, matched them to Eddie Lee Howard, and sent him to
death row for twenty-six years. Another man's DNA was on the knife.**

By now you know the shape of the danger. A bite mark, a confident expert, a
persuaded jury. In Ted Bundy's case the method caught a monster. In Ray Krone's it
caught an innocent man for a decade. Eddie Lee Howard's case is the one where the
whole apparatus is laid bare, because in his case the bite marks were not just
unreliable. They may not have existed at all until someone needed them to.

On the evening of February 2, 1992, in Columbus, Mississippi, a neighbor noticed
smoke. Firefighters arrived to find a small fire smoldering in the living room of
eighty-four-year-old Georgia Kemp, and Kemp herself dead on her bedroom floor. On
the bed was a bloody knife. The telephone line had been cut. An autopsy the next
day, performed by Dr. Steven Hayne, concluded that Kemp had been beaten, strangled,
stabbed, and raped, and that she had died from two stab wounds to the left side of
her body.

Read that autopsy carefully, because of what it does not contain. Dr. Hayne, who
examined the body closely enough to catalog the beating and the strangulation and
the fatal stab wounds, noted no bite marks. None.

## The marks that appeared after burial

The bite marks entered the story only afterward, and by an unusual route. Days
after the autopsy, Kemp's body was exhumed for an additional study. And on February
7, a forensic dentist named Dr. Michael West examined the exhumed body and
announced that he had found human bite marks, on Kemp's right breast, the right
side of her neck, and her right arm, marks that the pathologist who conducted the
autopsy had not recorded.

Dr. West took molds of Eddie Lee Howard's teeth and performed what he called a
"wound duplication test with ink," pressing a direct comparison between the
defendant's dental impressions and the marks he said he had found. He declared the
marks on the neck and arm "consistent with" Howard's teeth, and the mark on the
breast, he testified at the time, was "indeed" made by Howard. Howard lived two
blocks away. He was arrested the next day. And on the strength of Dr. West's
bite-mark testimony, delivered with the credentials of a member of the American
Board of Forensic Odontology who assured the jury he had followed the Board's
guidelines, Eddie Lee Howard was convicted of capital murder and sentenced to
death.

There was precious little else. This was, once again, a case that stood or fell on
teeth.

## Twenty-six years

Eddie Lee Howard spent twenty-six years on Mississippi's death row, and the story
of how he got off it is a slow-motion collapse of everything that put him there.

The first crack was in the science itself. Dr. Michael West would become one of the
most notorious figures in American forensics, his bite-mark work implicated in a
string of wrongful convictions. And the field he represented turned on its own
central claim. Since Howard's trial, the American Board of Forensic Odontology had
revised the very guidelines West swore he'd followed, and the expert consensus had
hardened into something close to a repudiation: an individual perpetrator cannot be
reliably identified through bite-mark comparison. The method that had sent Howard to
die was, the scientific community now conceded, incapable of doing the one thing it
had claimed to do.

The second crack was the evidence that bite marks had always stood in for. When the
physical evidence from Kemp's home was finally subjected to modern DNA testing, it
did not merely fail to implicate Howard. The nightgown, the stockings, the
sexual-assault kit, Kemp's fingernail scrapings, none of them carried male DNA that
pointed to him. And then there was the knife, the bloody knife found on the bed, the
apparent murder weapon. Its blade was tested for the DNA left by touch. Male DNA was
there. It did not belong to Eddie Lee Howard. He was excluded as its source.

Put the two cracks together and the case simply falls through the floor. The bite
marks, the only real evidence against him, came from a technique the scientific
world had disowned, applied by an expert history had discredited, to marks a careful
autopsy never found until a body was dug up. And the knife that killed Georgia Kemp
carried the DNA of some other man's hand.

In 2020 the Supreme Court of Mississippi reversed the denial of Howard's petition,
vacated his conviction and his death sentence, and held that he was entitled to a
new trial. The court's logic was the mirror image of every bite-mark chapter before
it: strip out the discredited odontology, add the DNA of another man on the murder
weapon, weigh the thinness of everything else, and the verdict cannot stand. *(Eddie
Lee Howard was exonerated the following year, a coda that belongs to the case's
later history rather than to this decision.)*

## How'd he get caught? And who was really on trial

Eddie Lee Howard was caught by bite marks that a forensic dentist found on an
exhumed body after the pathologist found none, and that a jury trusted because a man
with impressive letters after his name told them to. He lost twenty-six years to it.
And here, at the end of this part of the book, the real defendant finally stands up,
because it was never truly Eddie Lee Howard, or Ray Krone, on trial. It was a
technique. Bite-mark comparison walked into American courtrooms wearing the robes of
science, and for decades juries could not tell the difference between the confidence
of an expert and the reliability of a method. On a guilty man like Bundy, that
difference was invisible, and the applause was deafening. On Krone and Howard, the
difference was a man's life, handed over on the say-so of a discipline that could
not actually do what it promised. The teeth never lied, exactly. They simply could
not speak, and we spent decades pretending we could hear them.
