# Chapter 24 - The Residue Doesn't Lie, But It Doesn't Explain

**A single particle of lead, antimony, and barium, fused by the heat of a
gunshot, does not tell you whose gun it was. It just tells you that you were
close to one.**

At about 7:20 in the evening on December 23, 2006, Stefanie Stevenson walked
the short distance from her home to her van. She was leaving for a shift at the
local hospital. An assailant who had been waiting beside her house stepped out
and shot her once in the head.

About two hours later, her estranged husband, Andrew Stevenson, was under
arrest and charged with first-degree murder.

There was no dispute at trial that whoever fired that shot was guilty of
first-degree murder. This was not a robbery gone wrong; the killer had lain in
wait. The only question, the only issue in the entire case, was identity. Who
was the person beside the house? There were no eyewitnesses. The murder weapon
was never found. Andrew Stevenson did not testify. The Crown's case was
entirely circumstantial, and one of its most important circumstances was
microscopic.

## Motive, opportunity, and an unaccounted hour

Before the science, the ordinary evidence pointed toward the husband, though
never cleanly.

The couple had separated thirteen months earlier and were divorcing. Stevenson
believed his wife had cheated during the marriage and carried what the judgment
calls "a considerable grudge"; he had been convicted of threatening her only
months before, in the summer of 2006. The day before she was killed, Stefanie
told him she had rejected his latest financial settlement offer, roughly
$30,000, and that he would be hearing from her lawyer. There was evidence he
was upset she had begun living with another man, and had said he did not want
another man raising his children. He also knew the things a lurking killer would
need to know: that she had moved into this home about three weeks earlier, and
her work schedule, because he took their daughters while she worked.

But the Court of Appeal was careful to record that motive "did not point only
in one direction." The two were largely getting along about the children; the
settlement conversation had not been acrimonious; Stevenson was in a new
relationship and appeared to have moved on.

Opportunity had the same shape: suggestive, not conclusive. He left his
sister's home near Brockville around 5:00 p.m., and video surveillance tracked
him Christmas shopping until 6:35 p.m., when he was filmed at a McDonald's about
twenty minutes from the murder scene. Stefanie was killed between 7:20 and 7:25.
He arrived back at his sister's home, a twenty-to-twenty-five-minute drive from
the scene, around 8:30. The stretch between 6:35 and 8:30, the window that
contained the murder, was simply unaccounted for.

That is a case that needs a piece of physical evidence to close the gap. The
Crown's was gunshot residue.

## What the particles were, and what they weren't

When the police arrested Stevenson, they wrapped his hands in plastic bags to
preserve any residue, and later daubed his hands and seized his clothing for
testing at the Centre of Forensic Sciences.

The results were substantial. Examiners found two gunshot-residue particles on
the back of his left hand, nine on the web of his right hand, thirteen on the
back of his right hand, and more on his jacket sleeve and on the front and back
of his pants. These were not loose approximations. A particle counts as GSR only
if it contains all three elements, lead, antimony, and barium, fused together by
the heat and pressure of a firearm discharging. The forensic scientist explained
that such particles are environmentally persistent, they do not easily degrade,
and, crucially, they are easily transferred from surface to surface by simple
contact.

That last property is the hinge of the whole case, and the reason this chapter
sits a little uneasily among the others in this book. As the expert testified,
and as the Court of Appeal emphasized, the presence of GSR on a person's hands
"does not assist in identifying how the particles came to be" there. The
residue proves proximity to a fired gun. It does not, by itself, prove you
fired it. The defence pressed exactly this point, raising innocent possibilities
including transfer after the arrest, from the hands of officers or from surfaces
in police custody.

The admissibility and the proper use of that evidence formed the main ground of
appeal. The Court of Appeal for Ontario examined how the residue had been
presented to the jury and, in November 2014, dismissed the appeal. The GSR
evidence was properly admitted. Combined with the lying-in-wait shooting, the
grudge, the killer's knowledge of the victim's new home and schedule, and the
unaccounted-for hour, the particles on Andrew Stevenson's hands helped a jury
conclude, beyond a reasonable doubt, that the man waiting beside the house was
her husband.

## How'd he get caught?

He was caught by a constellation, not a single star. No one saw the shooter; no
one found the gun. What the Crown assembled instead was a killer who knew where
the victim now lived and when she left for work, a husband with a grudge and a
fresh financial grievance, an hour on the night of the murder that he could not
explain, and then the residue: dozens of three-element particles across both
hands and his clothing, the signature left by standing next to a gun at the
instant it fires. Any one of those facts had an innocent explanation. The
gunshot residue could transfer. The hour could be spent anywhere. The grudge
could be just a grudge. It was the improbability of all of them landing on the
same man, on the same night, that a jury found could not be explained away.
