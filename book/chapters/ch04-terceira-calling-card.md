# Chapter 4 - The Calling Card

**A six-year-old girl vanished from outside her apartment building. Her body was
hidden in a boiler room so obscure the police never found it, on a floor they
didn't know existed. But the man who put her there left four different kinds of
himself on her body.**

Some killers are undone by a single, dramatic clue. This chapter is about the
opposite, and the more common, truth of forensic science: that a killer leaves not
one trace but many, and that the case against him is built the way a mosaic is,
from fragments that mean little alone and everything together. It is also, and
there is no gentle way to write this, about the murder of a child, so the humor
that runs through much of this book has no place here. Only the evidence does.

On the morning of October 14, 1990, six-year-old Andrea Atkinson said goodbye to
her mother and went outside to play at their Toronto apartment building. She was
last seen around 9:30 a.m. By 11:00 her mother was searching, checking with
neighbors and friends and combing the building, and finding nothing. Sometime
after 1:30 she called 911. Andrea had simply, impossibly, disappeared.

She had not gone far. Nine days later, on October 23, a janitor and a maintenance
supervisor discovered her body by accident, in the boiler room on the sixth floor
of the very building she lived in. She had been sexually assaulted and asphyxiated,
and her body hidden behind a hot water tank.

## The room almost no one knew

The location was, in its way, the first piece of evidence, because the sixth-floor
boiler room was a place almost no one knew existed. The residents didn't know about
it. Astonishingly, the police didn't either; despite a massive nine-day search of
the building, they never discovered there was a sixth floor with such a room on
it. Whoever hid Andrea Atkinson there was not a stranger who had wandered in. It
was someone who knew the building's hidden anatomy intimately.

John Terceira knew it. He worked as a janitor in the building, he had keys to the
boiler room, and he admitted he went there sometimes to smoke drugs. Other janitors
had keys too, but Terceira was the only one scheduled to work the day Andrea
disappeared, and two residents saw him in the building after she was last seen
alive. He had known the little girl, had ruffled her hair, had once chased off
some older boys who were bothering her and her friends. To Andrea, her mother
said, he had been a kind of hero. She looked up to him. It made him exactly the
sort of familiar face a trusting six-year-old would follow.

Knowledge and opportunity make a suspect. What made Terceira a convicted murderer
was what he left on the body, and it came in four forms.

## Four kinds of himself

The forensic evidence against John Terceira was not one finding but a convergence,
and the appellate courts would later treat it as a case study in how such evidence
is properly weighed.

At the attack site and on Andrea's clothing, investigators recovered **hair** that
matched Terceira. They found **blood**. On the concrete floor just outside the
boiler room, at the base of the stairs, was a mixed stain of blood and **semen**,
the semen present in enough quantity to be analyzed. And that analysis produced the
fourth thread, **DNA** that matched the appellant, the molecular confirmation
binding the other traces to one man.

Then there were the fibres. Numerous blue fibres, consistent with the blue outer
material of the sweatpants Terceira wore to work on October 14, were found on
Andrea's clothing and on the floor outside the boiler room. His clothes had, in
effect, shed onto the scene and onto the child, a silent record of contact.

Hair. Fibre. Blood and semen. DNA. Any one of them a thread a defense might try to
fray. Woven together, on the body of a child in a room only he really knew how to
find, they formed something no innocent explanation could survive. The DNA match
came wrapped in the same statistical questions that run through this part of the
book, and the courts wrestled with how those probabilities should be presented to
a jury. But the strength of the case never rested on a single number. It rested on
the sheer, compounding improbability of one man's hair and fibres and blood and
semen and DNA all arriving, together, on a murdered six-year-old.

Terceira compounded it himself. After Andrea disappeared, he lied to police about
how many times he had been in and around the boiler room, the kind of small,
unnecessary lie that betrays a consciousness of guilt. He was convicted of first-
degree murder. The Court of Appeal for Ontario upheld the conviction in 1998, and
the Supreme Court of Canada dismissed his further appeal in 1999, in a decision
that helped settle how DNA statistics are handled in Canadian courtrooms.

## How'd he get caught?

He was caught by everything at once. John Terceira chose a hiding place so secret
it defeated the police for nine days, gambling that a room no one knew about would
keep its secret. But a body is not a locked room, and a killer does not leave a
scene the way he entered it. He left his hair on her clothes and his fibres from
his own work pants scattered across the floor and the child, his blood and his
semen on the concrete, his DNA threaded through all of it. He knew the building's
hidden corners better than anyone. What he did not understand is that there is no
hidden corner inside forensic science, no boiler room where hair and fibre and
blood and DNA do not follow you. He left a calling card in four different hands,
and every one of them was signed.
