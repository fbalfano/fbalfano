# Chapter 27 - What Happens When You Die

**After they shot an innocent man in a parking lot, one of the killers pulled out
his phone and Googled "what happens when you die." He was not being
philosophical. He was checking his work.**

Nnamdi Ogba had no idea he was about to become a message.

He was an engineer who lived in Brampton, Ontario, and on the night he died he
was simply visiting a friend from his soccer team who lived at Scarlettwood Court
in Toronto. He had no gang ties, no criminal entanglements, no quarrel with
anyone. He had the profound misfortune of walking through a parking lot at the
wrong moment, in a place that two groups of young men had decided to turn into a
battlefield.

The Crown's theory, supported by a great deal of video, was that three men, whom
it identified as members of a gang called C3, had driven into the territory of a
rival gang, the Scarlettwood Crips, intending to shoot someone, anyone, to send a
message. The victim did not have to be a rival. He only had to be there. Nnamdi
Ogba was there.

## A murder on camera

Very little about this killing had to be reconstructed, because a remarkable
amount of it was captured on closed-circuit television, the quiet, unblinking
witness that has become the modern city's memory.

The cameras show three men taking a cab to a parking lot, climbing into a stolen
Nissan Rogue fitted with stolen licence plates, and driving to Scarlettwood Court.
The driver stays with the car and positions it for a fast exit, the automotive
equivalent of leaving the engine running, because that is exactly what it was. Two
of the men get out and walk through the lot. At the same moment, Nnamdi Ogba is
leaving one of the apartment buildings. The footage shows the two men approach him
from behind. There are several flashes of light in quick succession. Then the two
turn and sprint back to the waiting car, pile in, and are driven away.

Ogba was left in the parking lot, struck by five of the nine bullets fired. One of
them reached his heart. It was not seriously disputed that this footage showed his
murder. What the footage could not do, by itself, was prove which men were behind
the guns and, crucially for a first-degree murder charge, prove that the killing
was planned and deliberate rather than some chaotic eruption. For that, the Crown
turned to the killers' phones, and the phones delivered something colder than any
camera.

## The search

Watch what the CCTV captured after the shooting, because this is the detail that
will stay with you. The three men are together in an elevator. The driver is
showing the other two something on his phone. He rubs their heads, the gesture of
a coach after a good game, and offers them what looks like a pill, a little
celebration in a moving box.

What was on the phone, a later search revealed, was the news. In the minutes after
gunning down a stranger, one of the men had used his phone to pull up the
information the police were releasing about a shooting in the Scarlett Road area,
and then a news article about it. He was following the coverage of his own crime
in real time, the way a gambler refreshes a score.

And then, a few hours later, that same phone was used to run a Google search. Two
phrases. The first was "without vital signs." The second was "what happens when
you die."

Sit with what those searches are. They are not grief, and they are not fear.
They are quality control. A man who has just fired into another man's back is
checking, with the same casual thumb he'd use to look up a restaurant, whether the
job was finished, whether a person shot five times stays down, what the machinery
of a human being does when you switch it off. The next morning, a second
defendant's phone was also used to read news articles about the shooting. The
killers could not stop checking the box score, and every check was a timestamp,
saved to a server, waiting.

Together with the stolen car, the stolen plates, the getaway position, the
unhesitating ambush of a man who had done nothing, and the after-the-fact
celebration, those searches painted precisely the picture the Crown needed: not a
brawl gone wrong, but a planned and deliberate execution carried out to make a
point. A jury convicted all three of first-degree murder.

## An honest ending

This is the part of the chapter where honesty requires a turn, because two things
are true about *R. v. Mohamed* that the cinematic version would leave out.

The first is about the gun. A firearms examiner did testify. She took the cartridge
casings and bullet fragments recovered from the scene and from Ogba's body and ran
their characteristics through an FBI database, which spat back a list of firearms
that could have fired them. She noted that one of the two guns visible in the
phone videos appeared to be on that list. But she was careful, as good experts
are, about what she could not say: she could not definitively identify the murder
weapon. The ballistics here did not slam a door. It leaned, gently, in a
direction. In this case the smoking gun, so to speak, was never the gun. It was
the cameras and the callous curiosity of the searches.

The second turn is about the verdict. In 2025 the Court of Appeal for Ontario set
the convictions aside and ordered a new trial, concerned with how certain
evidence, including gang-expert testimony and some of the video and image
material, had been admitted. The men who were caught so vividly on camera and
condemned so plainly by their own phones did not, in the end, stay convicted on
this appeal. Their case goes back to be tried again.

That does not unmake how they were caught. It simply means that being caught and
being convicted are two different stations on the same line, and the law insists,
correctly, that the trip between them be done properly.

## How'd they get caught?

They were caught by a city that never stops recording and by their own inability
to look away from what they'd done. The CCTV followed them from the cab to the
stolen car to the ambush to the elevator, a continuous ribbon of evidence they
seem never to have considered. And then, handed a chance to simply vanish into the
night, one of them instead opened his phone and asked the internet whether the
man he had shot would die, turning his own curiosity into a signed, timestamped
statement of intent. There is an old idea that the guilty return to the scene of
the crime. These men did something the modern age makes far easier and far more
damning. They looked it up.
