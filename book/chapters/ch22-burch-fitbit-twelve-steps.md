# Chapter 22 — Twelve Steps

**The Fitbit cleared the boyfriend. A cellphone handed over in a fender-bender
case buried the man who did it.**

When police find a woman murdered, they look first at the man she loved. It is
grim arithmetic, but it is arithmetic that usually works.

On the night of May 20, 2016, Nicole VanderHeyden went to a bar near Green Bay,
Wisconsin, with her boyfriend, Douglass Detrie. They got separated. A phone
call and a string of text messages turned into an argument, and Detrie went
home alone to the house they shared.

The next day, Nicole's body was discovered next to a field. Her blood-stained
clothing turned up discarded along a freeway on-ramp. Some of her blood and
hair was found outside a neighbor's house. The Brown County Sheriff's Office
opened a homicide investigation, and the grim arithmetic did its work: Detrie —
the boyfriend, the argument, the last man with her — became the first suspect.

Then investigators looked at his wrist.

Detrie wore a Fitbit Flex, a small band that does nothing but count its
wearer's steps, hour after hour, whether or not anyone ever asks. During the
hours of Nicole VanderHeyden's death, Detrie's Fitbit logged **twelve steps**.

Twelve steps is a man getting up for a glass of water. It is not a man beating
a woman, moving her body to a field, and scattering her clothes along a
freeway. The investigation turned away from Detrie — and for months, it had
nowhere else to go.

## The phone in the evidence locker

Here is where this case becomes something stranger and more instructive than
any episode of television: the killer had already given the police everything
they needed. Voluntarily. With a signed form.

Three weeks after the murder, the Green Bay Police Department — a different
agency entirely — was investigating three minor incidents involving the same
vehicle: a stolen-vehicle report, a vehicle fire, and a hit-and-run. Their
suspect was a man named George Burch. An officer asked Burch if he could take
a look at the text messages on his phone to check his alibi. Burch agreed. The
officer explained it was easier to download the phone's contents than to
photograph the screen, and Burch said that would be fine. He signed a consent
form: *"I, George Stephen Burch . . . voluntarily give . . . permission to
search my . . . Samsung cellphone."*

The department's forensic examiner did what forensic examiners do: a full
physical extraction of everything on the device, saved to long-term storage,
standard practice. Burch got his phone back. The file sat in the archive.

Two months later, the Sheriff's Office — still working the VanderHeyden
homicide — matched a DNA sample from Nicole's sock to George Burch. When
detectives searched local records for prior police contact with him, they
found the Green Bay file: the consent form, and a complete copy of everything
that had been on his phone.

What was on his phone was damning twice over. His internet history included
**sixty-four viewings of news stories about Nicole VanderHeyden's death** — a
man compulsively watching the coverage of a murder he claimed to know nothing
about. And the phone pointed to a Google account. With a warrant, detectives
obtained Burch's "Google Dashboard" data, and Google's location records placed
his phone at the bar Nicole visited that night, near her residence, at the
field where her body was found — and at the on-ramp where her bloody clothing
had been thrown from a vehicle.

The phone had followed him through every stop of the crime, and then he had
handed it to a police officer over a fender-bender.

## The story he told the jury

At trial, Burch admitted he was with Nicole the night she died — he had to;
the data left him no choice. So he told the jury a story. They met at a bar,
he said, left together, parked near her house, became intimate. Then, he
claimed, he blacked out — and woke up on the ground with Nicole dead and
*Detrie* standing over them with a gun. In Burch's telling, the boyfriend
forced him at gunpoint to load Nicole's body into his own vehicle, drive it to
the field, and carry her into the ditch. Only then did Burch heroically shove
Detrie, run to his vehicle, and escape — pausing, on the drive home, to throw
Nicole's clothing out the window "in a panic."

He had, of course, mentioned this to no one — not during the months police
publicly begged for tips, not to a single friend.

The jury was asked to believe that the man whose Fitbit counted twelve steps
that night had orchestrated all of it. They deliberated and convicted George
Burch of first-degree intentional homicide. He was sentenced to life.

## What the appeal decided

Burch's appeal reached the Wisconsin Supreme Court, and it is the reason this
case matters far beyond Green Bay. He argued the police had betrayed him twice:
first when Green Bay downloaded his *entire* phone after he'd only discussed
text messages, and again when a different department mined that download,
months later, for a murder he'd never consented to being investigated for.

The court was unmoved (June 29, 2021, in a decision with several separate
writings — the justices themselves disagreed about how far digital consent
should stretch). Burch had signed a form authorizing a search of his cellphone,
full stop; nothing in his conversation with the officer limited it to texts.
And once the data lawfully sat in police storage, one department sharing it
with another was, in the circuit court's words, a common and long-understood
practice.

The Fitbit ruling was quieter but just as consequential. Burch — in a mirror
image of the fight in *State v. Dabate* (Chapter 21) — demanded the State
produce an expert to prove the science of Detrie's Fitbit before the twelve
steps could clear his rival. The court held the step-count records could come
in *without* expert testimony on the underlying technology. Where Connecticut
would put a Columbia professor on the stand to validate the device, Wisconsin
let the little band speak for itself.

## How'd he get caught?

He was caught by his own helpfulness. A murderer, three weeks clear of the
crime, signed a consent form in a trivial case to make himself look
cooperative — and handed police a complete map of the killing: his phone at
the bar, at her house, at the field, at the on-ramp, and sixty-four anxious
returns to the news coverage. Meanwhile, the man the arithmetic said should go
to prison was saved by a $99 wristband that had counted him fast asleep.
Twelve steps.
