# Chapter 22 - Twelve Steps

**A Fitbit cleared the boyfriend. Then the real killer walked into a police
station, over a fender-bender, and handed the cops his own phone.**

Every homicide detective starts with the same short list, and the name at the
top is almost always the last man to share the victim's bed. It is not
prejudice. It is arithmetic. Most people are killed by someone who loved them,
or claimed to, and the odds have closed a lot of cases before the coffee got
cold.

So when Nicole VanderHeyden turned up dead in a field outside Green Bay,
Wisconsin, on the morning of May 21, 2016, the arithmetic pointed where it
always points. The night before, she had gone drinking with her boyfriend,
Douglass Detrie. They had gotten separated, then tangled up in one of those
long, poisonous arguments that ricochet across a phone in calls and texts.
Detrie went home alone. By morning Nicole was lying next to a field, her
blood-stained clothes dumped along a freeway on-ramp, some of her blood and
hair left outside a neighbor's house like a trail nobody was meant to follow.

The Brown County Sheriff's Office did the math and looked hard at the boyfriend.
Motive, opportunity, a bad night that ended with a dead woman. It was a tidy
theory.

Then a detective looked at Detrie's wrist.

## Twelve steps

Detrie wore a Fitbit Flex, one of those little rubber bands that spends its
whole existence doing exactly one thing: counting the steps of the person
wearing it, tireless and dumb as a turnstile. It does not care whether you are
running a marathon or asleep on the couch. It just counts.

During the hours Nicole VanderHeyden was being beaten to death, moved to a
field, and scattered along a freeway, Douglass Detrie's Fitbit counted twelve
steps.

Twelve. That is a man getting up to find the bathroom in the dark. It is not a
man committing a murder that involved carrying a body and driving across town to
lose the evidence. You cannot strangle someone, load them into a car, and redecorate
a crime scene in twelve steps; you can barely reach the refrigerator. The
smallest, cheapest gadget in the case had just given the prime suspect the one
thing money usually buys and innocence rarely can: an airtight alibi, printed by
a machine with no reason to lie.

The tidy theory collapsed. And for months, the investigation had nowhere left to
go.

## The gift-wrapped suspect

Here is where the story stops being a tragedy for a moment and becomes something
you'd expect narrated over a laugh track, because the man who killed Nicole
VanderHeyden had already handed the police everything they needed to convict
him. He just hadn't gotten around to it yet.

Rewind three weeks. Across town, the Green Bay Police Department, a completely
different outfit working a completely different file, was chasing a small-time
mess involving one vehicle and three headaches: a stolen-car report, a car
fire, and a hit-and-run. Their guy was a man named George Burch. An officer sat
Burch down and, checking his alibi, asked if he could take a look at the text
messages on his phone.

Burch said sure.

Not only sure. When the officer explained it would be easier to just download
the whole phone than to sit there photographing the screen like it was 1995,
Burch agreed to that too, and signed a consent form to prove it: "I, George
Stephen Burch . . . voluntarily give . . . permission to search my . . . Samsung
cellphone." The department's forensic examiner did what examiners do when handed
a signed permission slip: he vacuumed the entire device, every byte, and filed
the copy away in long-term storage. Burch got his phone back and went on with
his life, presumably relieved to have been so cooperative.

The download sat in a drawer, quietly holding a confession nobody had read yet.

## What the drawer was holding

Two months later, the Sheriff's Office, still stuck on the VanderHeyden case,
finally caught a break the old-fashioned way: DNA on Nicole's sock came back to
George Burch. The detectives ran his name through the local records to see if
anyone had ever crossed paths with him, and up popped the Green Bay file, the
one about the stolen car and the fire, with a signed consent form stapled to a
complete mirror image of his phone.

They opened it. And there, in the browser history, was the detail that should be
taught in schools: **sixty-four separate visits to news stories about Nicole
VanderHeyden's death.** Sixty-four. A man who told anyone who'd listen that he
had nothing to do with it could not stop refreshing the coverage of it, the way
you worry a loose tooth you swear doesn't hurt.

The phone had more to say. It was tied to a Google account, and a warrant to
Google produced the "Dashboard" location history, a quiet little map of
everywhere the phone had been. It put Burch at the bar Nicole visited her last
night alive. It put him near her home. It put him at the field where her body
was found. And it put him at the on-ramp where her bloody clothes were thrown
from a moving car. The phone had ridden shotgun through the entire murder,
taking notes, and its owner had personally carried it into a police station and
offered it up over a traffic beef.

## The story he brought to court

By trial, Burch had no choice but to admit he'd been with Nicole that night; the
data had nailed his shoes to the floor. So he built a story around it, and it
was a beauty. Yes, he'd met her at a bar. Yes, they'd left together, parked near
her house, gotten intimate. And then, he told the jury, he blacked out, and
woke up on the ground to find Nicole dead and her boyfriend, Detrie, standing
over the two of them with a gun. At gunpoint, Burch said, Detrie forced him to
load the body into his own car, drive it to a field, and carry it into the
ditch, after which Burch heroically shoved him, sprinted to his vehicle, and
escaped, pausing only, on the drive home, to fling Nicole's clothing out the
window "in a panic."

It was the sort of tale that requires a very specific listener: one who has
never met a coincidence he didn't adore. It cast, as the villain, the one man in
Wisconsin with a twelve-step, machine-printed alibi. And Burch had shared this
harrowing gunpoint ordeal with precisely no one, not during the months police
publicly begged for tips, not with a single friend, until the witness stand
made it convenient.

The jury took the twelve steps over the blackout. George Burch was convicted of
first-degree intentional homicide and sentenced to life.

## The law catches up

Burch appealed all the way to the Wisconsin Supreme Court with a genuinely
interesting complaint: sure, he'd signed a consent form, but he'd only meant to
show the cops his *texts*, not donate the entire contents of his phone to a
murder investigation that hadn't even started yet. And once one police
department had it, could another one really go rummaging through it months later
for a different crime?

In 2021 the court said yes, and yes, though the justices split enough ways to
show these are not easy questions in the smartphone age. Burch's form authorized
a search of his cellphone, full stop, with nothing limiting it to text messages;
and one department sharing lawfully held evidence with another was, in the trial
court's words, common and long-understood practice. The court also ruled the
Fitbit's step count could go to the jury without an expert first climbing onto
the stand to vouch for the science, a mirror image of the fight in the Dabate
case a chapter over, where the same little gadget got the full white-glove
treatment. Wisconsin let the wristband speak for itself.

## How'd he get caught?

By being helpful. Somewhere out there is a version of George Burch who keeps his
mouth shut and his phone in his pocket, and that man is a much harder case. The
real one signed a form over a fender-bender and gift-wrapped a map of the
killing: his phone at the bar, at her house, at the field, at the on-ramp, and
sixty-four anxious little pilgrimages back to the news. Meanwhile the man the
odds had elected, the boyfriend, the easy answer, was saved by a nine-dollar-a-month
gadget that had clocked him doing twelve steps' worth of nothing. The oldest
rule in the detective's book says look at the one she loved. The newest footnote
says check his wrist first.
