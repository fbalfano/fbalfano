# Chapter 17 - The Girl Who Never Held the Knife

**She was fifteen. She never touched the victim. She talked a boy into the
stabbing from behind a keyboard, and the law called it first-degree murder.**

On New Year's Day 2008, seventeen-year-old David Bagshaw stabbed fourteen-year-old
Stefanie Rengel to death outside her Toronto home. He was tried separately and
convicted of first-degree murder. He was the one who held the knife.

But the person the Crown put at the center of this killing never met Stefanie
Rengel, never went to her house on the day she died, and was not present when
she was stabbed. Her name was Melissa Todorovic. She was fifteen years old, she
was Bagshaw's girlfriend, and the theory of the case was that she had
counselled and encouraged him to commit the murder; that the crime was, in
substance, hers.

The evidence that made that theory stick was almost entirely a record of things
Melissa Todorovic typed.

## A murder ordered by message

The Court of Appeal for Ontario's judgment describes a Crown case "based on a
large body of circumstantial evidence including computer chats by the appellant
with Bagshaw and others." Those communications, the Crown argued, showed
Todorovic was obsessively jealous of Rengel, a girl who had briefly dated
Bagshaw years earlier. The jealousy was not a passing mood; it was a campaign,
conducted in text and instant message, to get Bagshaw to eliminate the girl she
saw as a rival.

How relentless that campaign was becomes clear from an incident the judgment
records as happening about three months before the killing. Bagshaw went to
Stefanie Rengel's home and told her, to her face, that his girlfriend wanted him
to stab her. He explained his plan: he would leave his cell phone behind to make
it look as though he had been interrupted mid-attempt, and he wanted Stefanie to
confirm to his girlfriend that he had tried, so that his girlfriend would leave
him alone about it. This was not yet the murder. This was a fourteen-year-old
being told, in person, that a girl she had never wronged was demanding her death,
and being asked to help fake the attempt to satisfy that demand.

Stefanie told her mother. Her mother and stepfather, who happen both to be
Toronto police officers, confronted Bagshaw and told him and his girlfriend to
stay away from their daughter.

They did not stay away. Three months later, Bagshaw finished what the messages
had been pushing him toward.

## The keyboard and the confession

After the killing, Melissa Todorovic did two things that helped convict her, and
both were acts of communication.

First, she called Bagshaw. A friend of his who overheard part of the
conversation testified that Todorovic asked him a single question: "Did you do
it?" It is the question of a person waiting for confirmation that an order has
been carried out.

Second, when police interviewed her, she eventually made an incriminating
statement to the effect that she had asked Bagshaw to kill Stefanie Rengel. Much
of the appeal turns on the admissibility of that statement and a second one; on
whether a fifteen-year-old had been properly given her rights under the Youth
Criminal Justice Act and whether she had been detained when she confessed. The
Court of Appeal worked carefully through those protections, which exist because
the person being questioned was a child, and upheld the trial judge's rulings.

A jury convicted Melissa Todorovic of first-degree murder. The trial judge
ordered that she, fifteen at the time of the offence, be sentenced as an adult:
life imprisonment with no parole eligibility for seven years. On appeal she
challenged both the conviction and the adult sentence. In February 2014, the
Court of Appeal dismissed both. The sentencing judge, it held, had been entitled
to conclude she remained dangerous; the murder had been "conceived out of the
appellant's distorted view of her relationship with Bagshaw," she showed no
empathy for the victim, and she "harboured ongoing thoughts of hurting other
persons."

*(A note on sourcing: this appeal decision is concerned mainly with the
admissibility of Todorovic's police statements and her adult sentence, so it
summarizes the messaging evidence rather than quoting it at length. The detailed
content of the chats, widely reported at trial, lives in the trial record; this
chapter stays with what the appellate judgment sets out.)*

## How'd she get caught?

She was caught because counselling a murder, in the age of the instant message,
leaves the same kind of evidence trail as committing one. Melissa Todorovic
never had to be at the scene, never had to hold the weapon, never had to get
blood on her hands. She directed a killing through a boyfriend she could work on
day and night through a screen. But every push, every demand, every expression
of the jealousy that drove it existed as data on a device, and the law of
counselling murder does not require your hand on the knife; it requires proof
that you put it in someone else's. The chats were that proof. In this case, the
messages were not evidence of the crime. They were the crime.
