# Chapter 16 - The Hard Drive in the Rafters

**He researched how to destroy DNA evidence and saved the research to a hard
drive. Then he hid the hard drive instead of burning it.**

Douglas Garland was a man who kept score, and the ledger he kept was a strange
one, because the debt he was collecting on had never actually existed.

He was fifty-seven, and the Alberta Court of Appeal describes him with a few cold
words that tell you almost everything: a highly intelligent loner, an expert with
computers, unmarried, usually unemployed, living with his parents on a small farm
north of Calgary and driving an old 1994 Ford pickup. Years earlier he had worked
for one of Alvin Liknes's oil-and-gas ventures, and the two had fallen out over a
pump Liknes patented. Garland believed he'd helped invent it and had been robbed
of the credit.

Here is the detail the court sets down almost in passing, and it should stop you
where you sit: the pump was not economically viable. It never earned a single
dollar. There was, in the most literal sense, nothing to have been cheated out
of. The grievance was pure invention, a wound Garland had drawn on himself and
then nursed for the better part of a decade.

He kept tabs on Alvin Liknes for years, and over time the fixation spread to
Alvin's wife, Kathy. He tracked the couple through regular computer searches, a
private surveillance operation running quietly out of a farmhouse basement. In
December 2013 he convinced himself Alvin had stolen items from the Garland
property, and the old imaginary wound tore open again.

Then, in early 2014, the Likneses announced they were leaving. They listed their
Calgary home, planned a retirement move to the town of Evansburg, and advertised
an estate sale on Kijiji for the final week of June. Whatever clock had been
ticking inside Douglas Garland had just been handed a deadline.

## The preparations

What Garland did next he did with the patient, methodical care of a man who
plans, and the record of that planning is its own kind of horror.

He began researching, online, how to kill and dismember a human being. He
gathered restraints: handcuffs, straightjackets, tasers. He went to a butcher's
supply shop and bought meat hooks, butcher knives, and a meat saw. He studied how
to defeat one specific model of Schlage lock, the exact lock that secured the
Liknes front door. And in the same weeks, the same browser carried him through
"gore" sites showing bodies being dissected and taken apart, the appetite and the
instruction manual feeding each other.

On the evening of June 29, 2014, Kathy Liknes's daughter Jennifer brought her two
little boys, Nathan, five, and Max, three, to their grandparents' house for dinner
and a last sleepover before the move. Max would not settle, so around 11:30 p.m.
Jennifer drove him home. Nathan stayed behind.

About three hours later, in the dead middle of the night, Garland came. He
disabled the home's wireless router. He drilled out the Schlage lock he had
studied. And then, in the court's spare language, there followed a violent attack
that ended with Alvin, Kathy, and five-year-old Nathan overpowered and taken from
the house.

When Jennifer returned the next morning to collect her son, she walked into the
aftermath: blood through the house, mixed with broken teeth. A forensic
pathologist called to the scene offered the investigators a single thread of
terrible hope. This much blood, she said, meant one or more of the victims was
seriously hurt, but perhaps still alive. That thread, and the race it started,
would shape everything that came next.

## Five days

Blood is loud, and this house was screaming. Security cameras on neighbouring
homes had caught a truck closely matching Garland's distinctive old Ford passing
the Liknes house at 3:10 a.m. and again at 7:45. When police released the image,
Garland's own sister and brother-in-law came forward: that was almost certainly
Douglas's truck, and Douglas had carried a grudge against Alvin Liknes for years.

The router sealed it. The device was built to check in with its server every six
hours; it reported at 2:10 a.m. and then went silent, never calling home again.
Someone who understood computers had pulled the plug to keep his devices off the
network, and the police were now looking for exactly that someone: a loner with
computer expertise, driving a truck seen forty kilometres from his own farm at
3:10 in the morning, holding an old and specific hatred. On July 4 they came to
the Garland farm.

They arrived at a brutal fork. Wait for a warrant, or go in now, on the slim
chance that a man, a woman, or a small boy was still alive somewhere on the
property and could be saved. They went in, under strict orders to search only the
places large enough to hide a body. They found a large firepit, still smouldering.
Fresh cut marks scored into the recently cleaned floor of a greenhouse.
Restraints. No people.

Garland was stopped as he drove away from the farm and held in investigative
detention; police seized his shoes, and a victim's blood was on them. The next
day, warrant in hand, they searched in earnest. In the firepit, and on a meat
hook and a meat saw, they found DNA, blood and flesh both, from all three
victims.

And tucked up in the rafters of the house, hidden, they found a computer hard
drive.

## What the hard drive remembered

The forensic examination of that drive opened Garland's inner life like an
autopsy. Months of computer surveillance of Alvin and Kathy Liknes. Articles with
titles like "Kill Without Joy." An autopsy manual. Files of mutilated corpses.
And, sitting in that same collection, files explaining how to destroy DNA
evidence.

Consider the shape of that for a moment, because it is the closest this grim
chapter comes to a lesson. Douglas Garland had studied, carefully, how to erase
the biological traces a killer leaves behind. He understood the science well
enough to research countermeasures. And then he saved that research, along with
his surveillance logs and his how-to library of murder, onto a hard drive that he
tucked into the rafters of his parents' house rather than feeding to the same fire
that had consumed the bodies. The man who knew how to destroy evidence could not
bring himself to destroy the evidence of knowing.

Two more facts closed the circle. The day after the kidnapping, a car-wash
employee in nearby Airdrie noticed Garland power-washing his truck, and yet
forensics still recovered Kathy Liknes's blood from the back of the licence plate,
the one surface a hurried man forgets. And by sheer chance, an aerial survey had
photographed the region two days after the abduction, before police had any reason
to look at Garland at all. In those photographs of the Garland farm, near the burn
pit, lie what appear to be three bodies, two adults and a child.

The remains themselves were never recovered. The firepit had finished its work.

Garland did not testify and called no evidence. He did not, on appeal, even
dispute that he was the killer; that had been proven beyond any doubt. He argued
instead that police had trampled his Charter rights at nearly every turn, and the
Court of Appeal walked through each complaint and dismissed the appeal. The
searches that mattered had been driven by the one motive the law places above
almost everything else: the chance, however faint, that someone was still alive to
be saved.

## How'd he get caught?

He was undone by his own thoroughness, the very trait he was most proud of. The
grudge handed police his name within days, nursed so long and so loudly that his
own family recognized it on sight. The unplugged router announced a killer who
understood machines. The truck was too old and too distinctive to belong to anyone
else. And the hard drive in the rafters preserved, in flawless order, the
surveillance, the manuals, the appetite, and the plan, including his own careful
notes on how to erase the kind of evidence that ultimately buried him. Douglas
Garland understood technology better than almost anyone around him. What he never
understood was that a machine remembers everything you teach it, especially the
things you would most like it to forget.
