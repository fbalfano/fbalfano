# Chapter 16 - The Hard Drive in the Rafters

**The patent he felt cheated over never earned a dollar. He killed three people
for it anyway, and hid a hard drive that remembered every search.**

Douglas Garland kept score.

He was fifty-seven years old, described in the Alberta Court of Appeal's
judgment as a highly intelligent loner with an expertise in computers;
unmarried, usually unemployed, living with his parents on a small farm north
of Calgary and driving a 1994 Ford pickup. Years earlier he had worked for one
of Alvin Liknes's oil-and-gas businesses, and the two men had fallen out over
a pump Liknes patented. Garland believed he had contributed significantly to
its creation and had been cheated of the credit.

Here is the detail the court records almost in passing, and that should stop
you cold: the pump was not economically viable. It had never earned any money.
There was nothing to be cheated *of*.

It didn't matter. For years, Garland kept tabs on Alvin Liknes. His obsession
grew to include Alvin's wife, Kathy. He monitored the couple's activities
through regular computer searches, a private surveillance program running
quietly out of a farmhouse. In December 2013, he came to believe Alvin had
stolen items from the Garland farm, and the old wound reopened.

Then, in early 2014, the Likneses announced they were leaving. They listed
their Calgary house, planned a retirement move to Evansburg, and advertised an
estate sale on Kijiji for the last week of June. Whatever clock had been
ticking inside Douglas Garland ran out. If he was going to settle the score,
it had to be now.

## The preparations

What Garland did next, the trial evidence showed, he did methodically. He
began researching online how to kill and dismember people. He accumulated
restraints; handcuffs, straightjackets, tasers. He visited a butcher supply
shop and bought meat hooks, butcher knives, and a meat saw. He researched how
to defeat one specific model of Schlage lock, the kind securing the Liknes
front door. And through it all he browsed what the court describes as "gore"
sites showing bodies being dissected and dismembered.

On the evening of June 29, 2014, Kathy Liknes's daughter Jennifer brought her
two little boys, Nathan, five, and Max, three, to their grandparents' house
for dinner and one last sleepover before the move. Max was restless and
couldn't sleep, so around 11:30 p.m. Jennifer took him home. Nathan stayed.

About three hours later, Garland arrived. He disabled the home's wireless
router. He drilled out the Schlage lock. What followed, in the court's words,
was a violent attack that ended with Alvin, Kathy, and five-year-old Nathan
being overpowered and taken from the home.

When Jennifer returned the next morning to pick up her son, she walked into
the aftermath; blood throughout the house, mixed with broken teeth. A
forensic pathologist brought to the scene offered investigators a sliver of
terrible hope: this much blood meant someone was seriously injured, but
perhaps still alive.

## Five days

That sliver of hope drove everything that followed, including the legal fight
this case became.

Security cameras on neighbouring homes caught a truck closely resembling
Garland's distinctive old Ford passing the Liknes house at 3:10 a.m., and
again at 7:45 a.m. When police released the image to the media, Garland's own
sister and brother-in-law came forward: the truck was almost certainly
Douglas's, and Douglas had carried a grudge against Alvin Liknes for years.

The router sealed the suspicion. It was set to check in with its server every
six hours; it reported at 2:10 a.m. and never again. Someone who understood
computers had unplugged it to keep his devices off the network. A loner with
computer expertise, a matching truck forty kilometres from home at 3:10 a.m.,
and an abiding grudge: on July 4, police descended on the Garland farm.

They faced a brutal choice. Wait for a warrant, or go in immediately, on the
chance that a man, a woman, or a little boy was still alive somewhere on that
property. They went in, under orders to search only places large enough to
conceal a body. They found a large smouldering firepit. Fresh cut marks on the
recently cleaned floor of a greenhouse. Restraints. No bodies.

Garland was stopped driving away from the farm and detained; his shoes were
seized, and a victim's blood was on them. The next day, warrant in hand,
police searched the farm thoroughly. In the firepit, and on a meat hook and a
meat saw, they found DNA, blood and flesh, from all three victims.

And in the rafters of the house, hidden, they found a computer hard drive.

## What the hard drive remembered

The forensic examination of that drive laid Garland's inner life out like an
autopsy. Months of computer surveillance of Alvin and Kathy Liknes. Articles
with titles like "Kill Without Joy." An autopsy manual. Files of mutilated
corpses. Videos feeding a fetish for adult women wearing only diapers. And,
the detail that turns the stomach twice, files offering information on **how
to destroy DNA evidence**.

He had studied how to erase the physical traces. He never thought to burn the
drive that held the studying.

Two further pieces completed the case. The day after the kidnapping, a car
wash employee in nearby Airdrie noticed Garland power-washing his truck;
forensic examination still found Kathy Liknes's blood on the back of the
licence plate. And by grim coincidence, an aerial survey had photographed the
area two days after the abduction, before police ever looked at Garland. In
the photos of the Garland farm, near the burn pit, lie what appear to be three
bodies, two adults and a child, wearing only diapers.

The bodies themselves were never recovered. The firepit had done its work.

Garland did not testify and called no evidence. The jury convicted him of
three counts of first-degree murder, and he was sentenced to life with no
parole eligibility for seventy-five years. On appeal he didn't dispute that he
was the killer; he argued the police had violated the Charter at nearly every
step: the takedown, the shoe seizure, the warrantless search, the warrant
built on it, even the trial judge's gentle warnings to the jury before the
worst evidence ("I know yesterday was difficult. It was difficult for me
too."). The Court of Appeal worked through every ground and dismissed the
appeal. The police had erred in small ways, a black bag opened too soon, the
shoes taken during investigative detention, but the searches that mattered
were driven by the one motive the law protects above almost everything: the
chance that someone was still alive to save.

## How'd he get caught?

His own meticulousness convicted him. The grudge gave police his name within
days, nursed so long and so openly that his own family recognized it. The
unplugged router pointed to a killer who understood computers. The truck he
drove past the cameras was too old and too distinctive to be anyone else's.
And the hard drive in the rafters preserved, in perfect forensic order, the
research, the surveillance, the manuals, and the plan, including his notes on
destroying the very kind of evidence that hanged him. Douglas Garland
understood machines better than almost anyone around him. He just forgot that
they never forget.
