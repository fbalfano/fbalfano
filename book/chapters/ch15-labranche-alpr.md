# Chapter 15 - The Cameras on the Interstate

**He drove to the killings in his dead sister's car, a black Honda with
distinctive chrome trim. It was the most recognizable vehicle he could have
chosen, and the licence-plate readers on the interstate recognized it all the
way home.**

Grief curdles, sometimes, into something murderous, and Cody LaBranche's had. His
sister Brittany had died of a drug overdose, and LaBranche blamed the man she had
lived with, Jonathan Pizzuto, convinced Pizzuto was responsible for her death and
suspecting she had been murdered. The grievance festered, feeding on smaller
insults, disputes over Brittany's belongings, her cremated ashes, her pets, all
still in Pizzuto's house. In the days before the killings, the record showed
LaBranche trying again and again to contact Pizzuto.

On January 17, 2022, the bodies of Jonathan Pizzuto and William Mitchell, a guest
who had been sleeping on Pizzuto's sofa, were found at the house on Rosa Avenue in
Metairie, Louisiana. There were no weapons at the scene, but investigators
recovered ten spent .45-caliber casings. Pizzuto's dog was gone.

LaBranche had a plan for the getaway, of a sort. What he did not have was an
inconspicuous car.

## The most memorable car he could have picked

When Brittany died, LaBranche took possession of her vehicle, a black Honda Accord
with chrome trim, and it was that car he drove to Rosa Avenue. As the
investigation began, a suspect vehicle came into focus quickly and stayed there,
because it was hard to miss: a small, black, four-door sedan with distinctive
chrome features, captured on home surveillance video leaving the scene minutes
after the gunshots. A neighbour saw a figure in dark clothing walk out of the
victims' house, get into that car, and accelerate away.

From there the car was traceable, because it had a paper identity. Police linked
the distinctive sedan to Brittany LaBranche's Honda Accord, now registered to Cody
LaBranche's mother, at the same Ponchatoula address where LaBranche himself lived.
A dead woman's car, inherited by her aggrieved brother, still carrying its plates
and its papers, is not a getaway vehicle. It is a return address.

And then the interstate finished the job. Investigators pulled data from Automatic
Licence Plate Reader systems, the networks of cameras that photograph passing
plates and timestamp their locations, and the readers had seen the Honda. Hours
after the murders, the Accord was recorded travelling northbound on Interstate 55
near Hammond, leaving the New Orleans area within the exact window of the killings,
on a trajectory that matched a drive from Metairie, where two men lay dead, home
toward Ponchatoula. The killer had not been followed by any detective that night.
He had been followed by the road itself, one automated camera handing him to the
next as he fled north, building a timestamped map of his escape that no one had to
be awake to draw.

## The confession, and the river

The plate readers put the car on the highway. LaBranche's own mouth put him behind
the wheel. Arrested on February 11, 2022, and advised of his rights, he gave a
recorded statement in which he laid it out: he had armed himself at home, driven to
the Rosa Avenue house to confront Pizzuto about his sister, entered through the
side door, and opened fire, shooting Mitchell first and then Pizzuto. He denied
taking the dog. He admitted throwing the murder weapon into a river, the one piece
of evidence he successfully made disappear, and the one that mattered least, since
the rest of the case did not need it.

He was convicted of two counts of first-degree murder and, on appeal in 2025, the
Louisiana courts affirmed. The ALPR trail was one strand among several, the
surveillance video, the neighbour, the vehicle's registration, the confession, but
it was the strand that turned a distinctive car glimpsed leaving a murder scene
into a documented, minute-by-minute flight up the interstate.

## How'd he get caught?

He was caught, in part, because he committed a murder in a billboard. Of all the
cars Cody LaBranche might have driven to Rosa Avenue, he chose the one nobody could
forget and everyone could trace: his dead sister's black Accord with its chrome
trim, registered to his own mother at his own address. It announced itself on the
victims' security cameras, and then, as he fled, it announced itself again to the
licence-plate readers strung along Interstate 55, which do nothing all day but
memorize the plates of passing cars and remember exactly when and where they saw
them. A getaway depends on being forgotten. LaBranche fled in a vehicle built,
almost, to be remembered, past a system of cameras designed for nothing else, and
by the time he reached Ponchatoula the road had already written down where he had
been.
