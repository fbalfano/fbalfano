# Chapter 25 - The Residue Doesn't Lie, But It Doesn't Explain

**A speck of lead, antimony, and barium, fused by the heat of a gunshot, cannot
tell you whose finger was on the trigger. It can only tell you that you were
standing much too close to one.**

Not every chapter in this book is a story about a criminal outsmarting himself.
This one is about the humbler, harder kind of case, the sort investigators
actually face most of the time, where no single clue shouts the answer and the
truth has to be built brick by circumstantial brick. Andrew Stevenson's case
turns on a piece of evidence that is famous for pointing straight at the guilty
and infamous for occasionally pointing at the innocent, and the honest tension
between those two things is the whole story.

At about 7:20 on the evening of December 23, 2006, Stefanie Stevenson walked the
short distance from her house to her van. She was leaving for a shift at the local
hospital. Someone who had been waiting beside her home stepped out of the dark and
shot her once in the head.

Roughly two hours later, her estranged husband, Andrew Stevenson, was under arrest
and charged with first-degree murder.

No one disputed that whoever fired that shot was guilty of first-degree murder.
This was not a robbery gone sideways; the killer had lain in wait, patient and
deliberate. The only question in the entire case was the biggest one of all:
identity. Who was the figure beside the house? There were no eyewitnesses. The
gun was never found. Andrew Stevenson did not testify. The Crown's case was built
entirely of circumstance, and one of its most important bricks was invisible to
the naked eye.

## Motive, opportunity, and a missing hour

Before the science, the ordinary evidence leaned toward the husband, though it
never leaned cleanly, and the Court of Appeal was scrupulous about saying so.

The couple had separated thirteen months earlier and were grinding through a
divorce. Stevenson believed his wife had cheated during the marriage and nursed
what the judgment calls "a considerable grudge," and this was not idle character
assessment: he had been convicted of threatening her only months before, in the
summer of 2006. The day before she was killed, Stefanie told him she'd rejected
his latest financial-settlement offer, some $30,000, and that his next word on the
subject would come from her lawyer. There was evidence he resented that she'd
begun living with another man and had said he didn't want another man raising his
children. And he knew the two things a lurker would need to know: that she'd moved
into this particular house only about three weeks earlier, and her work schedule,
because he took their daughters while she worked.

But, the court stressed, the motive evidence "did not point only in one
direction." The two were largely getting along where the children were concerned.
The settlement conversation had not been a screaming match. Stevenson had a new
relationship of his own and, by appearances, had moved on. This is not the file of
a man visibly boiling over. It is the file of a man with reasons, which is a
weaker and more human thing.

Opportunity had the same frustrating shape. Stevenson left his sister's home near
Brockville around 5:00 p.m., and video surveillance dutifully tracked him
Christmas shopping until 6:35, when a McDonald's camera caught him about twenty
minutes from the murder scene. Stefanie was killed between 7:20 and 7:25. He was
back at his sister's, a twenty-to-twenty-five-minute drive from the scene, by
around 8:30. Which leaves a hole in the evening, 6:35 to 8:30, unaccounted for by
any witness or camera, and precisely wide enough to contain a murder. A missing
hour is not proof. But it is the kind of silence a jury notices.

That is a case badly in need of a physical fact to close the gap. The Crown's
physical fact was gunshot residue.

## What the particles were, and what they weren't

When police arrested Stevenson, they bagged his hands in plastic to preserve any
residue, then later daubed them and seized his clothing for testing at the Centre
of Forensic Sciences.

The haul was substantial. Examiners found two gunshot-residue particles on the
back of his left hand, nine on the web of his right hand, thirteen on the back of
his right hand, and more on his jacket sleeve and on the front and back of his
pants. And these were not loose approximations or lookalikes. A particle only
counts as GSR if it contains all three elements, lead, antimony, and barium, fused
together by the specific heat and pressure of a firearm going off. The forensic
scientist explained that such particles are stubbornly persistent, they don't
readily degrade, and, crucially, they transfer from surface to surface with the
lightest contact.

That last property is the hinge the entire case swings on, and the reason this
chapter sits a little uneasily beside the others. As the expert testified, and as
the Court of Appeal underlined, the presence of GSR on a person's hands "does not
assist in identifying how the particles came to be" there. The residue proves you
were near a gun when it fired. It does not, on its own, prove your hand pulled the
trigger. The defence pressed exactly that point, floating innocent routes for the
particles to have arrived, including transfer after the arrest, from officers'
hands or from surfaces in police custody. It is a real argument, and the honest
truth is that gunshot residue has helped convict the guilty and has, in other
cases, helped rush the innocent toward the wrong verdict.

The admissibility of that evidence, and the way it was explained to the jury, was
the heart of the appeal. In November 2014 the Court of Appeal for Ontario examined
how the residue had been presented and dismissed the appeal. The GSR was properly
admitted. Stacked together with the lying-in-wait ambush, the grudge, the killer's
private knowledge of the victim's new address and schedule, and that missing hour,
the particles helped a jury conclude, beyond a reasonable doubt, that the patient
figure beside the house had been her husband.

## How'd he get caught?

He was caught by a constellation, not a single star, and that is the point worth
sitting with. Nobody saw the shooter. Nobody recovered the gun. What the Crown
assembled instead was a killer who happened to know where the victim now lived and
when she left for work, a husband with a grudge and a fresh money grievance, an
hour on the night of the murder he could not account for, and then the residue,
dozens of three-element particles across both hands and his clothes, the chemical
signature of standing beside a gun at the instant it speaks. Take any one of those
facts alone and Andrew Stevenson had an answer. The GSR could transfer. The hour
could be spent anywhere. A grudge is just a grudge. What he could not answer was
the sheer improbability of all of them landing on the same man, on the same night,
outside the same house. Some criminals are undone by one spectacular blunder.
Others are simply buried, one modest and innocent-looking fact at a time, until the
pile stops looking like a coincidence.
