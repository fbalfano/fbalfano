# Chapter 9 — The Test Drive

**They planned the murder before they picked the victim. Tim Bosma was chosen
by his own for-sale ad.**

In April 2013, Tim Bosma did what thousands of people do every week: he
listed his truck for sale online. He was thirty-two, living just outside
Ancaster, Ontario, with his wife and young daughter, and the family had
decided to part with his Dodge 3500 diesel pickup. An interested buyer
arranged to come see it on the evening of May 6.

Two men arrived. The three of them went for a test drive — Dellen Millard
behind the wheel, Tim in the front passenger seat, Mark Smich in the back.

Tim Bosma never returned.

What his family could not know, and what a jury would eventually piece
together from the killers' own text messages, is that the test drive was the
final step of a plan that had been months in the making — a plan that needed a
truck, and did not care who owned it.

## The mission

The Court of Appeal's judgment lays out the Crown's case in a single,
devastating paragraph, and it is worth walking through slowly.

Months of text messages between Millard and Smich showed them planning what
they called a "mission" — to steal a truck. The same messages showed them
discussing the acquisition and preparation of something else: a ten-foot-tall
animal incinerator, commercially branded **"The Eliminator."** Millard, heir
to an aviation business, had it waiting in the woods behind the barn on his
farm. Other texts showed Millard acquiring a .380-calibre handgun the year
before; a photograph showed Smich holding that same gun.

Read those items together and the horror clarifies: the gun, the incinerator,
and the "mission" all predate any contact with Tim Bosma. The victim was the
last component they shopped for. He was selected the way you'd select a
classified ad — because he had what the mission required, and because he would
come outside and get into the truck with them.

On the night of May 6, the truck's movements were captured piecemeal by
surveillance cameras. The footage tracked it through the night and placed both
men inside the Millard Air hangar at **1:33 a.m.** Eleven minutes later, at
**1:44 a.m.**, the video shows a large flame emerging from the hangar.

## What was left

When police found Tim Bosma's truck, it was parked at the home of Millard's
mother. His blood was spattered through the cabin; the passenger-side window —
Tim's window — was broken. Millard's fingerprints were on the rear-view mirror
and the driver's-side door. Inside the truck: gunshot residue and a
.380-calibre shell casing.

Behind the barn at Millard's farm, police found the Eliminator. Inside it were
male human bones. On its hatch was Tim Bosma's blood.

Even the aftermath kept generating evidence. After Millard's arrest, Smich
enlisted friends to bring him the murder weapon hidden in a toolbox; he buried
the gun and refused to disclose where. From jail, Millard wrote letters to his
girlfriend instructing her how to obstruct the investigation and shift blame
onto Smich — letters that became their own battleground at trial.

## Each pointing at the other

The trial ran six months, and its defining feature was that the two killers
turned on each other — what the judgment calls "cut-throat defences." Smich
testified that Millard brought the gun and shot Bosma, and that he himself
helped destroy the evidence only because he was "utterly shocked at
everything." Millard didn't testify, but his lawyer's closing invited the jury
to conclude Smich was the shooter.

The jury's answer, in June 2016, was first-degree murder convictions for both.
For the trial judge, the duel of co-accused had been a procedural minefield —
each man attacking the other's character, rap lyrics entered as evidence,
inflammatory closings — and it is that minefield, not the facts, that the
appeals were fought over. In June 2023, the Court of Appeal for Ontario walked
through every ground, found most of them meritless without needing the Crown's
response, and dismissed both appeals. The evidence, the Crown had submitted,
was overwhelming. Nothing in the judgment suggests the court doubted it.

## How'd they get caught?

They documented themselves. The texts planning the "mission," the paper trail
of the gun, the discussions of the Eliminator — a premeditated murder
conveniently preserved in writing, months before the victim existed to them.
Then the physical world corroborated the digital one: cameras caught the truck
and the 1:44 a.m. flame; the truck itself held Tim's blood, Millard's prints,
and the shell casing; the incinerator held what the fire couldn't erase, with
the victim's blood on its hatch. Two men who planned everything down to the
disposal machine never planned for the simplest fact of modern life: that a
truck, a hangar, and a pair of phones all keep records. Tim Bosma's family
learned what happened to him because his killers wrote it down.
