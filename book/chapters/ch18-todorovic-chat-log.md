# Chapter 18 - The Girl Who Never Held the Knife

**She was fifteen. She never met the victim, never went to the scene, never
touched the weapon. She committed the murder entirely through a screen, and the
law agreed that made it hers.**

On New Year's Day 2008, a seventeen-year-old named David Bagshaw stabbed
fourteen-year-old Stefanie Rengel to death in the snow outside her Toronto home.
He held the knife. He was tried separately, and he was convicted of first-degree
murder.

But the person the Crown placed at the true center of this killing was not at the
scene when it happened. She had never met Stefanie Rengel. She was fifteen years
old, she was David Bagshaw's girlfriend, and her name was Melissa Todorovic. The
theory of the case, which a jury would accept, was that she had counselled and
encouraged Bagshaw to commit the murder, that the crime had been conceived and
driven by her, and that the boy in the snow with the knife had been, in a sense,
her instrument.

The proof of it was a record of things Melissa Todorovic typed.

## A murder authored by message

The Court of Appeal for Ontario describes a Crown case "based on a large body of
circumstantial evidence including computer chats by the appellant with Bagshaw
and others." Those messages, the Crown argued, revealed a girl consumed by
jealousy of Stefanie Rengel, who had briefly dated Bagshaw years before. The
jealousy was not a passing sulk. It was a campaign, conducted keystroke by
keystroke, to press Bagshaw into removing the girl she had cast as her rival.

How relentless that pressure became is captured in a single earlier incident the
judgment records, one that happened roughly three months before the killing.
Bagshaw went to Stefanie Rengel's home and told her, to her face, that his
girlfriend wanted him to stab her. He laid out the plan: he would leave his cell
phone behind to make it look as though he'd been interrupted mid-attempt, and he
wanted Stefanie herself to confirm to his girlfriend that he had tried, so that
the girlfriend would finally let the matter drop. Sit with the arithmetic of
that. A fourteen-year-old girl was being informed, in person, that another girl
she had never harmed was demanding her death, and was being asked to help fake the
attempt in order to satisfy that demand.

Stefanie told her mother. Her mother and stepfather, both, as it happens, Toronto
police officers, confronted Bagshaw and warned him and his girlfriend to stay
away from their daughter.

They did not stay away. Three months later, on the first day of the new year,
Bagshaw carried out what the messages had been driving toward all along.

## The screen and the confession

After the killing, Melissa Todorovic did two things that helped seal her own case,
and both, fittingly, were acts of communication.

First, she called Bagshaw. A friend of his who overheard part of that call
testified that Todorovic asked him a single, chilling question: "Did you do it?"
It is the question of someone waiting to hear that an instruction has been carried
out.

Second, when the police interviewed her, she eventually made an incriminating
statement to the effect that she had asked Bagshaw to kill Stefanie Rengel. Much
of the appeal, in fact, turns on the admissibility of that statement and a second
one, on whether a fifteen-year-old had been properly given her rights under the
Youth Criminal Justice Act and whether she had been detained at the moment she
confessed. The Court of Appeal worked carefully through those protections, which
exist precisely because the person under questioning was a child, and upheld the
trial judge's rulings.

A jury convicted Melissa Todorovic of first-degree murder. The trial judge ordered
that she, fifteen at the time of the crime, be sentenced as an adult: life
imprisonment with no parole eligibility for seven years. She appealed both the
conviction and the sentence, and in February 2014 the Court of Appeal dismissed
both. The sentencing judge had been entitled to find she remained dangerous, the
court held. The murder had grown out of what it called "the appellant's distorted
view of her relationship with Bagshaw." She had shown no empathy for the girl she
had helped kill, and she still, the record indicated, harboured thoughts of
hurting others.

*(A note on sourcing: this appeal decision is concerned chiefly with the
admissibility of Todorovic's police statements and her adult sentence, so it
summarizes the messaging evidence rather than quoting it at length. The granular
content of the chats, aired at length during the trial, lives in the trial record.
This chapter stays within what the appellate judgment establishes.)*

## How'd she get caught?

She was caught because, in the age of the instant message, talking someone into a
murder leaves the same kind of trail as committing one yourself. Melissa Todorovic
never had to stand in the snow. She never had to hold the knife or get a drop of
blood on her hands. She ran a killing at arm's length, working on a susceptible
boyfriend through a keyboard, day after day, until he did the thing she wanted
done. But the law of counselling murder has never required your hand on the
weapon. It requires proof that you put the weapon in someone else's, and Melissa
Todorovic had generated that proof herself, in writing, message after message,
plus a phone call to confirm delivery. In most of the stories in this book, the
digital record is the evidence of the crime. Here, the digital record very nearly
was the crime.
