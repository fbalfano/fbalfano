# A Closing Note

The oldest lesson in this book is also the newest one. Long before there were cell
towers and fitness trackers, the person who took a life still had to walk into the
world and back out of it, and the world still kept its receipts. What our own century
has added is only the sheer number of receipts. We have surrounded ourselves with
patient, tireless witnesses that watch without judgment and remember without malice,
and we invited every one of them in. They count our steps because we asked them to.
They log our drives because it is convenient. They keep our questions because we
never told them to forget.

For the guilty, that is a closing net. For the rest of us, it is worth a longer
thought than this book can give it, and a few of these chapters, the black box the
court would not let the police read, the confession the law threw out, mark exactly
where that thought should start. The same machinery that catches a killer in an
afternoon could be turned on anyone, for any reason, and the line that keeps it from
being is not the technology. It is the warrant, the judge, the rules of evidence, the
insistence that even a guilty man have the case proved against him and not merely
announced. The killers in these pages were caught because they left a trail. They
were convicted, when they were rightly convicted, because someone did the slow and
careful work of proving what the trail meant. That difference is the whole thing.

And sometimes the careful work runs the other way, and frees a man the evidence
should never have caught. That is in here too, on purpose. A trail can be misread. A
science can be junk dressed as certainty. The best answer to a machine that never
forgets has always been a human being who insists on getting it right.

So: do not bring your phone to a murder. Better yet, do not commit one. But if you
take nothing else from these stories, take this. Everything remembers now. It is a
marvelous thing, and a dangerous one, and which of the two depends entirely on who is
allowed to read the memory, and how carefully they are made to prove what it says.

# A Note on Sources and Method

Every chapter in this book began with a court decision and was written from it.

The cases were chosen for one quality above all: that a single, understandable thread
of evidence, one kind of forensic or investigative proof, could be shown doing its
work, and that a genuine appellate court had set out the facts in a written opinion
the reader could go and verify. That last requirement did real work. Some famous
cases are absent from this book precisely because they never produced a qualifying
appellate decision, resolved instead by a guilty plea or an abandoned appeal, and this
book would rather leave a good story out than anchor it to something you cannot check.

For each chapter, the anchoring decision was read in full, and the facts, the
evidence, and the disposition were taken from it. Where the narrative reaches beyond
the four corners of that decision, into a case's later history, a retrial, a
reversal, an exoneration, or into widely reported detail the opinion did not itself
contain, the text says so in plain language. Figures that circulate in the press but
do not appear in the opinions are identified as such. Decisions in French were
translated for the reader. Where a court protected an identity, initials are used.

The citations were checked against free public legal databases: CanLII for the
Canadian cases, and Justia, CourtListener, and similar services for the American ones.
A small number of exact reporter page numbers, the volume-and-page assigned by
commercial publishers after a decision issues, are noted where they should be
confirmed against a final printed reporter; in every such instance the case, court,
date, and docket number are certain, and the decision can be found and read today.

# Acknowledgments

A book like this one is a debt to institutions before it is a debt to people. It owes
everything to the judges who wrote these decisions with care, to the court reporters
and clerks who preserved them, and to the free public databases, CanLII, Justia,
CourtListener, and their kind, that put the law within reach of anyone with a
question and an evening. Open access to court decisions is what made a book like this
possible for an ordinary reader to write and for an ordinary reader to check. It is
easy to take for granted. It should not be.

My thanks to the family and friends who read early chapters, argued with me about
which cases belonged, and reminded me, when a clever capture threatened to become the
whole point, that a real person had died in every one of these stories.

And to Silas, who arrived in the middle of the writing and put all of it in
proportion.
