# Chapter 7 - The Carpet in His Bedroom

**For two years a city buried its children and could not name the killer. In the
end he was undone not by a witness or a weapon, but by the carpet fibres he
carried out of his own bedroom and left on the bodies.**

Between 1979 and 1981, Atlanta lived through one of the most terrifying stretches
in any American city's history, a run of murders whose victims were, again and
again, young and Black, and whose killer left a metropolis afraid to let its
children out of sight. The case that finally ended it did not turn on an
eyewitness who saw a face or a gun that could be traced. It turned on something
almost invisible, the tiny textile fibres that human beings shed and carry
everywhere they go, and it became the landmark American case on convicting a
killer by the threads he leaves behind.

Wayne Bertram Williams was charged with the murders of two young men, Jimmy Ray
Payne and Nathaniel Cater. But the case against him was built on a pattern far
larger than two deaths, and the pattern was written in fibre.

## What the threads remembered

Every environment sheds itself. A home, a car, a pet, each is a source of
characteristic fibres, from carpets and clothing and upholstery and animal hair,
and when a person moves through the world he carries a signature of those fibres
with him and deposits them on whatever, and whoever, he touches. The state's
forensic experts had recovered fibres from the bodies of the victims, and they
set out to answer a single question: where did those fibres come from?

The answer kept leading back to Wayne Williams. Fibres associated with the
victims matched fibres recovered from Williams's home, from the cars he had used,
and even from his German Shepherd dog. The experts testified that these matches
supported an inference that Williams had been in contact with the victims before
they died, and the prosecution built from that the further inference that he was
the man who had killed them.

The centrepiece was a green fibre. One had been recovered from the hair of
Nathaniel Cater. It matched, in its dyes and colours, a green fibre from a carpet
in Williams's own bedroom. And here the forensic work reached a level of care
worth pausing on, because it shows how seriously the analysts took the challenge.
The bodies in this case had been disposed of in the Chattahoochee River, and a
defence might argue that water would alter a fibre's colour and defeat any
comparison. So the examiners took a fibre from Williams's bedroom carpet and
immersed it in Chattahoochee River water for three days, testing how the river
itself would change it, and compared that to what was found on the victim. The
carpet in his bedroom was being cross-examined as thoroughly as any witness.

## A pattern too specific to be chance

A single fibre match can be a coincidence. What made the evidence overwhelming was
its repetition. Williams was tried for two murders, but the state introduced
evidence of ten other killings as well, not to punish him for them directly, but
to establish his identity as the killer through the recurring fibre signature that
tied his environment to body after body, twelve in all. The same threads from the
same home, the same cars, the same dog, kept appearing on victim after victim.

The power of that kind of evidence lies in improbability. It is unremarkable for
one green household fibre to turn up somewhere. It is something else entirely for
the fibres of one man's bedroom carpet, his vehicles, and his dog to appear,
together and repeatedly, on a series of murdered young people across a city. The
prosecution's case was that no innocent explanation could account for that
convergence, occurring again and again, around a single man. Williams challenged
the science, arguing the fibre methodology had not been shown reliable enough to
go to a jury, but the Georgia courts held the evidence properly admitted.

On February 27, 1982, Wayne Williams was convicted of both murders and sentenced
to two consecutive life terms. In 1983 the Supreme Court of Georgia affirmed,
cementing fibre-comparison evidence, and the probabilistic reasoning behind it, as
a legitimate foundation for a murder conviction. It remains the landmark American
decision on trace-fibre evidence in a homicide.

## How'd he get caught?

He was caught by the parts of his own home that he could not help carrying with
him. Wayne Williams left no confession and, for a long time, no witness the
police could use. But he could not move through his house, his cars, and his life
without gathering their fibres onto his clothing, and he could not touch his
victims without leaving those fibres behind. A green thread from his bedroom
carpet in a dead boy's hair; the same signature of home and car and dog appearing
on body after body, a dozen times over. He thought, perhaps, that a fibre was too
small to matter, that no one could build a case out of something you cannot feel
on your own sleeve. The city of Atlanta learned otherwise. The smallest things we
carry are still ours, and they go where we go, and they stay where we have been.
