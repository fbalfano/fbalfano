# Chapter 3 - Under Her Fingernails

**An intruder climbed the stairs to their apartment in the dark. He killed
Michaël Cadieux with fourteen stab wounds. He did not count on the woman beside
him fighting back, grabbing him by the throat, and taking a piece of him under
her nails.**

Most of the victims in this book could not testify to how they died. The woman at
the center of this chapter could, because she lived, and because in the seconds
she had, she did something that turned her own hand into the evidence that named
her attacker.

It happened in Quebec City, in the small hours of June 12, 2011. Michaël Cadieux
and his partner, a young woman the court records call B.F.S. to protect her, had
showered and gone to bed. At 4:12 in the morning they woke to the sense of someone
in the apartment, a figure standing at the top of the staircase that led up to
their home from the rear of the building. Cadieux switched on a bedside lamp and
asked the intruder to say who he was. The figure said nothing. It simply stood
there in the half-light, a silence more frightening than any threat.

Then Cadieux and B.F.S. did the brave, human thing. They rushed him.

## The seconds that mattered

The intruder was armed, and Cadieux was overwhelmed fast. He was struck fourteen
times with a sharp, pointed weapon, wounds that would kill him. But B.F.S. did not
stop. She threw herself at the attacker, got both hands around his neck, and tried
to strangle him. She hauled him toward her until his head was down at the level of
her hips, and then she shoved him with everything she had against the wall.

She could not save Michaël Cadieux, and she could not save herself from grave
injury. What she could do, in that furious, terrified grappling, was hold on. And
when you seize a man by the throat and fight him at close quarters, you come away
with more than bruises. You come away with him, microscopic scrapings of him,
lodged beneath your fingernails.

That same day, as B.F.S. was treated, forensic technicians took samples from her
body, and among them they scraped under the nails of her hands. What they found
under the nails of her right hand was extraordinary. There were two complete
genetic profiles present, hers and one other, and that second full profile
belonged to a man named Pierre-Olivier Laliberté. Two *complete* profiles in a
single fingernail sample is, the forensic biologist Jean Bergeron testified, an
extremely rare thing; it does not happen often. There was as much of Laliberté's
DNA under her right-hand nails as there was of her own. The chance that the
profiles belonged to anyone other than B.F.S. and Laliberté was one in several
hundred billion. And the sample was so rich, so red, that the expert concluded
what was packed under her nails was not some stray trace but blood, or bleeding
flesh, torn from her attacker in the struggle.

## The dishwasher's hands

The defense understood exactly how damning that was, and it reached for the one
argument that might explain the DNA away: innocent transfer. Might Laliberté's DNA
have found its way under her nails some other, harmless way?

The evidence closed that door, and it closed it with a beautifully ordinary
detail. B.F.S. worked as a dishwasher at a restaurant, thirty-five hours a week,
her bare hands in hot water and cleaning chemicals, scrubbing dishes without
gloves. She had worked a full shift the very day before the attack, from morning
into the afternoon. And DNA, the expert explained, does not survive that. Humidity
is what destroys it; a dishwasher's hands, plunged in soapy water hour after hour,
are about as hostile an environment for lingering genetic material as you could
design. Whatever had been under her nails from ordinary life would have been
scrubbed away by the end of her shift.

So the rich, bloody deposit of Pierre-Olivier Laliberté's DNA under her right-hand
fingernails could not be some old, innocent trace. It had to be fresh. It had to
have gotten there after her last shift ended, in the only event that fit: the
early-morning fight for her life, when she grabbed her attacker by the throat and
would not let go. There had been no contact between them in the days before. The
DNA had one possible origin, and it was the struggle.

The Court of Appeal called the DNA identification evidence overwhelming, and it was
right to. Laliberté was convicted of the first-degree murder of Michaël Cadieux
and the attempted murder of B.F.S., and in 2015 the Quebec Court of Appeal
dismissed his appeal.

## How'd he get caught?

He was caught because the woman he left for dead fought back with her hands. An
intruder who kills in the dark counts on darkness, on the dead not talking and the
scene not remembering. Laliberté nearly got both. But B.F.S. did the one thing he
could not have planned for: in the middle of the worst moment of her life, she got
hold of him, strangled and shoved and clawed, and in doing so she reached past his
silence and took a piece of him for the police to find. Her fingernails testified
when she might not have survived to. He walked up those stairs believing he was the
only one who would leave that apartment knowing what happened. He was wrong, because
he did not leave all of himself with him. Some of him went under her nails, and it
gave his name.
