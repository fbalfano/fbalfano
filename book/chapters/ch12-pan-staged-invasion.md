# Chapter 12 - The Home Invasion That Wasn't

**She called 911 to report a home invasion, and police found her tied to the
banister. But the intruders had shot both her parents and left her without a
scratch, and in a house they had supposedly come to rob, her mother's car keys
sat untouched.**

A staged crime scene is a story told in objects, and like any liar, it can be
caught in a contradiction. The scene Jennifer Pan presented to the police on the
night of November 8, 2010, told one story out loud and another under its breath,
and the gap between them, widened by a mountain of digital evidence, is what
unravelled a daughter's plot to have her own parents murdered.

That night, in a subdivision in Markham, Ontario, three men entered the home Pan
shared with her mother and father, Bich-Ha Pan and Hann Pan. The men took control
of the family, marched the parents down to the basement, and shot them. Then they
left, and, tellingly, they left Jennifer Pan behind, unharmed. A neighbour's
surveillance camera caught the choreography with quiet precision: three people
entering at 10:14 p.m., one leaving at 10:30, two more by 10:32.

Pan called 911 to report a home invasion and a shooting. When officers arrived,
they found her tied to a banister. Her father, gravely wounded, had managed to
drag himself to a neighbour's house. Her mother died that evening. Her father,
against the odds, survived, and survival made him a witness.

## The story the objects wouldn't tell

On its face, it was a home invasion gone horribly wrong, a robbery turned double
shooting. But the scene, examined closely, kept refusing to cooperate with that
story.

Start with Jennifer Pan herself. Three armed men had taken control of a household,
executed both parents in the basement, and then, rather than eliminate the one
adult witness who could describe them, gently left her alive and tied to a
banister. Killers who have just shot two people do not usually leave the third
carefully unharmed. Then consider what the "robbers" took, which was next to
nothing. They had demanded money, and the master bedroom looked ransacked, but the
valuables that a real thief grabs on the way out were left sitting in place. Most
tellingly of all, the keys to Pan's mother's car, parked right there in the
driveway, an easy prize for anyone fleeing a crime, were never touched.

The forensic scene added only silence where a robbery-murder should have left
noise. Neither Pan nor her father could positively identify the men. There were no
relevant fingerprints. No usable DNA. None of the bullets could be matched to a
gun. Standing alone, that absence proved nothing. But a home invasion that leaves
a witness alive, ignores the car keys, and takes nothing worth taking is not a
robbery. It is a performance, and performances have scripts, and in 2010 the
script was written on a phone.

## Game time

The prosecution's case was carried by a vast body of cellphone evidence and text
messages, and it read, in the end, like the log of a project plan.

The theory the evidence supported was that Jennifer Pan had arranged for her own
parents to be killed, a murder-for-hire she routed through her former boyfriend,
Daniel Wong, and his friend Lenford Crawford, with the shooting itself carried out
by hired men including David Mylvaganam. Her motive, the Crown argued, was a life
her strict parents would not allow her to have: to escape a suffocating home, to
be with Wong, and to inherit her share of her parents' estate and life insurance.
The price was $10,000 for the killing, plus $2,000 the night of.

The digital trail tracked the conspiracy like a heartbeat monitor. This was not
even Pan's first attempt; earlier in 2010 she had tried to arrange the murder
through another man who simply never followed through. When that failed, she
turned to Wong, who connected her to Crawford, and from August 2010 she was
texting Crawford from her iPhone using a SIM card Wong had supplied. Through late
October the plan firmed up, the contract price settled by an exchange of calls and
texts among the conspirators. And on the morning of November 8, Crawford sent Pan
a text that a screenwriter would blush to invent. After work, he wrote, it would
be "game time."

Game time came. Cellphone records showed the gunmen driving from Rexdale to the
Pan neighbourhood in Markham that evening. Shortly before the shootings, one of
their phones called Pan twice. After the shootings, cell-tower records tracked
that phone retreating back to Rexdale, while a chain of intermediaries relayed word
up the line that everything was okay. And a friend of Pan's, Andrew Montemayor,
testified that just before the "home invasion," Pan had told him her plan: to stage
exactly such an invasion, that night, in which her parents would be shot.

The scene said robbery. The phones said contract. The phones were telling the
truth.

## An unfinished ending

Jennifer Pan and her three co-accused were convicted of the first-degree murder of
her mother and the attempted murder of her father. But this is one of those cases,
and the book is honest about them, where the appellate courts were not finished.

The Court of Appeal for Ontario, in 2023, set aside the first-degree murder
convictions and ordered a new trial on that count, not because it doubted the
evidence of the plot, but because of an error in how the jury had been instructed
about the lesser offences it could consider. It upheld the attempted-murder
convictions. The Crown appealed to the Supreme Court of Canada, seeking to restore
the murder convictions outright; the accused cross-appealed, seeking new trials on
everything. In April 2025, the Supreme Court largely held the line the Court of
Appeal had drawn: a new trial is required for all the accused on the first-degree
murder count, while the attempted-murder convictions stand. As of this writing,
then, the murder case built on that staged scene and those damning phones is
headed back to a courtroom to be proven again.

## How'd she get caught?

She was caught by the difference between the story she staged and the story her
own life had recorded. Jennifer Pan built a home invasion for the police to find,
and she got the broad strokes right, the terror, the ransacked room, the daughter
bound and weeping. What she could not control were the details that a real robbery
would have left differently: the witness inexplicably spared, the car keys no
thief would ignore, the valuables no thief would leave. And what she could not
erase at all was the plan itself, which she had not kept in her head but had typed,
sent, and received, over months, across a web of phones that remembered every call
and every "game time" text long after her mother was dead. A staged scene can fool
the eye for a night. It cannot outlast the paper trail of the people who agreed,
in writing, to build it.
