# Chapter 5 - A Coffee Cup and a Stranger's Family Tree

**A young couple was murdered on a road trip in 1987, and the case went cold for
thirty years. The killer was found not through his own DNA, but through the DNA
of cousins he had never met, and confirmed by a cup he threw away.**

The previous four chapters were about killers undone by their own bodies, the
hair, the fluid, the fibres they left at the scene. This chapter is about
something newer and stranger, a technique that can catch a killer who left his
DNA everywhere and still stayed invisible for decades, because there was no name
to match it to. It works by finding him through his family, and it announced
itself to the world in the case of William Earl Talbott II.

On November 18, 1987, Tanya Van Cuylenborg and Jay Cook, a young couple from
Victoria, British Columbia, set out on an overnight errand to the Seattle area to
pick up parts for Cook's father's business. They took the ferry across to Port
Angeles, Washington, and drove south in their van. Unfamiliar with the area, they
got lost trying to reach the Bremerton ferry, and a store clerk in Hoodsport
pointed them the right way. They were an ordinary couple on an ordinary drive,
and they were never seen alive again by anyone who wished them well.

Their bodies were found days later, in two different rural counties, each in a
remote place; their van abandoned in a third. Tanya Van Cuylenborg had been shot
and left partly nude. Jay Cook had been beaten and killed. It was the kind of
double murder that horrifies a region and then, slowly, defeats it. There was
biological evidence, semen on Van Cuylenborg's clothing and body, and a palm
print on the van's rear window. But in 1987 those things could only convict a
suspect, and there was no suspect. The DNA belonged to a ghost.

## Thirty years, and then a family tree

For nearly three decades the case sat, the DNA profile complete and useless, a
key with no lock. The killer had left his genetic signature all over the crime,
and it did not matter, because a DNA profile only names a person if that person's
profile is already sitting in a database somewhere to match against. Talbott's
never was.

What changed was not the killer's DNA. It was everyone else's.

By 2018, millions of ordinary people had spat into tubes and mailed their DNA to
consumer genealogy companies to find long-lost relatives and fill in family
trees, and many had uploaded the results to open databases like GEDmatch.
Investigators realized that a crime-scene DNA profile could be run against those
public databases, not to find the killer himself, but to find his *relatives*,
the cousins and second cousins who shared enough DNA to show a family connection.
Working with the DNA technology firm Parabon and a genetic genealogist, that is
exactly what happened here. The killer's profile, uploaded to GEDmatch, lit up
matches to people who shared his ancestry. From those matches, a genealogist
built family trees backward and forward, narrowing a sprawling web of relations
down, branch by branch, to a single name: William Earl Talbott II, a man who had
never been a suspect, whose DNA was in no criminal database, and who had lived
quietly for thirty years believing the couple in the van were his secret to keep.

The family tree pointed at him. But a pointing finger is not proof, and police
still needed his actual DNA to compare against the killer's. They did not need a
warrant to get it, because Talbott, like all of us, was constantly discarding
himself in public. Investigators followed him and collected an item he threw
away, a discarded cup bearing his DNA. It matched the DNA from the 1987 murders.
Thirty years of anonymity ended with a piece of trash.

Talbott went to trial and was convicted of both murders, the first person in the
United States convicted at trial on the strength of investigative genetic
genealogy. *(The GEDmatch identification and the discarded-cup collection are
drawn from the investigative record and reporting; the appellate decision anchors
the murders, the DNA and palm-print evidence, and the convictions.)*

## The appeal, and what stuck

Talbott's case took a detour through the appellate courts, but not over the
genealogy. The Washington Court of Appeals initially reversed his convictions
over a question about a biased juror. The Washington Supreme Court reversed that
decision and reinstated the convictions, holding that Talbott had not properly
preserved the juror issue because he had accepted the jury without using an
available challenge to remove the juror in question. On the case's return to the
Court of Appeals, that court struck a firearm sentencing enhancement but
otherwise affirmed. The murder convictions stand.

Tellingly, Talbott's defense at trial did not really contest that his DNA was
there. He conceded he'd had sexual contact with Van Cuylenborg and physical
contact with the van, which his DNA and palm print proved beyond argument. Thirty
years earlier that concession would have been impossible to force, because no one
could have put his name to the evidence. The genealogy did what a generation of
investigators could not: it turned an anonymous profile into a man with an
address.

## How'd he get caught?

He was caught by his relatives, most of whom he had probably never met, and none
of whom did anything but explore their own heritage. William Talbott left his DNA
on his victims in 1987 and then did the one thing that should have kept him safe
forever: he committed his crime before there was any way to connect that DNA to
his name. He waited out the science of his own era and won. What he could not
wait out was a future in which his cousins, of their own free will, would hand
their DNA to companies that would place it in databases a detective could search.
He did not put himself in the system. His family did it for him, without ever
intending to, and a discarded cup did the rest. You can decline to give the
police your DNA. You cannot stop the people who share it from giving theirs.
