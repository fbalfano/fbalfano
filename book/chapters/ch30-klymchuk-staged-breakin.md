# Chapter 30 - The Staged Break-In

**A husband said an intruder had killed his wife with an axe in the drive shed and
escaped through a broken window. The Crown said he had staged the whole scene. And
then the appeal turned on a harder question: how far can an expert go in telling a
jury a scene was staged?**

Maria Klymchuk was killed on Easter Sunday, 1998, in the drive shed about thirty
feet from the family home, the outbuilding where she trained her dogs. She had gone
out around eleven at night for a training session with one of them. She was struck
in the neck and the back of the head several times, and a bloodied axe was found on
the drive shed floor. Her husband, the appellant, said he had found her there,
unconscious, with obvious head injuries.

He also said something else, something the physical scene appeared, at first, to
support: that whoever did this had broken into the drive shed through a window and
fled. There was a torn screen. There were signs of forced entry. To a first
responder, it read as a home, or at least an outbuilding, invaded by a stranger.

The Crown's case was that the stranger did not exist, and that the appellant
himself had staged the break-in to point the police away from the person standing
closest to the body: her husband. And to prove that the scene was a fake, the
Crown reached for a kind of expert the courts were still learning how to handle.

## The expert who read the scene

The Crown called Special Agent Allan Brantley of the Federal Bureau of
Investigation, an expert in what is called crime-scene "staging," the analysis of
whether the physical evidence at a scene reflects what really happened or has been
deliberately arranged to tell a false story. The theory of staging is
intuitive and often powerful: real burglars behave in certain ways, take certain
things, enter and leave by efficient routes, and a scene that departs from those
patterns, a "break-in" that takes nothing, a point of entry no real intruder would
choose, can betray the hand of someone trying to manufacture a crime that never
occurred.

The trouble is that staging analysis, in the wrong hands or pushed too far, slides
from observation into something closer to mind-reading, an expert telling the jury
not just that a scene is unusual but that the accused arranged it and is therefore
guilty. And that is where Maria Klymchuk's case became important beyond its own
tragic facts.

The appellant was convicted. But on appeal in 2005, the Court of Appeal for
Ontario, in reasons that would guide Canadian courts afterward, held that parts of
the staging expert's evidence should not have been admitted, that the testimony had
gone beyond its proper limits. The court quashed the conviction and directed a new
trial. The staging evidence was not worthless, but it had been allowed to reach too
far, to do too much of the jury's work of deciding guilt, and a conviction resting
on it could not stand as it was.

## The scene was still staged

It would be a mistake to read that reversal as a finding that the husband was
innocent, and the later history makes that clear. This was not a case of a wrongly
accused man freed by an appeal; it was a case about *how* a true scene of staging
may be proved to a jury, and about keeping expert opinion within honest bounds.
After the 2005 decision and the further proceedings that followed it, the case
against the appellant did not go away. It was retried, and he was ultimately
convicted again, of second-degree murder, for the killing of his wife. *(The
retrial outcome comes from the case's later history, beyond the 2005 appeal
anchored here.)*

What Klymchuk leaves behind is a lesson that runs through this whole part of the
book like a warning label. The staged scene is one of the richest veins of
evidence a killer can leave, because a person who arranges a lie in physical
objects almost always gets a detail wrong, the untouched valuables, the impossible
point of entry, the break-in that no burglar would bother with. But proving that a
scene is staged is delicate work, and an expert who is allowed to tell the jury not
merely "this scene is inconsistent with a real break-in" but "the accused staged
it and is guilty" has crossed a line the law has to police. The break-in in the
drive shed was, the courts came to accept, a fake. Getting that fact in front of a
jury the right way took two trials.

## How'd he get caught?

He was caught by the same instinct that undoes so many stagers: he built a lie out
of objects, and objects can be read. A torn screen and a forced window were meant
to summon a phantom intruder, but a staged break-in has to convince not just a
frightened first responder but a trained analyst who knows how real intrusions look
and how faked ones fail, and this one did not survive that scrutiny. The wrinkle in
his case, the reason it took two trials, was not doubt about whether the scene was
staged but caution about how confidently an expert could say so to a jury.
Klymchuk got caught by his own set-dressing. He got a second trial because even a
guilty man is entitled to have the staging proved against him, and not simply
pronounced.
