# Chapter 30 - A Suicide Fired From Three Feet Away

**A man cannot shoot himself from three feet away. Jerry Johnson was killed by a
bullet fired from at least that far, with no powder residue around the wound, and
his wife told at least six different stories about how it happened.**

The physics of a self-inflicted gunshot are not negotiable. To shoot yourself, the
gun has to be close, close enough that the burning gases and unburnt powder that
erupt from the muzzle scorch and stipple the skin around the entrance wound. That
sooty halo is the signature of a contact or near-contact shot, and its presence or
absence is one of the first things a pathologist looks for when a death is dressed
up as a suicide. When that halo is missing, and the bullet came from across the
room, the suicide story is over before it starts.

Jerry Wayne Johnson was killed by a .25 caliber handgun in his own home on a night
when only three people were present: Jerry, his wife Mary, and their son, Jerry II.
The scientific evidence was undisputed and unforgiving. The fatal shot had been
fired from three feet or further away, and there was no firearm residue associated
with the entrance wound. Both facts are, as the Texas court put it plainly,
incompatible with suicide. No man shoots himself from three feet away. The gun
does not reach.

## Six stories in search of an alibi

If the forensic evidence quietly closed the door on suicide, Mary Johnson's own
words nailed it shut, because she could not settle on what had happened, and the
sheer number of her attempts is its own kind of confession.

Consider the versions she offered. She told a volunteer firefighter, simply, that
"Jerry has shot himself." She told her son she had fetched the gun at her husband's
request, and that when she sat down in the recliner beside him, it went off. She
told one deputy she had no idea how Jerry got shot, and floated the idea that
"they must have been waiting on him when he came back in from outside," conjuring
a phantom intruder. To an investigator she gave three separate accounts in
sequence: first that she heard a gunshot and found him shot; then that she had
carried the gun into the room, set it by the loveseat arm near his head, and seen
a flash; then, days later, that her husband told her to get the gun, and that she
was holding it in her left hand and fell as she sat down, and it discharged. And
at trial she abandoned all of it for a brand-new story, that their son had woken
her holding the pistol, and that she had asked him, "Son, what do you want me to
do? You want me to kill your daddy?"

A grieving, innocent spouse tells one story, because there is only one truth to
tell. Mary Johnson told at least six, each tailored to the objection it was meant
to answer, and one detail among them betrays the whole enterprise. At one point,
she asked a deputy how long gunshot residue remains on a person's hands. It is not
the question of a woman trying to understand her husband's suicide. It is the
question of a woman who knows there is residue on her hands and is trying to
find out how long she has to worry about it.

The jury convicted Mary Alvis Johnson of murder, and in 2004 the Texas Court of
Appeals affirmed. The gun that supposedly proved a suicide had, in the end, proved
the opposite, because it had spoken in the one language a liar cannot argue with:
the distance a bullet travels, and the marks it does and does not leave.

## How'd she get caught?

She was caught by three feet of empty air. A staged suicide has to obey the body
of the victim, and the body keeps records the stager cannot forge: the scorch of a
close shot, the clean skin of a distant one. Jerry Johnson's wound said the gun
had been three feet away when it fired, and a man's arm is not three feet long, so
the "suicide" was a physical impossibility from the moment the pathologist looked
at the entrance wound. Everything Mary Johnson said afterward only dug the hole
deeper, six shifting stories where an innocent woman would have had one, and a
whispered question about how long residue lasts that told the police exactly whose
hands to test. She tried to write a death as self-inflicted. The wound refused to
cooperate, and so did she.
