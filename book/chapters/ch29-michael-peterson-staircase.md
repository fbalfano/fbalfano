# Chapter 29 - The Blood on the Stairs

**A novelist called 911 to say his wife had fallen down the stairs. The blood on
the walls, on the steps, and on the inside of his own shorts told a different
story. This is also the chapter where the science that convicted him would later
be the reason the case fell apart.**

At 2:40 in the morning on the ninth of December, 2001, a Durham emergency operator
took a call from a distraught man who said his wife had fallen down the stairs. She
was still breathing, he said. How many steps had she fallen? "15, 20, I don't
know." He hung up. Moments later he called back to say she was no longer breathing,
and hung up again. When the paramedics arrived less than eight minutes after that
first call, they walked into a scene that did not look like any fall any of them had
ever answered.

The dead woman was Kathleen Peterson, a Nortel executive who had climbed seventeen
years to the top of her company. Her husband, the novelist Michael Peterson, said
he had stepped outside to turn off the lights by the pool, come back in, and found
her at the bottom of the staircase. The house was one of the grandest in Durham. And
by the account of the very first people through the door, it was drenched in a
quantity and pattern of blood that a tumble down fifteen steps does not produce.

## What a fall does not do

Paramedic James Rose had been to thirty or forty calls involving falls in his
career. The worst injury he had ever seen from one was a broken neck. He testified
that he had never seen wounding to the back of a head like the wounding on Kathleen
Peterson. There was, he said, an enormous amount of blood, and much of it on the
walls was already dry. The blood on the stairs looked as though it had been wiped,
or wiped on, smeared rather than dripped. A fall is a single sudden event. This
looked like something that had taken time.

Then there was the body itself. The autopsy found seven separate lacerations to the
back and side of Kathleen Peterson's scalp, each one, the forensic pathologist
concluded, caused by a separate impact. Seven. The pathologist had reviewed two
hundred eighty-seven North Carolina deaths attributed to falls down stairs, and
studied twenty-nine of them closely in Kathleen's age range. Of those twenty-nine,
seventeen had no scalp lacerations at all, and twelve had exactly one. Kathleen
Peterson had seven, on a skull that, tellingly, was never fractured. Whatever struck
her again and again had split the scalp without shattering the bone beneath it,
which is what a beating with something that is not quite solid does, and what a
staircase does not. The injuries, the pathologist said, were not consistent with a
fall. They were consistent with an assaultive, beating-type pattern. There were
bruises on Kathleen's arms and hands that she read as defensive wounds, the marks of
someone throwing up her hands against the blows.

## The witness on the inside of his shorts

The blood did not only accuse the staircase of innocence. It accused the man who
called it in.

Both paramedics noticed that Michael Peterson had blood on his shirt, his hands, his
arms. That, on its own, proves nothing; a grieving husband who cradles his injured
wife will be bloodied. But it was not just on him. It was on him in a pattern. His
shirt was not merely soaked; it was speckled, spattered with the fine spray that
flies off a surface being struck. Investigators later found blood spatter on his
tennis shoes and, most damningly, on the inside of the right leg of his shorts.

Sit with that last detail, because it is the one that does the quiet work. Blood on
the outside of a man's clothing is the blood of a rescuer. Blood driven onto the
inside of his shorts, on the leg, is spatter that arrived while he was standing over
a source throwing off droplets low to the ground, in a posture no man adopts to
help. Special Agent Duane Deaver of the State Bureau of Investigation performed a
blood-spatter analysis of the scene, tracing more than ten thousand drops across the
stairwell, the walls, the molding inside and out. To the State, that spatter was not
the geometry of an accident. It was the geometry of an arm rising and falling in a
confined space, and the man swinging it had been wearing those shorts.

There was a motive to hang it on, too: mounting debt, more money leaving the
accounts than coming in, a substantial life-insurance benefit that would fall to the
husband, and emails in which Peterson had tried to arrange an encounter with a male
prostitute, a private life the marriage did not know about. And there was a ghost.
The prosecution was permitted to tell the jury about Elizabeth Ratliff, a friend of
Peterson's found dead at the bottom of a staircase in Germany in 1985, her body
discovered by, of all people, Michael Peterson, who had been the last to see her
alive. Two women, two staircases, two pools of blood, one man present at both.

The jury deliberated after a trial that ran five months and convicted Michael
Peterson of first-degree murder. He was sentenced to life without parole. In 2007
the Supreme Court of North Carolina affirmed, holding that the blood-spatter and
medical evidence had painted a beating, not a fall.

## The chapter that turns on itself

And here the story does something none of the others in this part quite do. It
turns on the very science that anchored it.

Michael Peterson's conviction rested to a striking degree on blood-spatter
interpretation, the reading of those ten thousand drops and the speckles on his
clothing as the signature of a bludgeoning. Years later, the analyst who did that
reading, Duane Deaver, was exposed as part of a State Bureau of Investigation
scandal in which examiners were found to have overstated, misreported, and shaded
their findings toward the prosecution across many cases. Deaver's work was
discredited. In 2011 a judge granted Michael Peterson a new trial, finding that
Deaver had given materially misleading testimony. Peterson never faced that full
second trial. In 2017, in what is called an Alford plea, he pleaded guilty to
manslaughter while maintaining his innocence, was credited with time served, and
walked out a free man. *(The Deaver scandal, the 2011 new-trial order, and the 2017
Alford plea all come from the case's later history, after the 2007 decision anchored
here.)*

So was the blood on the inside of those shorts the truth, or was it a story a
flawed analyst told too confidently? The staircase has been argued both ways for
two decades, in a documentary watched around the world and in courtrooms that could
not make up their collective mind. What is not in dispute is the shape of the
lesson. Bloodstain-pattern analysis is one of the most persuasive forensic tools a
prosecutor can hold, because blood does move according to physics and a spattered
scene really can betray a beating dressed up as an accident. It is also one of the
most dangerous, because so much of it lives in the interpretation, and an examiner
who wants a beating badly enough can find one in the drops. Kathleen Peterson's
staircase is where both of those truths stand in the same stairwell.

## How'd he get caught?

He was caught by the blood, which said what he would not: seven wounds where a fall
leaves none, a scalp split without a skull cracked, walls dried and stairs smeared
in a way no eight-minute accident explains, and spatter thrown onto the inside of
his own shorts as he stood over the source of it. The staircase he blamed could not
have done that, and the ghost of a second woman on a second staircase made the
coincidence unbearable. That is how the State caught Michael Peterson. Whether the
catching should have held is the harder question, because the same blood evidence
that convicted him was later found to have been read by an analyst the courts could
no longer trust, and a case built on the certainty of spatter came undone on the
uncertainty of the man reading it. He was caught by the blood on the stairs. He went
free because the science that read it could not, in the end, be trusted to have read
it straight.
