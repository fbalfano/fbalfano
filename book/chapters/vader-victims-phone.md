# The Phone That Outlived Its Owners

**A retired couple vanished on a summer road trip and were never seen again. Two
hours after they disappeared, someone used their cell phone to call an old
girlfriend. That someone was not on their Christmas-card list.**

On the third of July, 2010, Lyle and Marie McCann, a retired married couple, left
Edmonton in a large motor home towing a Hyundai SUV, bound for a family vacation in
British Columbia. A store camera in St. Albert recorded them buying groceries and
gasoline, ordinary people doing an ordinary errand, and at 10:08 that morning they
pulled onto Highway 16 heading west. No one who knew them ever saw them alive again.
Their bodies have never been found.

What happened between that grocery run and their disappearance had to be
reconstructed almost entirely from the trail their killer could not stop leaving.
And the first thread in that trail was the most modern kind of witness there is: a
telephone that kept working after its owners were gone, and kept a record of every
person the new man in possession of it decided to call.

## Two hours later, a phone call

That summer, Travis Vader was unemployed, penniless, and deep in a substance-abuse
problem. On July 3 he drove to a friend's place in a Ford F350 truck he had stolen
five days earlier. The truck was so far gone that it needed oil and Vader had no
money for any; the friend, Olson, handed him a quart of lawn-mower oil to pour into
it. Vader left around 12:15 in the afternoon, broke, in a stolen truck running on
lawn-mower oil. It is worth fixing that picture in mind, because of what he was
driving, and spending, a few hours later.

Two hours after he left, Vader made a phone call. He used Lyle and Marie McCann's
cell phone to ring a former girlfriend, and then used it again to make more calls
and send text messages. Every one of those calls and texts was a record, logged by
the network, of the McCanns' phone being operated by someone who was not the
McCanns, at a time when the McCanns had just dropped off the face of the earth. A
phone does not care whose thumb is on it. It simply notes the number dialed, the
message sent, the tower it pinged, and the minute it happened, and hands that
ledger to anyone who later asks.

By about 5:15 that afternoon Vader was back at Olson's, and the picture had changed
completely. The penniless man in the stolen truck was now driving the McCanns'
SUV, and he had money. He peeled off fifty dollars for Olson to go buy beer, plus
some airtime for Vader's own phone, which had run dry days earlier. Olson came back
with Boxer beer. The two men drank some, and around seven Vader drove off, still in
the McCanns' SUV, taking the rest of the Boxer beer with him.

## The bonfires that proved nothing, and the SUV that proved everything

Vader clearly understood that physical evidence had to disappear, and he set about
making it disappear with fire. Two days later he drove the McCanns' motor home to a
remote spot west of Edmonton and burned it to nothing; no remains were found in the
ashes. A few days after that, he took the stolen Ford truck to another lonely place
and torched that too.

Here is the trouble with burning your way out of a murder: you have to burn all of
it, and Vader kept the one thing he should have destroyed. He abandoned the
McCanns' SUV instead of burning it, and on July 16 it was found and handed to
forensic examiners, who read it like a diary. Inside was blood from both Lyle and
Marie McCann. There was a Boag baseball cap, identical to the one Mr. McCann had
been wearing on the store video that morning, and it had a bullet hole through the
front and was stained with his blood. There were the groceries the couple had
bought at the Superstore, some of them, the trial judge found, sprayed with blood
before being loaded into the vehicle. And there, sitting in the middle of a double
homicide, was a can of Boxer beer wearing Travis Vader's fingerprints, alongside
his own blood. The man had incinerated the motor home and the truck with
professional thoroughness and then left the getaway vehicle parked with his prints
on the beer and the victims' blood on the upholstery. He remembered the two
bonfires and forgot the crime scene on wheels.

The trial judge, after a careful review, concluded that Vader had killed both
McCanns in the course of an armed robbery. On appeal he did not even seriously
dispute that he was the killer; the Court of Appeal noted that the fact he killed
the McCanns during a robbery and hid their remains had been proven beyond any
reasonable doubt and was "not disputed on this appeal." What he fought about was
something narrower, and it is the reason this case ends on an unusual legal note.

## Manslaughter, not murder, and why the distinction survived appeal

The trial judge had originally convicted Vader of murder, but he reached that result
by leaning on section 230 of the Criminal Code, a provision that had been struck
down years earlier because it allowed a murder conviction without proof that the
killer actually intended to kill. Realizing the error, the judge vacated the murder
convictions and substituted convictions for manslaughter, and sentenced Vader to
life imprisonment.

On appeal, Vader argued the whole thing was so tangled that he deserved a stay or at
least a fresh trial on manslaughter. In 2019 the Court of Appeal of Alberta
disagreed and dismissed his appeal. The judge's reasons, it held, showed he had a
reasonable doubt about whether the killings were intentional, which is exactly why
they were manslaughter and not murder, and the failure to spell that out had not
prejudiced Vader. The manslaughter convictions stood. There was, the court said, no
benefit in a retrial to test again what the evidence had already established.

This is not a tidy "murderer gets life for murder" ending, and the book is honest
about that. Vader was convicted of the unlawful killing of the McCanns, in a robbery,
and sentenced to life; the courts simply declined to call it murder because no one
could prove he set out that morning intending to kill. What is not in doubt, and
never was on appeal, is who did it, and how the state came to know.

## How'd he get caught?

He was caught, at the very start, by a phone that did not belong to him. The instant
Travis Vader used Lyle and Marie McCann's cell phone to call an ex-girlfriend, two
hours after the couple vanished, he tied himself by the wrist to the exact people
who had just disappeared, and the network wrote it all down: the number, the texts,
the time. Everything after that was corroboration piled on corroboration, his prints
on a beer can in the victims' SUV, his blood beside theirs, the bullet-holed cap.
He was thorough enough to burn a motor home and a truck to ash, and careless enough
to place a call on the dead couple's phone and to leave the getaway car sitting where
anyone could find it. A killer can destroy a great deal of evidence with a can of
gasoline. He cannot un-ring a telephone. Vader made his first and worst mistake
before he ever struck a match, when he picked up a phone that would keep telling the
truth long after its owners could not.
