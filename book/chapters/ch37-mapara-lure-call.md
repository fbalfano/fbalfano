# Chapter 37 - The Tapped Phone and the Lure Call

**A used-car dealer's role in a murder was simple: get the victim to the lot, where
the shooters were waiting. He made the call to set the meeting. He did not know the
police were already on the line.**

On the seventh of October, 1998, Vikash Chand was shot to death on a used-car lot in
British Columbia, a place with the unimprovable name of Rags to Riches Motor Cars. The
lot belonged to Sameer Mapara. The Crown's theory of the killing divided the labor the
way conspiracies often do: others planned it, others pulled the triggers, and
Mapara's assigned job was the quiet, essential one. He was the lure. His task was to
get Vikash Chand to come to the lot, at a time when the men waiting to kill him would
be there too.

The trouble with being the lure is that luring is done with words, and words, in the
autumn of 1998 as much as now, travel over telephones. And at least one of the phones
in this conspiracy was being listened to.

## The call that was already tapped

The police had a wiretap authorization targeting one of the players, a man named in
the order. During an intercepted three-way phone call, the various conspirators
passed the receiver around, and at one point Mapara himself was on the line. While
that call was live, Chand telephoned Mapara on Mapara's own phone, and the wiretap
caught Mapara's side of it. In that snatch of conversation, Mapara told the victim to
come meet him at the Rags to Riches lot in fifteen minutes. When that call ended,
Mapara turned back to the tapped line and reported the arrangement to his fellow
conspirator: the victim was on his way.

Read those two beats together, because that is the entire machinery of a first-degree
murder laid bare in a few recorded seconds. Fifteen minutes to the lot, Mapara told
the man who had minutes to live. Then, to the organization: he's coming. The victim
was steered to the place of his execution by the very phone call the police were
recording. It is one thing to be caught confessing after the fact. Mapara was caught
in the act of committing his part of the crime, narrating the trap as he set it,
into a microphone he did not know was there.

## What the recording proved

Murder becomes first-degree murder, in Canadian law, when it is planned and
deliberate. A man who lures the victim to a chosen spot at a chosen time, and then
confirms to the killers that the target is en route, is not caught up in a sudden
quarrel. He is executing a plan. The lure call was the plan made audible.

Mapara fought the evidence on appeal, and his case became an important one for reasons
that reach beyond his own guilt. Much of the legal argument concerned technical rules
of evidence, whether certain out-of-court statements by co-conspirators could be used
against him, and whether the police had stayed within the terms of their wiretap
authorization when they kept listening during the parts of the three-way call. These
are the unglamorous mechanics by which a recording becomes admissible proof, and the
courts worked through them carefully. In the end, in 2005, the Supreme Court of Canada
dismissed his appeal and upheld his conviction for first-degree murder. The evidence
that he had lured Vikash Chand to the lot, the Court accepted, made his conviction for
the planned and deliberate killing effectively inevitable.

## How'd he get caught?

He was caught doing his job on a wire. Mapara's role in the conspiracy was to speak,
to coax the victim into range with a friendly instruction to swing by the lot in
fifteen minutes, and speaking is exactly the act a wiretap exists to capture. He never
had to be turned, tricked, or worn down into a confession, because the police did not
need him to describe the crime later; they had recorded him helping to commit it in
real time, first steering Chand to his death and then reporting up the chain that the
target was coming. The lure only works if the victim can hear you. So can everyone
else on the line. Sameer Mapara talked a man into a parking lot to be shot, and the
one witness he could not intimidate, bribe, or outrun was the tape.
