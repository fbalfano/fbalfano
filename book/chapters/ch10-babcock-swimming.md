# Chapter 10 - If You Go Swimming

**They left no body, no crime scene, and no cause of death. What they left was
a rap song bragging about where they threw her phone.**

To convict someone of murder, the law does not require a body. It requires
proof. This chapter is about the most thoroughly self-documented murder in
Canadian law; a case where the victim vanished without a trace, and her
killers were convicted almost entirely out of their own phones.

Laura Babcock was 23, a Toronto woman who had briefly dated a rich young man
named Dellen Millard. Millard's father was a wealthy aviation entrepreneur;
Dellen, in his late twenties, enjoyed the family properties: a house at 5
Maple Gate Court in Etobicoke, an airport hangar near Waterloo, a farm. His
close friend Mark Smich came from the opposite world; a drug dealer with a
record, whom Millard kept supplied with food, drugs, cash for odd jobs, and a
place to stay. Together with other hangers-on, the two liked to go on what
they called "missions": petty crimes, done for the thrill.

In February 2012, Laura told Millard's girlfriend, Christina Noudga, that she
had recently slept with Millard. Noudga was furious. And in April 2012, in
text messages that a jury would one day read, Millard made his girlfriend a
promise. He was going to "remove [Laura] from [their] lives." He would first
"hurt her" and then "make her leave."

Noudga texted back that this made her "feel really loved and all warm on the
inside."

## The shopping list

What elevates this case from a jealous threat to first-degree murder is what
Millard did next, all of it timestamped.

By May 4, 2012, he was asking a friend to keep him updated "on where [Laura]
goes out to." Over the following two months, with Smich's knowledge and help,
he set about acquiring an incinerator capable of cremating a body. His cover
story was that he planned to go into the animal-cremation business with his
uncle, a veterinarian; the uncle later testified he'd never heard of any such
plan and called the idea "absurd." A homemade incinerator was attempted and
failed. So Millard ordered a commercial unit, shipped June 20, delivered to
his hangar on July 5. Stamped on its side was the brand name: **The
Eliminator**.

In the same window, between June 30 and July 3, Millard bought a .32-calibre
handgun from a man named "Isho," a friend of Smich's sister, and arranged for
ammunition. A year later, police would find a .32 Smith and Wesson revolver at
5 Maple Gate Court with Millard's DNA on the handle.

A gun, an incinerator, and a surveillance request. All before anything
happened to Laura Babcock.

## July 3, in cell towers

On the evening of July 3, 2012, the phone records take over the story, and
they tell it in fifteen-minute increments.

Between 5:40 and 7:03 p.m., Laura's cellphone can be traced from Kipling
subway station to the area of 5 Maple Gate Court. A phone used by Millard
moves through the same locations at the same times; the two of them,
travelling together. At 6:51 p.m., Millard texts Smich: "I'll be back in 15
mins, don't be out front."

At 7:33 p.m., Millard texts Smich again: he is on a "mission" and will be back
in an hour. The two phones, his and Laura's, are now travelling north on
Highway 427. By 8:21 p.m., both are back near Maple Gate. At 10:20 p.m.,
Noudga texts Millard: "I'll see you Friday, have fun doing big things."

Laura Babcock's phone made its last outgoing communication at 7:03 that
evening. She was never heard from again; not by her family, not by the friends
she talked to constantly. Her bank account and credit card went silent. She
left behind an envelope holding $1,000 in cash, and her beloved dog.

The next morning, July 4, her phone died for good around 11:00 a.m. In its
final minutes it can be tracked moving along Lakeshore Boulevard, beside Lake
Ontario. A phone used by Millard travels the same route at the same time. The
Crown's theory was simple: he was driving along the lake, and he threw her
phone in.

## "How's the spliff"

That same morning, at 10:00 a.m., Millard texted Smich: "I rolled my first
spliff." At 2:40 p.m., Millard's phone took a photograph of something rolled
in a tarp, consistent in size and shape with a human body; the tarp's metal
grommets are visible in the picture, and part of his father's vehicle, which
has an elevating gate, appears in the background. At 3:21 p.m., Smich texted
back: "How's the spliff."

When police found The Eliminator a year later, in the course of a different
murder investigation, five grommets matching the tarp in the photograph were
in the ash inside it.

Millard's calendar for July 7 contained the entry "barn smell check."

The incinerator arrived July 5, and the two men spent days getting it
operational, building a trailer so the huge unit could travel. On July 21,
Millard texted, "Just picked up the BBQ, seems ready to go," and asked Smich
to come over without his girlfriend. Smich's reply: "She can wait in the front
while we [are] out back talkin to that girl … if [you] know what I mean." They
discussed using it "tomorrow after dark."

On July 23 at about 3:00 p.m., Millard sent the text this case will always be
remembered for: **"BBQ has run its warm up, it's ready for meat."**

That night, Smich's girlfriend was driven to the farm and told to wait in the
car with her headphones on. The two men spent half an hour in the barn, then
drove to the hangar towing the incinerator; she saw smoke coming from it. At
10:38 p.m., Millard's phone ran an internet search: *"what temperature is
cremation done at?"* Just after 11:14 p.m., his phone photographed Smich
standing in front of the running Eliminator, smiling, holding an ash rake.
Four minutes later it photographed the inside of the incinerator, in
operation. Bone fragments can be discerned in the frame.

## The song

At 12:40 a.m. on July 24, within two hours of the cremation, a file was
created on an iPad; Laura's own iPad, which had been plugged into Millard's
computer the morning after she vanished and renamed "Mark's iPad." The file
held rap lyrics Smich was composing:

> The bitch started off all skin and bone,
> now the bitch lay on some ashy stone,
> last time I saw her was outside the home,
> and if u go swimming u can find her phone.

He performed it. In August 2012, in his mother's garage, Smich rapped it for
two high-school boys he sold drugs to, then explained that "they" had torched
a woman's body and thrown a cellphone in the lake. In September, he performed
it again in Millard's basement, and Millard recorded the performance on his
iPhone, where police later found it. Smich was ultimately found in possession
of Laura's iPad and her red suitcase.

A jury convicted both men of first-degree murder in December 2017. In June
2023, the Court of Appeal for Ontario dismissed every ground of both
conviction appeals. The only relief either man received was technical: the
Supreme Court had struck down consecutive parole-ineligibility periods in
another case, so their 25-year terms for Laura's murder now run alongside,
rather than after, the ones they were already serving for murdering Tim Bosma
(Chapter 9).

## How'd they get caught?

They were caught because they could not stop performing. The motive was in
Millard's texts to his girlfriend; the planning was in his shopping trail for
a gun and a crematorium; the night itself was in the cell towers, which walked
the jury through an abduction in fifteen-minute steps; the disposal was in a
Google search, a "BBQ" joke, and a photograph of a smiling man with an ash
rake. And when all of that was done, the killers wrote a song about it,
performed it for teenagers, and filmed it. Laura Babcock's body was never
found. It didn't matter. Her murderers had spent a year building the exhibit
list themselves.
