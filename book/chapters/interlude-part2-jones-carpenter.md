# Interlude: The Rules of the Road

*A short framing note for Part II. Not a murder case, but the law that governs
every one of them.*

Before the chapters that follow, in which cell towers and GPS units and geotagged
photos deliver killers to the police, it is worth pausing on a question those
cases quietly assume has an answer: when *can* the police use these tools against
you? A phone that logs your every location is a prosecutor's dream, but it is also,
in the wrong hands, a government's dream, and the same technology that catches a
murderer can track an innocent citizen. Two decisions of the United States Supreme
Court drew the constitutional lines that the American location cases in this book
operate within, and they are worth knowing before we watch the machines testify.

The first is *United States v. Jones* (2012). Antoine Jones was a Washington, D.C.
nightclub owner suspected of dealing cocaine. Investigators obtained a warrant to
attach a GPS tracker to a Jeep registered to his wife, but the warrant let them
install it in the District within ten days, and they installed it on the eleventh
day, in Maryland. In other words, they did it without a valid warrant. Then they
tracked the vehicle's every movement for twenty-eight days and used the data to
tie Jones to a stash house.

The Supreme Court held that this was a "search" under the Fourth Amendment. The
government's act of physically attaching a device to Jones's vehicle, an "effect"
in the language of the Amendment, in order to gather information, was exactly the
sort of trespass the Constitution was written to check. The police cannot simply
bolt a tracker to your car and follow you for a month; doing so triggers the
Fourth Amendment's protections. It is the reason the GPS evidence in the murder
cases that follow had to be obtained properly, and the reason a defendant like
Raja Dosanjh (Chapter 11) would try, however unsuccessfully, to have his car's
tracklog thrown out.

Its companion is *Carpenter v. United States* (2018), which extended the same
caution from a device the police attach to one they merely ask a company to hand
over. There the government had obtained months of historical cell-site location
records, the log of which towers a suspect's phone connected to, without a warrant,
and used them to place him near a string of robberies. The Court held that
acquiring that kind of pervasive, long-term location history is also a search
requiring a warrant, refusing to treat the digital trail we leave with our phone
companies as fair game simply because a third party holds it. *(Carpenter is the
essential companion to this interlude; its own chapter-length treatment can be
slotted here once the decision is in hand.)*

Together, *Jones* and *Carpenter* are the rules of the road for everything in this
part of the book. They do not stop the police from using cell towers and GPS to
catch killers; the chapters that follow are proof that they use them to
devastating effect. What the two decisions insist on is that the power be used
under a warrant, with a judge in the loop, because a tool precise enough to convict
the guilty is precise enough to surveil the innocent, and the line between the two
is drawn not by the technology but by the law that governs it. Keep these rules in
mind as the machines begin to talk.
