# Chapter 13 - He Said He Went Fishing

**His alibi was that he'd been fishing in San Francisco Bay. Four months later
his wife's body washed ashore a mile from the exact spot, and the GPS on his
rental cars showed he kept driving back to it.**

On Christmas Eve 2002, Laci Peterson, eight months pregnant, vanished from her
home in Modesto, California. Her husband, Scott, told police he had spent the day
fishing. Not nearby. He had driven roughly ninety miles to the Berkeley Marina on
San Francisco Bay, launched a boat, fished for an hour or two in the cold, and
come home to find her gone.

It is worth pausing on how strange an alibi that is before a single piece of
forensic evidence enters the room. December 24 in the Bay was gray, damp, and
raw. The marina was nearly empty. A man whose very pregnant wife was at home for
the holidays had, on this account, driven three hours round trip to stand in a
boat in the wind. Even Laci's stepfather, an avid fisherman himself, remarked at
the time that it was an awfully late, awfully cold hour to be going out.

The alibi was a place. And the trouble with building your alibi around a place is
that the place can be watched, and, as it turned out, the place could give up a
body.

## The fisherman who couldn't fish

Before the machines said anything, Scott Peterson's own story kept tripping over
its feet.

He had, it emerged, bought the boat in near-total secrecy. On December 7, the day
after he learned he could no longer hide the existence of his marriage from a
woman he was seeing, someone on his home computer went searching classified ads
for boats. He inspected one the next day and bought it on December 9, taking it
without the anchors that came with it. He never registered it. He never mentioned
it to his father, or to his fisherman stepfather who had invited him out many
times, or to the friend he regularly talked fishing with. A man does not usually
hide a new boat from the exact people who would be delighted by it, unless the
boat is not really for fishing.

The same computers showed searches, on December 8, for boat ramps and nautical
charts, for currents and maps of the Berkeley Marina and the waters around a spot
called Brooks Island. In his boat, police found a homemade anchor made of cement.
In his truck bed, more cement, and a claw hammer dusted with cement powder, which
is a curious amount of masonry for a fishing trip.

And when the state put a genuine expert on the stand, a published author on Bay
fishing, the alibi didn't just weaken, it was dismantled point by embarrassing
point. Brooks Island was the wrong place for the sturgeon Peterson's own searches
suggested he was after; they schooled elsewhere that time of year. Sturgeon want
live bait, not the lures he carried. His rod was too weak to land one anyway.
Reeling one in demands solid anchoring, which his sad little cement block could
not provide. And trolling for sturgeon, the method he claimed to have used, was
flatly illegal. Peterson had constructed an alibi around a kind of fishing that,
in that place, at that time, with that gear, was essentially impossible. He had
picked a cover story he did not know enough to tell.

Then there was the reason for all of it. On December 30, a woman named Amber Frey
walked into a police station. She and Peterson had been dating since November,
seriously enough that he had met her daughter and discussed having children,
seriously enough that she had no idea he had a wife. When she learned the missing
pregnant woman on the news was married to the man she'd been seeing, she went to
the police, and soon she was recording their calls.

## The tracks he couldn't stop making

Here is where this becomes a location chapter, and one of the most quietly damning
in the book.

In January, with a warrant, the Modesto police mounted a surveillance camera
outside Peterson's home and attached GPS tracking devices to his vehicles. Not one
vehicle, because Peterson had taken to renting a rotating series of cars and
trucks, a few days at a time, a habit that itself invites a question. The GPS
data, backed up by officers' own eyes, told a story no innocent explanation ever
quite covered. At least five times that January, Scott Peterson drove the ninety
miles from his home to the Berkeley Marina. On January 5 he went in a gray Subaru,
stayed five or ten minutes, and left. On January 6 he came back in a red Honda,
again for only minutes. On January 9, a white pickup. A different vehicle nearly
every time, each making the same long pilgrimage to the same cold stretch of
water, to stand there briefly and go home. On January 11, realizing the police
were onto him, he stopped, and the department shut the surveillance down.

Think about what those trips are, and are not. They are not fishing; he never
launched a boat. They are a man drawn back, again and again, ninety miles each
way, in a carousel of borrowed cars, to the one place on earth he should have
wanted never to see again. The GPS did not record a killing. It recorded
something almost worse: the helpless orbit of a man circling the water he had put
his wife into.

Because that is where she was. In mid-April, after a heavy storm churned the Bay,
a couple walking their dog found the body of a baby near the southern tip of
Brooks Island. The next morning, Laci's body washed ashore, just over a mile from
the same island. DNA confirmed them: Laci and her son, Conner. They had surfaced
at the precise place Scott Peterson said he had gone fishing, and the precise
place his rented cars kept quietly returning to for a month.

Peterson seemed to sense the tide turning before the bodies did. On April 12, the
day before the baby was found, he bought a car using his mother's name. He grew a
goatee, and he dyed his hair. When police, fearing he would run, arrested him on
April 18, the disguise told them everything the GPS already had.

## What the appeal decided

A jury convicted Scott Peterson of the first-degree murder of Laci and the
second-degree murder of Conner, found the multiple-murder special circumstance
true, and sentenced him to death. In 2020 the Supreme Court of California
affirmed the guilt convictions, rejecting his claim that the storm of pretrial
publicity had denied him a fair trial. It did, however, reverse the death sentence,
finding the trial court had made significant errors in selecting the jury that
decided his punishment. *(He was later resentenced to life, a development that
comes from news coverage rather than this decision.)* His convictions for the
murders stand.

## How'd he get caught?

He was caught by the place he chose to hide behind. Scott Peterson wanted an
alibi with distance in it, ninety miles of it, so he pointed to a marina and a
fishing trip he was too ignorant of fishing to fake. But an alibi anchored to a
spot on the map is a hostage to that spot, and this one betrayed him twice. First
the GPS on his ever-changing rental cars caught him returning to that cold water
over and over, the compulsive circling of a man who could not stay away from what
he'd sunk there. Then the Bay itself, on its own schedule, handed Laci and Conner
back to shore within a mile of it. He said he went fishing. The one thing he
managed to catch was himself.
