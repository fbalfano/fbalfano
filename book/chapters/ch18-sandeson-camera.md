# Chapter 18 - The Camera He Installed Himself

**His own security camera caught Taylor Samson walking in with a duffel bag
full of marijuana. No camera on earth ever caught him walking out.**

In August 2015, William Sandeson was days away from starting medical school at
Dalhousie University in Halifax. He was also a drug dealer. So was Taylor
Samson, a Dalhousie physics student who lived nearby, and on the night of
August 15 the two arranged a deal at Sandeson's apartment on Henry Street: a
large quantity of marijuana, for which Sandeson was to pay $40,000.

Samson told his girlfriend he'd be back soon. He left his wallet, his keys,
and his prescription medication behind. At about 10:30 p.m., a security camera
recorded him arriving at Sandeson's building with Sandeson, carrying a large
black duffel bag.

That was the last time anyone saw Taylor Samson. His body has never been
found.

The camera that recorded his arrival was Sandeson's own; a surveillance
system he had installed himself, wired to a DVR inside his apartment. It
would become one of the Crown's best witnesses, twice over: once for what it
recorded, and once for when it mysteriously didn't.

## The gunshot across the hall

Across the hall lived Pookiel McCabe, and that evening McCabe had a friend
over, Justin Blades. Sandeson had been hanging out with them, then went back
to his own apartment. Some time later, the two men heard a single gunshot.
Sandeson's camera caught him at McCabe's door moments afterward; both men
described him as in a state of shock or panic.

What they said they saw when they crossed the hall would anchor the eventual
trial: a man slumped over in a chair in Sandeson's apartment, with blood, cash,
and drugs. When Blades returned a short time later, he saw streaks of blood
running from the chair toward the bathroom. Sandeson said he had to clean up,
and asked to borrow Blades's car. Blades refused.

Then the record goes dark, literally. The DVR that faithfully recorded the
comings and goings of the building did not record between roughly 11:30 p.m.
and 1:00 a.m. Whatever happened to Taylor Samson's body in those ninety
minutes, the camera's owner made sure it happened off screen.

When Sandeson's girlfriend arrived at 12:30 a.m., the apartment smelled of
bleach. Blades noticed the same smell that morning. It later emerged that
Sandeson had asked both his roommate and his girlfriend, in advance, not to be
at the apartment that evening.

## The cover story, in writing

At 2:52 a.m., Sandeson began building his alibi by text message. He wrote to
another man in the drug trade that Samson had taken his money without
delivering the marijuana. He texted Samson's phone, a phone whose owner no
one would ever hear from again, complaining that Samson hadn't come back with
"that stuff."

Sunday morning, his own camera filmed him cleaning out the trunk of his car.
Samson's DNA was later found in that trunk. Monday morning, the camera filmed
him carrying garbage bags out of the apartment, wearing gloves. Cell tower
records put his phone in Truro on Tuesday, where his family kept a farm;
investigators later found items there matching what he'd carried out,
including a shower curtain and a large duffel bag. Samson's DNA was on them.

The police reached Sandeson through one more digital thread: the last call to
Samson's phone traced to an IP address at a group home where Sandeson worked.
When he came in voluntarily on August 18, he told a tidy story: he hadn't seen
Samson since the 13th; the man simply never showed up on the 15th. Then he
handed police his phone and let them photograph his texts, apparently
confident his story would hold. The messages said otherwise. They showed a
major deal, and they showed Samson had arrived. The inconsistencies, and fear
that Samson might still be alive somewhere, sent police into the apartment
that evening, where they saw the surveillance DVR and an empty box for a
handgun; the gun itself was gone.

A jury convicted William Sandeson of first-degree murder.

## The twist: betrayed by his own investigator

What happened next is one of the strangest episodes in Canadian criminal
procedure, and it is the reason this particular judgment exists.

Blades and McCabe, the two men who saw the aftermath, had originally told
police they knew nothing. They were afraid. What changed? Mid-trial, the
defence learned the answer: a private investigator working for *Sandeson's own
defence team* had located the two witnesses, won their confidence, and then
quietly steered them to the police, encouraging investigators to re-interview
them. The police welcomed the help and took steps to keep the investigator's
name confidential. Nobody told the defence that its own agent had delivered
the Crown's two eyewitnesses.

The defence moved for a mistrial. The trial judge agreed there had been a
disclosure breach but refused the mistrial, offering a short adjournment
instead. In June 2020, the Nova Scotia Court of Appeal held that was not
enough. The right to make full answer and defence includes the right to bring
Charter challenges to how evidence was gathered, and the late disclosure had
gutted that ability; nor were the police mere passive recipients of a tip, but
"active participants in a common venture" with the defence's own
investigator. The conviction was set aside and a new trial ordered.

Note what the court did not say: it never suggested the evidence pointed
anywhere but at Sandeson. The judgment is a lesson in process, and the facts
it recites are the reason process is all the defence had. At the retrial in
2023, a second jury heard the case without the tainted shortcut and convicted
Sandeson of second-degree murder. *(The retrial outcome is news-sourced, not
part of this decision; verify current status before print.)*

## How'd he get caught?

He was caught by his own infrastructure. The camera he installed recorded the
victim walking in and never walking out; its ninety-minute silence marked the
disposal window as clearly as any recording could have. His texts to a dead
man's phone were a confession to staging. His car, his farm, and his gloves
all carried Samson's DNA forward anyway. And when he sat across from police
and handed over his phone to prove his story, the phone told them he was
lying. William Sandeson surveilled everyone, including himself. The only thing
his system couldn't record over was the truth.
