# Chapter 36 - The Sting That Went Too Far

**For thirty-two years, Michel Laflamme had gotten away with strangling his wife and
staging her body in a shopping-mall parking lot. Then the police built a fake
criminal empire to make him confess. He did confess. And a court threw the whole
thing out anyway.**

This part of the book is a catalogue of killers talked into their own undoing, men
who told the truth to the wrong stranger. Michel Laflamme belongs here for the
opposite reason. His is the chapter where the state, having finally caught a man who
had eluded it for three decades, caught him the wrong way, and a court took the win
off the board. It is the counterweight the whole part needs, because a book about how
confessions catch killers has to be honest about the confessions the law refuses to
keep.

In December 1976, Carmen Waltz was seen shopping at a mall in Beloeil, Quebec, less
than two kilometers from the home her husband had built. A little before five in the
afternoon, Michel Laflamme was in the Canadian Tire at that same mall. Around five,
he was in the parking lot flagging down passers-by, calling for help for his wife,
who lay unconscious in his car. One of the people who came over noticed the purse on
the back seat had been emptied out across it, and a handkerchief that looked freshly
stained with blood. When the ambulance crew lifted the fur coat draped over Carmen,
they found a red mark on her neck and her clothing disarranged. She had been
strangled with a ligature. She was already gone.

The scene was staged to look like a woman attacked or taken ill in a parking lot. It
held. Detectives searched the couple's home and found nothing amiss. A coroner's
inquest the next spring named no killer. Laflamme was a suspect and was never charged.
He had, by every appearance, gotten away with it.

## The empire built to catch one man

Thirty-two years later, the police came back for him with a technique that did not
exist in 1976: the Mr. Big operation. Undercover officers posing as a criminal
organization drew Laflamme into a fictional underworld, running him through scenario
after scenario, building the illusion of a powerful gang that could solve any
problem, offer any protection, so long as a man was honest about his past. Forty
scenarios in, Laflamme was brought to the staged meeting with the boss, "Mr. Big,"
the forty-first scene. There, on the ninth of April, 2008, he confessed. He said he
had strangled Carmen in their home, loaded her into the car waiting in the garage,
and driven her to the mall, with their eighteen-month-old daughter along, then walked
into the Canadian Tire with the child and come back out to perform the discovery of
his wife's body.

It was a detailed confession, and it matched the staging investigators had puzzled
over in 1976. He was convicted of first-degree murder in 2010.

## Why the confession did not count

And then, in 2015, the Quebec Court of Appeal set the conviction aside and ordered a
stay of proceedings, which is the law's way of saying not "you are innocent" but "the
state cannot be allowed to have done what it did to get here."

The court's concern was abuse of process. A Mr. Big operation works by manufacturing
inducements and, sometimes, an atmosphere of implied menace: confess your worst deeds
to the organization, or lose its protection and favor. There is a point at which the
pressure and the enticement become so overwhelming that the resulting confession can
no longer be trusted as freely given, and the conduct of the police becomes something
a court will not put its name to. Laflamme testified that his confession was false,
that he had told Mr. Big what the fictional boss wanted to hear because he feared the
organization. Weighed against the way this particular operation had been run, the
appeal court concluded the confession could not stand and that the proceedings had to
be stayed.

So Michel Laflamme walked out of a first-degree murder conviction, not because the
staging in that parking lot was ever explained, and not because anyone else was ever
suggested, but because the tool that finally extracted his words was found to have
been pushed past the line the law draws around it. The same Mr. Big technique that,
in other chapters, put men away for life here produced a confession the courts would
not use.

That is not a comfortable ending, and it is not meant to be. It sits in this book as
the necessary warning label on every confession chapter around it. A confession is
the most powerful evidence there is and the most treacherous, because human beings
can be pressured, frightened, and flattered into saying things, and a legal system
that wants convictions badly enough can build a machine designed to produce them. The
line between catching a killer and manufacturing a confession is real, and Laflamme
is where a court decided the machine had crossed it.

## How'd he get caught, and why didn't it hold?

He was caught, in the first instance, the way every stager in this book is caught: by
a scene that did not add up, a purse emptied for show, a body arranged in a parking
lot, a red ligature mark under a fur coat. But the staging alone was never enough to
charge him, and for thirty-two years it wasn't. What finally produced a case was his
own confession to a criminal empire that existed only in the minds of the police who
built it. The confession was detailed, and it fit. It also came from a man who said
he was frightened into giving it, wrung out by an operation the Court of Appeal
decided had gone too far. So the answer to "how'd he get caught" is: by his own
mouth, and then he was un-caught by the same mouth, because a free country does not
let the state win at any cost. Laflamme was undone by a confession. The confession was
undone by how it was taken.
