# Chapter 11 - Never Rent a Getaway Car

**He rented the getaway car under a fake name to keep it far from him. The fake
name is the exact reason the car's testimony could never be thrown out.**

There is a certain kind of criminal who plans everything, and Raja Dosanjh was
that kind of criminal, right up until the part where the planning turned around
and bit him.

At 4:38 on the afternoon of March 1, 2016, Aly Sunderani stepped out of his
Range Rover under the carport of the Comfort Inn in Guelph, Ontario. He managed
the place. It was his workplace, an ordinary Tuesday, the sun still up. A man
walked up to him and opened fire with a submachine gun, hit him thirty-one
times, three of the rounds finding his heart, dropped the weapon beside the
body, slid into a waiting black Infiniti QX60, and was gone before the echo
faded.

No masked prowler in the dark. No body spirited away to a shallow grave. A
killing committed in broad daylight, in front of a hotel lobby, on camera. And
yet the case that followed was built entirely on circumstance, because the one
thing all those cameras never quite caught was the gunman's face.

What the Crown had instead, when Dosanjh finally stood trial three years later,
were four pillars, and the fourth is the one that makes this a chapter and not
just a tragedy. The first three were solid enough: his DNA on the grip of the
gun left at the scene, a mix some 3.7 million times likelier to include him than
not. The getaway Infiniti, rented from a Mississauga agency a few hours before
the shooting by a man calling himself "Jaspinder Nagra," who was, nobody
disputed, Dosanjh. And the rental agency's cameras, which had a good long look
at that man's pants, shirt, and shoes, the very outfit worn by the gunman on the
hotel's video, filmed from the chest down.

The fourth pillar was the car itself. Because the Infiniti, it turned out, had
been keeping a diary.

## The car that kept a diary

The QX60 came with an infotainment system, the glossy touchscreen that runs the
maps and the music and, like nearly every car built this decade, quietly logs
everywhere it goes without being asked and without ever forgetting. When Ontario
Provincial Police pulled the tracklog, they didn't get a vague sense of the
vehicle's day. They got an itinerary a travel agent would be proud of:

**1:16 p.m.** Rolls out of the rental lot in Mississauga.
**1:28 p.m.** Arrives at the home of the parents of Dalvir Passi, Dosanjh's
brother-in-law. Sits eleven minutes.
**3:52 p.m.** Pulls into the Comfort Inn lot in Guelph and parks around back,
where it waits the better part of an hour, patient as a cat.
**4:38 p.m.** At the exact minute Aly Sunderani is shot, rolls to the front of
the hotel, then bolts.

From there the diary keeps writing: back to the Passi parents' place, then on to
the home of Dosanjh's sister and brother-in-law. And the hotel's own surveillance
video confirmed the car's account frame for frame, two witnesses telling the
identical story, one of them a Nissan product.

Dosanjh seems to have sensed, on some level, that the car knew too much. The next
evening he returned it to the agency and asked his brother-in-law to pick him up,
but not at the agency itself. He wanted to be collected from a gas station across
a busy street, a careful little moat of distance between himself and the vehicle,
as if the Infiniti were a witness he didn't want to be seen leaving the
courthouse with. The instinct was correct. The execution was three years too late.

## Two bald men and a video game defence

The defence's answer to all this arrived by way of the brother-in-law, and it
was ambitious. Passi testified that after the rental, two tall, white, balding
strangers he had never seen before turned up at his parents' house, borrowed the
QX60 from Dosanjh to run a drug deal, and returned it later that day. And what,
you may ask, were Dosanjh and Passi doing while the Infiniti was allegedly off
committing a submachine-gun murder in another city? They were, Passi said,
playing video games.

The story had exactly one thing going for it, and to be fair it was a real
thing. The Comfort Inn's front-desk manager had watched the shooting through the
lobby window and described the gunman as fair, white-complexioned, "less hairs on
his head." Dosanjh's own lawyer described his client, accurately, as a tall,
brown-skinned young man with a full head of dark hair. It is not nothing to have
an eyewitness put a bald white man where your client's head of hair should be.

But two other eyewitnesses pointed the other way, and juries are asked to weigh
whole cases, not single threads. On one side of the scale: two mysterious bald
men, a borrowed car, and an afternoon of video games no one else witnessed. On
the other: Dosanjh's DNA on the murder weapon, his outfit on two separate
videos, and a car that had personally driven the jury to the crime scene and
parked there at the killing minute. On August 1, 2019, the jury returned a
verdict of first-degree murder, unmoved by the bald men.

## The trap inside the fake name

On appeal, Dosanjh's lawyers went after the diary, and their argument was a good
one. The police, they said, had searched the infotainment system without a
warrant, digging through GPS records that laid bare their client's movements, a
"biographical core" of personal information that section 8 of the Charter is
supposed to protect. If that search was unconstitutional, the tracklog could be
thrown out, and the fourth pillar would come down.

The Court of Appeal for Ontario's answer, handed down by Associate Chief Justice
Fairburn in October 2022, is the dark joke this whole chapter has been walking
toward. Charter privacy claims belong to people who have a genuine connection to
the thing being searched, through ownership, or control, or a right to be where
they were. And what was Dosanjh's connection to the Infiniti? A rental contract
signed by Jaspinder Nagra, a man who does not exist. The GPS data, the court
noted almost drily, came from a vehicle Dosanjh had "no right to be driving." The
synced contact list wasn't even from his own phone. Tracking data, the court
added, sits pretty far down the privacy ladder to begin with, since it shows
where a car went, not the secrets of a soul.

So Dosanjh had no reasonable expectation of privacy, no standing to complain,
and, the court said for good measure, even if the search had crossed a line, the
evidence was reliable and would have come in regardless.

Sit with the shape of that for a second. The fake name was supposed to make the
car impossible to trace back to him. What it actually did was make the car's
memory impossible for him to touch. He had disowned the Infiniti so completely,
so successfully, that when the machine climbed into the witness box and recited
his entire afternoon, he had no legal standing to so much as object. He had won
the argument that it wasn't his car, and the prize was a conviction.

The appeal was dismissed.

## How'd he get caught?

He was caught by the passenger seat, the one part of the plan he never thought
about. The false identity, the two-bald-men story, the gas station across the
street, the whole architecture was engineered to keep Raja Dosanjh's name off
that getaway car. And it worked. The name never stuck. But the car had never
needed his name. It logged the rental lot, the family driveway, the hotel, and
the murder minute all on its own, his DNA rode the gun, and the same cameras
that never saw his face remembered his wardrobe cold. The modern getaway car is
the worst accomplice a killer can choose. It follows you everywhere, remembers
everything, keeps its receipts, and owes you absolutely nothing, least of all
when you've spent three years insisting you were never really its driver.
