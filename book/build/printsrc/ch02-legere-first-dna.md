# Chapter 2 - First Blood

**An escaped killer terrorized a New Brunswick river valley for seven months,
leaving four bodies and almost no witnesses. He was convicted on a technology so
new that no Canadian jury had ever hanged a murder on it before. His blood was
the first.**

Every forensic tool in this book was, at some point, brand new, unproven, and
frightening to the courts asked to trust it. This chapter is about the moment DNA
crossed that threshold in Canada, and it is fitting, and chilling, that the man
who forced the question was one of the most dangerous the country has produced.

Allan Joseph Legere was already a convicted murderer. He was serving time for the
1986 killing of a shopkeeper named John Glendenning in the Miramichi region of New
Brunswick when, in May 1989, he was taken to a Moncton hospital for treatment.
There he slipped his guards and vanished into the New Brunswick spring. For the
next seven months, the communities strung along the Miramichi River lived under a
terror that has few parallels in Canadian history, locking doors they had never
locked, because somewhere out there a killer was moving among them and the police
could not catch him.

He did not waste the time he had stolen.

## Seven months

The bodies came over that terrible stretch of 1989. Annie Flam, who ran a grocery
in Chatham, was killed and her apartment set on fire, the flames meant to erase
whatever the killer had left behind. Two sisters, Linda and Donna Daughney, who
lived together, were murdered in their home, the scene suggesting the killer had
taken his time. And Father James Smith, a Roman Catholic priest, was beaten to
death in his own rectory. Four victims, across months, in a region small enough
that everyone knew someone touched by the horror.

What Legere did not leave, for the most part, was the ordinary evidence that
convicts a killer. No reliable eyewitnesses to the murders themselves. No
confession. He was a careful, brutal man who understood, in the folk way that
criminals do, how not to be seen. In an earlier era, the era of nearly every
murder that came before his, that carefulness might have been enough.

But Legere left something behind that he did not know how to hide, because in 1989
almost no one yet understood it could be seen at all. At the scenes, in the
biological traces of the attacks, in semen and blood, he left his DNA.

## The science on trial

When Legere was finally recaptured in November 1989 and brought to trial, the
Crown's case rested on a technology that had never before grounded a murder
conviction in Canada. DNA typing was, in 1991, genuinely novel. It asked a jury to
convict a man of capital crimes on the say-so of scientists reading patterns in
biological material, patterns invisible to the eye, explained in the unfamiliar
vocabulary of molecular biology and probability. Courts are, rightly, cautious
about new science, and much of Legere's trial and appeal was a contest over
whether this new thing was reliable enough to help send a man to prison for the
rest of his life.

The forensic evidence tied the biological traces from the murder scenes to Allan
Legere, and a jury convicted him in 1991 on four counts of first-degree murder. It
was, by common reckoning, the first murder conviction in Canada built on DNA
evidence. When the case reached the New Brunswick Court of Appeal in 1994, the
court examined the admissibility and the reliability of that evidence, the
foundational question of whether this molecular fingerprinting was sound enough to
be trusted by the justice system, and it upheld the convictions. The appeal was
dismissed. DNA had arrived, and it had arrived by convicting a serial murderer no
older method had managed to touch.

Everything in this book that depends on genetic evidence, the touch DNA under a
victim's fingernails, the cold-case hit decades later, the exonerations that
finally freed innocent men, stands on the door that cases like this one pushed
open. The science had to prove itself somewhere, on someone, first. In Canada, it
proved itself on Allan Legere.

## How'd he get caught?

He was caught by the one part of himself he could not think to burn. Legere was
cunning enough to leave no witnesses, disciplined enough to set fire to a scene to
destroy what he'd left, careful enough to evade a massive manhunt for seven
months. Against the forensic tools of the whole history of murder before him, he
might have stayed a phantom. But he committed his crimes at the exact hinge of
history when a new science was learning to read the body's smallest leavings, and
he had no idea that the blood and fluid he left behind spelled out his name in a
language he could not see and could not wash away. He beat the old ways of getting
caught. He had the terrible misfortune to be among the first men in the country
undone by the new one.
