# Chapter 10 - If You Go Swimming

**They burned the body, sank the phone, and left no crime scene. Then one of them
wrote a rap song about exactly where to find the phone, and performed it on
video.**

The law does not require a body to prove a murder. It requires proof. Dellen
Millard and Mark Smich seem to have understood the first half of that sentence
and given no thought whatsoever to the second, because they went to enormous
trouble to erase Laura Babcock from the physical world and then documented her
killing so thoroughly, so chattily, so proudly, that a jury could reconstruct it
almost entirely from their own phones.

Laura Babcock was twenty-three, a Toronto woman who had briefly dated Millard.
Millard was the son of a wealthy aviation entrepreneur, and at nearly thirty he
was still enjoying the family's toys: a house in Etobicoke, an airport hangar
near Waterloo, a farm. His close friend Smich came from the wrong side of every track
there is, a drug dealer whom Millard kept flush with cash, food, and
somewhere to sleep. The two of them liked to run what they called "missions,"
petty crimes committed for sport.

The trouble started in February 2012, when Laura told Millard's girlfriend,
Christina Noudga, that she had recently slept with Millard. Noudga was not
pleased. And in April, in text messages a jury would one day read back to him
line by line, Millard made his girlfriend a promise. He was going to "remove
[Laura] from [their] lives." He would first "hurt her," then "make her leave."
Noudga replied that this made her feel "really loved and all warm on the inside,"
which tells you most of what you need to know about the emotional weather of this
relationship.

## The shopping list

What lifts this from an ugly threat to premeditated, first-degree murder is what
Millard did next, and he did all of it with the discretion of a man live-tweeting
his own crime.

By May 4 he was asking a friend to keep him posted "on where [Laura] goes out
to." Over the next two months, with Smich's help, he set about acquiring an
incinerator large enough to cremate a human being. His cover story, offered to a
mechanic at his hangar, was that he planned to go into the animal-cremation
business with his uncle, a veterinarian. The uncle later testified he had never
heard of any such plan and pronounced the idea "absurd," with a particular
contempt reserved for the notion of a *mobile* animal-incineration service, which
Millard had also floated. When the homemade unit failed, Millard simply ordered a
commercial one. It arrived at the hangar on July 5, with a brand name stamped
proudly down the side: **The Eliminator**. You cannot make this up, and more to
the point, neither could the defence.

In the same stretch, between June 30 and July 3, Millard bought a .32-calibre
handgun from a friend of Smich's sister and arranged for ammunition. A year
later, police would turn up a matching .32 Smith and Wesson revolver at Millard's
house, wearing his DNA on the handle.

A gun. A crematorium. A request to track the victim's movements. All of it in
place before anything happened to Laura Babcock.

## July 3, in fifteen-minute intervals

On the evening of July 3, 2012, the phone records take over the storytelling, and
they narrate an abduction in increments a bus schedule would envy.

Between 5:40 and 7:03 p.m., Laura's cellphone travels from Kipling subway station
to the area of Millard's Etobicoke house. A phone used by Millard moves through
the identical locations at the identical times, the two of them plainly
travelling together. At 6:51 p.m., Millard texts Smich: "I'll be back in 15 mins,
don't be out front." At 7:33 p.m. he texts again, saying he's on a "mission" and
back in an hour, while both phones, his and Laura's, drift north on Highway 427
and then return. At 10:20 p.m., Noudga texts him: "I'll see you Friday, have fun
doing big things."

Laura Babcock's phone made its last outgoing contact at 7:03 that evening. She
was never heard from again. Not by her family, not by the friends she spoke to
constantly. Her bank account and credit card went silent. She left behind an
envelope with $1,000 in cash and the dog she adored. Her phone finally died the
next morning, July 4, around 11:00 a.m., and in its last minutes it can be
tracked moving along Lakeshore Boulevard, beside Lake Ontario, with a phone used
by Millard travelling the very same route at the very same time. The Crown's
theory was that he was throwing hers into the lake. Remember the lake.

## "It's ready for meat"

If the planning was reckless, the disposal was practically a group project with a
comment thread.

At 10:00 a.m. on July 4, Millard texted Smich, "I rolled my first spliff." At
2:40 p.m., his phone photographed something rolled in a tarp, the size and shape
of a body, its metal grommets visible in the shot, part of his father's vehicle
with its handy elevating gate parked in the background. At 3:21, Smich texted
back, "How's the spliff." When police later found The Eliminator, five grommets
matching the tarp in that photo were sitting in the ash inside it. Millard's
calendar, meanwhile, carried a July 7 entry that read, with breezy horror, "barn
smell check."

Over the following weeks the two men got the incinerator running and built a
trailer so the massive thing could travel. On July 21, Millard texted that he'd
"picked up the BBQ" and asked Smich to come over without his girlfriend. Smich's
reply: "She can wait in the front while we [are] out back talkin to that girl …
if [you] know what I mean." And on July 23, at around 3:00 p.m., Millard sent the
line that will outlive them both: **"BBQ has run its warm up, it's ready for
meat."**

That night, Smich's girlfriend was driven to the farm and told to sit in the car
with her headphones on, which is a difficult instruction to make innocent. The
two men spent half an hour in the barn, then towed the incinerator to the hangar,
where she saw smoke rising from it. At 10:38 p.m., Millard's phone ran a search
that no one has ever run for a wholesome reason: "what temperature is cremation
done at?" Just after 11:14, his phone photographed Smich standing before the
running Eliminator, smiling, holding an ash rake like a proud host at a cookout.
Four minutes later it photographed the inside of the machine, in operation, bone
fragments visible in the frame.

## The song

And then, because apparently nothing about this crime could be kept off the
record, they set it to music.

At 12:40 a.m. on July 24, within two hours of the cremation, a file was created
on an iPad. Not just any iPad. Laura's iPad, which had been plugged into
Millard's computer the morning after she vanished and helpfully renamed "Mark's
iPad." The file contained lyrics Smich was composing:

> The bitch started off all skin and bone,
> now the bitch lay on some ashy stone,
> last time I saw her was outside the home,
> and if u go swimming u can find her phone.

He performed it, too. In a garage that August, Smich rapped it for two high-school
kids he sold drugs to and then explained that "they" had torched a woman's body
and thrown a phone in the lake. In September he performed it again in Millard's
basement, and Millard, ever the archivist, recorded the whole thing on his
iPhone, where police eventually found it. Smich was later caught holding Laura's
iPad and her red suitcase, the last souvenirs of a woman he'd helped burn.

A jury convicted both men of first-degree murder in December 2017. In June 2023
the Court of Appeal for Ontario dismissed every ground of appeal. The only relief
either received was a technicality about how their parole-ineligibility periods
stack, a footnote generated by a different Supreme Court case, and cold comfort
for men already serving life for the murder of Tim Bosma (Chapter 9).

## How'd they get caught?

They could not stop performing. Every single stage of this murder, the motive,
the planning, the abduction, the cremation, the disposal, was committed to a
device, a text, a photograph, a calendar entry, a Google search, and finally a
song they wrote, rapped, and filmed. Laura Babcock's body was burned to fragments
and her phone was thrown, per the lyrics, exactly where you'd go swimming to find
it. It didn't save them. Two men had spent a year assembling the Crown's exhibit
list on her behalf, and one of them had even set it to a beat. The rap ended with
a taunt about a phone at the bottom of a lake. It should have ended with a warning
about the one in his own pocket.
