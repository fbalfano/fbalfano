# Chapter 43 - The Boy, the Flies, and Forty-Eight Years

**A pathologist looked at the food in a dead girl's stomach and declared she had
died within a forty-five-minute window. That window sent a fourteen-year-old boy
to the gallows. It took forty-eight years, and a study of blow flies, to prove
the window was never real.**

This book is called *How Not to Get Away With Murder*, and every chapter until
now has been a story about the guilty being caught. The book cannot honestly end
there, because the same forensic machinery that catches killers is operated by
human beings who make mistakes, and when it fails, it does not simply let a
guilty man walk. Sometimes it takes an innocent one and nearly hangs him. To
close, we have to tell that story too, and in Canada there is no story that tells
it better than the case of a fourteen-year-old boy named Steven Truscott.

On the evening of June 9, 1959, near the Royal Canadian Air Force station outside
Clinton, Ontario, Steven Truscott gave his twelve-year-old classmate Lynne Harper
a ride on the handlebars of his bicycle, north along the County Road. He said he
dropped her near the highway and watched her get into a car. It was, he insisted
then and for the rest of his life, the last he saw of her.

Two days later, searchers found Lynne Harper's body in a wooded area called
Lawson's Bush. She had been sexually assaulted and strangled with her own blouse.
And within days, the boy who had last been seen with her, Steven Truscott, was
arrested, tried as an adult for capital murder, convicted, and sentenced to hang.
The sentence was commuted to life. He was fourteen years old, and every court
that reviewed his case, including a majority of the Supreme Court of Canada on a
1966 reference, upheld the conviction.

The case against him was entirely circumstantial, and it stood on four pillars.
Three of them were the ordinary stuff of a 1950s murder trial: witnesses along
the County Road, disputed evidence about his later conduct, and some physical
findings on his body offered to suggest he was the assailant. But the pillar that
held up all the rest, the one that gave Steven Truscott what the Crown called
"exclusive opportunity" to commit the murder, was a piece of forensic science. It
was the word of a pathologist about the contents of a dead girl's stomach.

## The window

The pathologist was Dr. John Penistan, and he performed Lynne Harper's autopsy.
On the basis of three observations, the degree to which her last meal had digested
in her stomach, the state of the rigor mortis in her body, and the extent of
decomposition, he delivered an opinion of striking, almost surgical precision. He
told the court that Lynne Harper had died between 7:00 and 7:45 p.m. on June 9.

Understand what that narrow window did. Steven Truscott was demonstrably with
Lynne on the County Road shortly after 7:00 p.m. If she died by 7:45, then he,
and effectively only he, had the opportunity to kill her before she could reach
the highway and the car he described. Dr. Penistan's forty-five-minute window was
not merely evidence in the case. It *was* the case. It converted a boy who gave a
girl a ride into the only man on earth who could have murdered her.

And it was built on sand. The science of estimating time of death from stomach
contents, of assuming that a stomach empties on a fixed and knowable schedule,
was exactly the kind of confident, precise-sounding forensic claim that juries
find irresistible and that later scrutiny so often dissolves. Digestion does not
run on a stopwatch. It varies enormously with the person, the meal, the stress,
the fear. A stomach can take hours longer to empty than Dr. Penistan assumed. The
window he drew with such authority was a guess wearing the costume of a
measurement, and a fourteen-year-old's life was hung inside it.

## The flies

In 2007, on a reference from the Minister of Justice, the Court of Appeal for
Ontario examined Steven Truscott's conviction one final time, as though it were a
fresh appeal, and this time it did something no court had done before. It let in
new evidence about the time of death, and that evidence came from two directions,
both of which pointed away from Dr. Penistan's window.

The first was a reassessment of the pathology itself. Modern expert review
rendered the old claim, that Lynne Harper *must* have died before 8:00 p.m.,
scientifically untenable. The certainty was gone, and with it the exclusive
opportunity it had manufactured.

The second came from the humblest witnesses imaginable: the blow flies that had
found Lynne Harper's body. Dr. Penistan, at the autopsy, had removed maggots from
around her nose and mouth and, in his notes, recorded their size, about
one-sixteenth of an inch. Decades later, forensic entomologists took that
recorded detail and did what entomologists do. A blow fly's life runs on a
biological clock so dependable that the size and developmental stage of the
maggots on a body can be read backward to estimate when insects first arrived,
and therefore, roughly, when death occurred and the body lay exposed. Working
from the maggots Dr. Penistan himself had described, the entomology pointed to a
death later than his window allowed, potentially so much later that it could
place the killing at a time when Steven Truscott was nowhere near Lawson's Bush.

The Court of Appeal was careful about the science. It did not declare the
entomology certain; it noted its frailties and acknowledged the assumptions it
rested on. But it held that the evidence was sound enough to go to a jury, and
that, together with the collapse of Dr. Penistan's pathology, it could raise a
reasonable doubt that Lynne Harper died before 8:00 p.m. And a reasonable doubt
about the time of death was a reasonable doubt about the entire case, because the
whole edifice had been built on that single, false window.

The court found that Steven Truscott's conviction was a miscarriage of justice
and quashed it. A new trial, half a century on, was a practical impossibility. So
the court did the only just thing left to do. It held that if a new trial could
be held, an acquittal would clearly be the more likely result, and it entered
that acquittal itself. In 2007, forty-eight years after a fourteen-year-old boy
was sentenced to die, Steven Truscott was declared, at last, an innocent man.

## The question this book cannot avoid

For every other subject in these pages, the closing question has been "how'd they
get caught." For Steven Truscott, the question is the opposite and far more
uncomfortable: how was an innocent boy *convicted*, and how was he made to wait a
lifetime to be uncaught? The answer is the same forensic overconfidence that runs
like a warning through this entire book. A murderer is caught when the evidence,
honestly read, points only at him. But evidence does not read itself. It is read
by people, and when a person in a white coat mistakes a guess for a measurement
and says it with enough authority, a jury will convict on it, whether the man in
the dock is Ted Bundy or a fourteen-year-old who gave a classmate a ride home.

That is the note this book has to end on, because it is the truth that makes all
the other chapters mean something. The forensic sciences that catch the guilty,
the cell towers and the Fitbits and the search histories and the tracklogs, are
genuinely powerful, and genuinely good, precisely to the degree that we hold them
honest. When we let them promise more than they can deliver, they do not stop
convicting people. They simply start convicting the wrong ones. Steven Truscott
was freed, in the end, by insects too small to lie and a court finally willing to
listen. He should never have needed either. The lesson of how not to get away
with murder is also, unavoidably, the lesson of how not to convict a boy of one:
follow the evidence, but never trust it more than it has earned.
