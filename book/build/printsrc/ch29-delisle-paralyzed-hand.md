# Chapter 29 - The Judge and the Paralyzed Hand

**A retired appeal-court judge told police his wife had shot herself. But a stroke
had left her right arm completely paralyzed, and the gunpowder was on her left
hand, in a pattern no suicide could have made. This is also the chapter where the
forensics, years later, came under suspicion themselves.**

Jacques Delisle had spent his career on the other side of the bench. He was a
retired judge of the Quebec Court of Appeal, the very court that would one day sit
in judgment of him, and in 2012 a jury convicted him of the first-degree murder of
his wife, Nicole Rainville. His account, given to police in his own home, was that
the seventy-one-year-old Rainville had taken her own life, and that he had no idea
what had happened.

The scene seemed, at a glance, to fit. Rainville lay on the living-room sofa, on
her back, bloodstained, a wound visible at her left temple. On the floor, level
with her left arm, was a .22-calibre Sterling handgun. A woman, a gun, a wound to
the head: the tableau of a suicide. But a staged suicide, like every staged scene
in this part of the book, is a story that has to survive contact with the victim's
own body, and Nicole Rainville's body told a story her husband's could not.

## What the left hand could not do

Two facts, set beside each other, dismantled the suicide.

The first was medical. Some time before her death, Nicole Rainville had suffered a
stroke. It had left her with a weakened right leg and a right arm that was
completely paralyzed. Whatever she could still do, she could not do with her right
hand. So if she had shot herself, she had done it left-handed.

The second was forensic. When the pathologist and the firearms experts examined
her, they found the telltale residue of a gunshot, the black smoke and embedded
gunpowder, on her left hand, with a smaller, fainter deposit near the left thumb.
The question, then, was narrow and answerable: was there any way for Nicole
Rainville to have fired that pistol into her own left temple, using her one working
hand, that would leave residue in that pattern? A firearms expert worked it
through and reached a flat conclusion. There was no position consistent with
suicide that would have left such traces on the left hand. Her wound, he
determined, could not have been self-inflicted.

A woman with one paralyzed arm, a wound to the temple, and gunpowder deposited on
her surviving hand in a way that no act of self-destruction could produce. The
suicide Delisle described was not merely unlikely. According to the science put
before the jury, it was impossible. He was convicted, and in 2013 the Quebec Court
of Appeal, his own former court, dismissed his appeal.

## The chapter's second, harder turn

This book is honest about its cases, and Delisle's does not end where a tidy
morality tale would like it to. Because the very forensic confidence that convicted
him, the certainty that the wound could not have been self-inflicted, later became
the subject of serious doubt.

Years after the appeal, the case was reviewed at the federal level, and in 2021 the
Minister of Justice, citing concerns that the forensic evidence about the gunshot
may have been overstated, ordered that Delisle's conviction be set aside and a new
trial held. The staged-suicide science that had seemed airtight in the courtroom
looked, on later scrutiny, less certain than it had been made to sound. And in
2024, rather than face that new trial, Jacques Delisle pleaded guilty to
manslaughter and, credited with the years he had already served, walked free.
*(That later history, the ministerial review, the ordered new trial, and the
manslaughter plea, comes from the record of the case after the 2013 appeal
anchored here, not from the appeal itself.)*

So what is Delisle, in the end? A wife-killer caught by the physics of a wound his
lie could not survive, or a man convicted on forensic certainty that turned out to
be softer than advertised, who then admitted enough to end the ordeal? The honest
answer is that the case has been, at different moments, told as both, and that is
exactly why it belongs here. The staged-suicide analysis in this part of the book
is a powerful tool. Delisle is the reminder that a tool that powerful must be
wielded with humility, because the same confident expert who exposes a real killer
can, in another case, sound just as sure and be reaching too far.

## How'd he get caught?

He was caught, first, by the gap between the death he described and the body that
described it back. Jacques Delisle asked the police to believe that a
seventy-one-year-old woman with a completely paralyzed right arm had shot herself
in the left temple, and the residue on her left hand said she had not, and could
not have. It was the physics of the wound, not any confession, that turned a
grieving widower into a convicted murderer. What makes his story different from the
others in this part is what came after: the discovery that the science which
seemed to catch him so cleanly was, itself, open to challenge. He was caught by
forensic certainty. Years later, that certainty was the thing put on trial.
