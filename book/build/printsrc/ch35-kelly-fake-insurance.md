# Chapter 35 - The Fake Insurance Investigator

**A man collected on his dead wife's life insurance, remarried, and moved a
province away, certain the cold case would stay cold. Then a friendly insurance
investigator named "Bob" showed up with a check for three thousand dollars and a
very tempting problem.**

On the twenty-first of November, 2000, Michael Kelly reported his common-law wife,
Judith Thibault, missing. Her body would not surface for almost four years, until
land surveyors working a remote stretch near Wolf Lake Road, in the District of
Thunder Bay, found her about ten feet off the road in the woods. She had been killed
by a single .22-caliber bullet to the back of the head, at the base of the skull, and
her body had been rolled in carpet and tied. There was no forensic evidence linking
Kelly to the killing. He was the prime suspect, and for years that was all he was.

The police kept three facts to themselves, the kind of details only the killer could
know: that the fatal shot was a single round to the base of the skull, that the body
had been wrapped and tied with white coaxial cable and yellow rope,
and the precise spot, ten feet east of Wolf Lake Road, where she lay. Those three
secrets were the lock. The problem was getting Kelly to hand over the key.

## Bob, and the check, and the beautiful problem

In 2009 the police launched an undercover operation and sent in an officer playing a
character named "Bob," a private investigator supposedly hired by an insurance
company to track Kelly down. Kelly, by then remarried and living in Alberta, was the
beneficiary of a small policy on Judith's life, three thousand dollars. Bob paid it
out, in cash, like a man simply doing his job. It was bait, and it was cheap.

Then Bob came back with a far more interesting story. He had discovered two more life
insurance policies, he said, worth a combined five hundred seventy-one thousand
dollars, with Kelly named as beneficiary. There was just one snag, and Bob showed him
an official-looking document to prove it: the policies had been flagged, held up for
nonpayment, because Kelly was suspected of murdering the insured. Bob was
sympathetic. He had an idea.

The idea, floated at their fourth meeting, was a con. Bob had a terminally ill
friend, "Donnie," a dying man with nothing to lose. If Kelly briefed Donnie on the
real details of the murder, Donnie could walk into a police station playing the
guilt-wracked confessor, take the blame, and clear Kelly's name, freeing up the
half-million in policies. For his trouble, Donnie would want a cut, and Kelly would
walk away rich. All Kelly had to do was tell a dying stranger exactly how Judith
Thibault died, so the false confession would ring true.

Kelly flew from Edmonton to Toronto on Bob's dime to meet Donnie in a hotel room. The
room was wired for sound and video. And this is the trap sprung on so many people who
think they are the ones running a scam: to make his fake confession convincing, Kelly
had to make it accurate. He denied involvement seven or eight times. Then, coached
that he was about to be set for life, he told Donnie how it was done, a single shot
to the back of the head, the body wrapped and tied three times around the middle with
yellow rope, and dumped off the road. He even handed over one of the victim's rings.

## The detail he was never supposed to know

Over fourteen more recorded phone calls, Kelly filled in the picture. He had shot her
"just the once," at the "base of the skull," made her "turn around" and shot her
"right in the back of the head square on," and wrapped her in carpet tied three times
with yellow "polyethylene rope."

Now set his confession beside the police holdback list. The single shot to the base
of the skull: correct, and secret. The number of shots: correct, and secret. The
yellow rope wound three times around the body: correct, and secret. None of it had
ever been released to the public. A man repeating a story he "gleaned from news
reports," as Kelly later claimed, cannot get the secrets right, because the secrets
were never in the news. He got them right because he was there. He did slightly
misdescribe the carpet, calling it blue when it was grey with darker spots, but as
the Court of Appeal drily noted, it had been dark when he grabbed it, and the honest
mistake only made the accurate details more damning.

When the police finally arrested him, Kelly did not bluster. He said, "it's finally
over." And then, meeting the undercover officers who had played Bob and Donnie, he
delivered the sentence that should be printed in the front of every con man's
handbook: "I thought I smelled a rat a long time ago but I let my greed get the best
of me."

He was convicted of first-degree murder. In 2017 the Court of Appeal for Ontario,
applying the strict new rules that govern these "Mr. Big" confessions, held that his
statement had been properly admitted and dismissed his appeal.

## How'd he get caught?

He was caught by his own greed, dangled on a string he was sure he was holding. The
whole operation worked because it flattered him into believing he was the
mastermind, running a scam on an insurance company through a dying patsy, when in
truth he was the mark. And the moment the scheme required his confession to be
convincing, it required it to be true, and a true account of Judith Thibault's death
contained three things only her killer knew: one shot to the base of the skull, and
yellow rope wound three times around a body left off Wolf Lake Road. Kelly thought he
was feeding a lie to a dying man. He was feeding the truth to a video camera. He
smelled the rat too late, and by his own admission, greed had already eaten him
alive.
