# Chapter 24 - The Car That Almost Testified

**Your car remembers the last five seconds before a crash: how fast you were
going, whether you braked, whether you were belted in. It is the most honest
witness to a fatal wreck. And in these two cases, the courts told the police they
could not read it without a warrant.**

Almost every chapter in this book is about a machine that testified against a
killer. This one is about two machines that were ready and willing to, and were
silenced, not because they lied, but because of how the police went about making
them talk. It is the counterweight the book needs, a reminder that the same
technology that convicts is powerful enough to frighten the law into guarding it.

The machine in question is the event data recorder, the "black box" tucked inside
nearly every modern car. It is always listening to the vehicle around it, and in
the instants surrounding a crash it freezes a record of the things that matter
most to a collision investigator: the speed, the throttle, the brake, the steering,
whether the seatbelts were buckled. After a fatal wreck, that little box holds the
closest thing to an eyewitness account that exists, an unblinking, mechanical
memory of exactly what the driver did in the seconds before someone died.

## Two crashes, two boxes, two silences

Charles Worsham was driving a car in a high-speed crash in Florida that killed his
passenger. The vehicle was impounded, and twelve days after the crash, the police
downloaded the data from its event data recorder. They did not have a warrant.
When they finally applied for one, four days later, it was denied, for the simple
reason that the search they wanted permission for had already happened. Worsham
was charged with DUI manslaughter and vehicular homicide, and he moved to suppress
the black-box data.

The state's argument was blunt and, it turned out, fatal to its own case: it
claimed Worsham had no privacy interest in the data at all, so that reading it was
not a "search" the Fourth Amendment even touched. In 2017 the Florida Fourth
District Court of Appeal disagreed and affirmed the suppression. There is, the
court held, a reasonable expectation of privacy in the information an event data
recorder retains, and downloading it from an impounded car without a warrant, and
without any emergency to justify skipping one, violated the Fourth Amendment. The
box had recorded the truth of the crash. The truth was thrown out.

A few years earlier and a continent away, a California case had run the same
course. George Xinos, intoxicated, struck and killed a pedestrian and fled the
scene, and was convicted of vehicular manslaughter. Central to the case was the
data police had pulled, again without a proper warrant, from the sensing and
diagnostic module in his car, the same kind of black box, holding the same kind of
last-seconds record. On appeal, the court held that the warrantless download was
not supported by probable cause and that admitting the module's data violated the
Fourth Amendment, and it reversed. *(A note of caution: the Xinos opinion was
later ordered depublished, meaning it cannot be cited as binding precedent in
California, though its reasoning remains a clear illustration of the principle.)*

## The witness the law makes wait outside

There is an irony at the heart of these two cases that is worth sitting with,
because it defines the boundary this whole book runs along.

In every other chapter, a device's memory is a gift to the prosecution: the
Fitbit that keeps counting, the phone that logs the towers, the car whose GPS
retraces the route to the body. Here the device's memory was every bit as
damning, arguably more so, because a black box does not deal in inference. It does
not merely place you near a crash; it tells the court how fast you were going and
whether you hit the brake. It is as close to a perfect witness as forensic
technology has produced.

And that is precisely why the courts flinched. A witness that reliable, that
total, that effortless to summon, is exactly the kind of power the Fourth
Amendment exists to keep on a leash. If the police can pull a complete record of
your final drive out of your impounded car whenever they like, with no warrant and
no judge, then the same box that convicts a drunk driver can be read on any of us,
for any reason, or none. So the law drew the line not at whether the machine could
testify, but at *how* it could be compelled to: with a warrant, through a judge,
the way the Constitution insists the most powerful searches be run. Both drivers'
black boxes were ready to tell the whole story. Both courts said the state had
skipped the step that lets a free country listen.

## How'd they get caught? And why didn't it stick?

They were caught, in the plain sense, by their own cars, which recorded their
speed and their braking and their failures with the loyalty of a machine that has
no idea whose side it is on. Charles Worsham's car knew what happened on that road,
and so did George Xinos's. The catching was never in doubt.

What these cases teach, and why they earn a place among the stories of killers
undone by evidence, is the flip side of every other chapter: that being recorded
and being convicted are not the same thing, and the gap between them is the law.
The event data recorder is the future this whole book is describing, a world in
which the objects around us witness everything and forget nothing. These two
decisions are the reminder that the future still has to come through the
courthouse door, and that a witness powerful enough to convict the guilty in an
instant is powerful enough that we should want a judge in the room before it
speaks. The car almost testified. The Fourth Amendment sent it home.
