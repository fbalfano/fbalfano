# Chapter 26 - No Gun, But the Bullets Matched

**The murder weapon was never found. It didn't have to be. The killer kept the
matching ammunition in his bedroom and photos of the rifle on his Facebook.**

Most shooting cases begin with a gun. This one never produced one, and it is a
useful reminder that a firearm leaves a great deal of itself behind even when it
walks away, in the round it fires, the ammunition it feeds on, and, increasingly,
in the photographs its owner cannot resist posting.

The story starts, as more and more of them do, with a fight arranged by phone. A
sixteen-year-old, whom the law shields behind the initials J.M.F. because he was
a young person, exchanged messages with the man who would become the victim.
Between the appellant's phone, the victim's phone, and their Facebook records,
investigators could later read the whole negotiation: the two had agreed to meet
that evening to fight, and they had set terms. No weapons. No backup. No police.

It was, in other words, supposed to be a fair fight, and exactly one of the two
young men showed up honoring the deal.

## Two gunshots on a borrowed phone

The victim did not come alone. He arrived with two other people, and one of them,
a person the record calls M.P., pulled out the victim's own cell phone and
recorded what happened next. The video is not the clean piece of evidence a
prosecutor dreams of. It shows the victim getting out of the vehicle and walking
toward a figure whose face cannot be made out in the dark. Then two gunshots crack
across the audio, and that is all the camera can tell you. It captured a murder it
could not identify.

What the camera couldn't supply, the physical evidence began to. At the scene and
on the autopsy table, the story sharpened: the victim had been killed with a
.22-calibre bullet. And a .22 is a common enough round that on its own it proves
little, which is why the next fact matters. When police later searched the
sixteen-year-old's bedroom, they found ammunition, and it was consistent with the
calibre and make of the round that killed the victim. No gun. But the food the
gun ate was sitting in his room.

Then his own phone finished the job the crime-scene video had started. Extracted
from the device and from his Facebook were images of at least three firearms, and
one of them was a rifle of a calibre, and taking ammunition, consistent with the
casing found at the scene, a gun that could have fired exactly the round that
killed a young man in the street. He had agreed to a no-weapons fight, brought a
gun, kept its diet in his bedroom, and kept its portrait on his social media.

The phone had one more thing to say, and it said it in his own words. After the
shooting, the appellant texted M.P., and although he made a point of trying to
delete the messages afterward, they were recovered, and they could be read to place
him at the scene and to have him admitting his involvement. Further messages
between the appellant and his sister, sent as the police were closing in, read as
a small anxious scramble to hide what he had done. A killer who deletes his
confession has at least grasped that the confession is a problem. He has simply
misunderstood, as so many in this book have, where deleted text messages go, which
is nowhere.

## The foyer

On that evidence a trial judge found the sixteen-year-old guilty of first-degree
murder. And then the case did something the previous paragraphs would not lead you
to expect: it fell apart, not over the ballistics, not over the confession, but
over a doorway.

The Facebook messages on the victim's phone had pointed police to the appellant,
and they obtained a warrant for the home where he lived with his mother. He got a
lawyer, and his counsel arranged for him to surrender at his father's house, with
the understanding that a lawyer would be present when he gave his statement at the
station. Two officers came to the father's home and were invited into the foyer.
There, in front of the young man's parents and sister, they told him he was under
arrest, and they did not advise him of his right to counsel or his right to remain
silent. Their reasoning, they later testified, was that the crowded little
entryway was no place for it, and that the privacy and control of the police
vehicle would be better. Before they got him to that vehicle, an officer asked
whether he had all his belongings, and specifically whether he had his cell phone.
The teenager first said he had gotten rid of it, then, seconds later, admitted it
was in the house but that he would not hand it over.

That small exchange, conducted before a child in custody had been told he could
stay silent or call a lawyer, is what unraveled the conviction. The Manitoba Court
of Appeal held that the statement had been obtained in breach of the appellant's
right to counsel under section 10(b) of the Charter, protections the law makes
especially robust for young persons precisely because they are young. Because that
tainted exchange led to the seizure of the phone and everything on it, the
statement and the phone's contents had to be excluded. Strip that evidence out,
the court reasoned, and you remove a great deal of what the trial judge had leaned
on. It did not necessarily mean there was too little left to convict; the
ammunition, after all, had come from a separate search. But it meant the whole
case had to be weighed again, fairly, the second time. In 2022 the court set the
conviction aside and ordered a new trial.

## How'd he get caught?

He was caught by the gun that wasn't there. A firearm can vanish into a river or a
friend's basement and never surface, but the sixteen-year-old had surrounded
himself with everything a missing gun implies: the matching ammunition in his
bedroom, photographs of a compatible rifle on his Facebook, and a set of
confession texts he trusted the delete button to erase. On the strength of it, a
judge convicted him. And then the same case became a lesson pointed in the other
direction, a reminder that the rules about how police may treat a suspect, above
all a child, are not technicalities to be brushed aside in a crowded foyer, and
that cutting the corner can cost the Crown the very conviction the evidence had
earned. He got caught by keeping the bullets. He got a second trial because the
police forgot the warnings.
