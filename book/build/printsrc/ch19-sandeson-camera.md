# Chapter 19 - The Camera He Installed Himself

**He wired his apartment with security cameras to protect his drug money. They
worked perfectly. That was the problem.**

William Sandeson was, by any reasonable measure, a young man with a future. In
August 2015 he was days from starting medical school at Dalhousie University in
Halifax, the kind of achievement that gets your photo in the hometown paper. He
was also, on the side, a drug dealer, which does not get your photo in the paper
until considerably later, and under a different headline.

His problem that August was a business meeting. Taylor Samson, a Dalhousie
physics student who lived nearby and moved weight in the same trade, had a large
quantity of marijuana to sell, and Sandeson had agreed to buy it for $40,000.
They set the meeting for the night of August 15, at Sandeson's apartment at 1210
Henry Street. Samson told his girlfriend he'd be back soon and left behind his
wallet, his keys, and his prescription medication, the small anchors of a man who
fully intends to return.

Sandeson's own security camera filmed Samson arriving at about 10:30 p.m.,
walking into the apartment carrying a large black duffel bag. That footage is the
last confirmed sighting of Taylor Samson alive. His body has never been found.

Here is the detail that should have given Sandeson pause, if criminals were the
pausing sort: the camera that filmed Samson walking in was his own. He had
installed it himself, to guard his cash and his product. It did its job
flawlessly. It just did the job for the wrong team.

## The apartment that talked

To grasp how thoroughly this crime documented itself, you have to meet the
neighbors.

Across the hall lived a man named Pookiel McCabe, who that evening had his friend
Justin Blades over, along with Sandeson. Sandeson slipped back to his own
apartment shortly after Blades arrived. Then Blades and McCabe heard a single
gunshot from across the hall, the kind of sound that reorganizes an evening.
Seconds later, Sandeson's security camera, still faithfully rolling, filmed him
appearing at McCabe's door in what both men described as a state of shock or
panic.

The two neighbors walked across the hall and looked into Sandeson's apartment.
What they saw, they later testified, was a man slumped over in a chair, and blood,
and cash, and drugs. They retreated, then came back a short time later, and Blades
saw streaks of blood leading from the chair to the bathroom. Sandeson said he had
to clean up. He asked Blades for the use of his car. Blades, to his eternal
credit, said no.

Now, Sandeson's cameras recorded to a DVR sitting in his own apartment, and the
DVR captured all of this coming and going with the diligence of a machine that
does not know what it is looking at. It filmed the neighbors leaving. And then,
in the single most suspicious act of stagecraft in the entire case, the recording
simply stops. The DVR did not record anything between roughly 11:30 p.m. on
August 15 and roughly 1:00 a.m. on August 16, which is to say it went dark for
exactly the ninety minutes a person would need to deal with a body and mop a
floor. A camera system that captured everything, right up until the precise
window when its owner would very much have preferred it hadn't. The gap did not
look like a coincidence. It looked like a confession with the middle torn out.

## Cleaning up on camera

If the missing ninety minutes were Sandeson's one moment of caution, the hours
around it were a festival of the opposite.

His girlfriend, Sonja Gashus, arrived around 12:30 a.m. and noticed the apartment
reeked of bleach, an observation Blades would echo later that morning. Sandeson
had, tellingly, told both his roommate and Gashus to stay away that evening,
clearing the theater before the show. At 2:52 a.m. he texted a fellow dealer to
report that Samson had run off with his money without handing over the drugs, and
he texted Samson's own phone complaining that Samson never came back "with that
stuff," building a paper alibi to a man he knew would never answer.

Then, in daylight, he went back to work in front of the lenses. Surveillance
video caught Sandeson cleaning out the trunk of his car on the morning of August
16. Forensics later found Taylor Samson's DNA in that trunk. On August 17,
another camera filmed him carrying garbage bags and other items out of his
apartment while wearing gloves, the universal costume of a man who has just
learned about DNA and is a day late applying the lesson. Cell-tower records then
tracked his phone to Truro, where his family keeps a farm, and investigators
found items there matching what he'd hauled out of the apartment, including a
shower curtain and a large duffel bag, both wearing Samson's DNA.

When Sandeson finally sat down with police, he offered a tidy story: he'd last
seen Samson days earlier, they'd arranged to meet on the 15th, but Samson simply
never showed. He even let officers photograph the texts on his phone, apparently
confident they backed him up. They did not. The messages made plain that the
planned deal was enormous and that Samson had, in fact, shown up exactly as
arranged, which is a hard thing to square with a man who never came.

A jury convicted William Sandeson of first-degree murder.

## The lawyer's ghost

The appeal, oddly, had nothing to do with the cameras, and everything to do with
a ghost in the defence's own camp.

It emerged that a private investigator working for Sandeson's defence team had
quietly been helping the police. Two key witnesses, the neighbors Blades and
McCabe, had at first told investigators they knew nothing useful. The defence PI
interviewed them, realized they knew a great deal, and then nudged the police to
re-interview them, even helping the officers locate and win the confidence of the
very witnesses whose testimony would help bury his own client. None of this was
disclosed to the defence. In fact, the police took steps to keep the
investigator's name confidential.

When it surfaced mid-trial, the defence moved instantly for a mistrial. The trial
judge found a disclosure breach but figured a short adjournment and some extra
cross-examination would patch it. In June 2020 the Nova Scotia Court of Appeal
disagreed and ordered a new trial, holding that the failure to disclose had
crippled Sandeson's ability to mount process-based challenges, and that the
police had been not passive bystanders but active partners in a common venture
with the defence's own investigator. The only fair remedy was a mistrial. *(At
the retrial that followed, Sandeson was again convicted, a result reported in the
news rather than in this decision.)*

## How'd he get caught?

He filmed it. Not on purpose, which is the whole point. William Sandeson spent
money and effort wiring his apartment with cameras to protect his cash, and in
doing so he built the most patient and honest witness in the case, one that
recorded Taylor Samson walking in with a duffel bag and never, on any of its
tireless hours of footage, recorded him walking out. Then Sandeson went and
cleaned his trunk, hauled out his gloved garbage, and reeked up his apartment
with bleach, all on or around the same lenses, adding chapter after chapter to a
story he thought he was erasing. He wanted a security system to catch anyone who
came to rob him. It caught the one person in the building he never thought to
watch: himself.
