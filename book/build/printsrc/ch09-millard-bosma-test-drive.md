# Chapter 9 - The Test Drive

**They wanted a truck and didn't much care who came attached to it. Tim Bosma
was chosen by his own online for-sale ad.**

Some victims are hunted. Tim Bosma was, in the most ordinary and heartbreaking
way imaginable, simply *shopped for*.

In April 2013 he did the thing thousands of people do every week: he posted his
truck for sale online. He was thirty-two, living just outside Ancaster, Ontario,
with his wife and small daughter, and the family had decided to part with his
Dodge 3500 diesel pickup. A buyer messaged, and a viewing was arranged for the
evening of May 6.

Two men turned up. The three went for a test drive, Dellen Millard at the wheel,
Tim in the passenger seat, Mark Smich in the back. It is the most unremarkable
scene in the world, a man showing strangers his truck, and it was the last thing
Tim Bosma ever did. He never came home.

What his family could not have known, and what a jury would later assemble from
the killers' own text messages, is that the test drive was not the beginning of
anything. It was the last step of a plan that had been running for months, a plan
that required a truck and was supremely indifferent to who owned it. Tim Bosma
did not stumble into a robbery. He answered a classified ad that a pair of killers
had, in effect, been waiting for someone to answer.

## The mission

The Court of Appeal set out the Crown's case in a single paragraph so damning it
is worth reading slowly, the way you'd read a shopping list found at a crime
scene, because that is more or less what it is.

For months before Tim Bosma died, Millard and Smich had been texting each other
about a "mission" to steal a truck. In the same messages they discussed acquiring
and preparing something considerably more sinister than a crowbar: a ten-foot-tall
animal incinerator. Millard, the aviation heir, kept it in the woods behind the
barn on his farm, and it came with the sort of brand name a Hollywood screenwriter
would reject as too on-the-nose. It was called **The Eliminator**. Other texts
showed Millard buying a .380-calibre handgun the year before, and a photograph
showed Smich cheerfully posing with it.

Line those facts up and the sequence turns your stomach. The gun, the incinerator,
and the "mission" all existed before Tim Bosma entered the story. He was not the
motive. He was the last item acquired, the truck's unlucky current owner, selected
the way you'd pick a listing off a screen, because he had the vehicle they wanted
and because he would, quite reasonably, come outside and climb in to show it off.

The night of May 6 was reconstructed from surveillance cameras that caught the
truck moving through the dark in pieces, like a flip-book with pages missing. The
footage placed both men inside the Millard Air hangar at 1:33 a.m. Eleven minutes
later, at 1:44, it captured a large flame blooming out of that same hangar. You do
not need the rest of the evidence to know what that fire was. But the rest of the
evidence came anyway, in piles.

## What was left

When police found Tim Bosma's truck, it was sitting at the home of Millard's
mother, which is not where careful men park the evidence. Tim's blood was sprayed
through the cab. The passenger window, his window, was shattered. Millard's
fingerprints were on the rear-view mirror and the driver's door. Inside the truck
were gunshot residue and a .380-calibre shell casing, the spent twin of the round
that started this.

Behind the barn on Millard's farm sat The Eliminator. Inside it were male human
bones. On its hatch was Tim Bosma's blood. The incinerator had done what it was
purchased to do, but a fire that erases a body does not erase the machine that
held it, and Millard had kept the machine.

The aftermath kept manufacturing evidence like a factory nobody could shut off.
After his arrest, Smich had friends fetch the murder weapon from a toolbox, then
buried the gun and refused to say where, a plan that works right up until a friend
decides to testify. From jail, Millard wrote letters to his girlfriend
instructing her on how to obstruct the investigation and pin the whole thing on
Smich, correspondence that did not stay private and became its own small disaster
at trial.

## Each pointing at the other

The trial ran six months, and its signature feature was the sight of two friends
turning on each other with the enthusiasm of men who had just realized only one
lifeboat remained. The lawyers call these "cut-throat defences," and the name has
never been more literal.

Smich took the stand and testified that Millard had brought the gun and shot
Bosma, and that he, Smich, had only helped destroy the evidence afterward because
he was "utterly shocked at everything," a shock that apparently did not prevent
him from being extremely helpful with the cleanup. Millard declined to testify at
all, but his lawyer's closing pointed the finger squarely back at Smich as the
triggerman. The jury was invited, in effect, to believe both men's accounts of
the other, which together added up to a complete confession with the names left
as an exercise for the reader.

In June 2016 the jury convicted both of first-degree murder. The appeals that
followed were fought almost entirely over the procedural chaos of that two-headed
defence, the character attacks, the rap lyrics admitted as evidence, the
inflammatory closings, rather than over whether they'd done it. In June 2023 the
Court of Appeal for Ontario worked through every ground, found most so weak it
didn't need to hear from the Crown, and dismissed both appeals. The evidence, the
Crown had said, was overwhelming. The court gave no sign of disagreeing.

## How'd they get caught?

They wrote it all down. That is the short answer and very nearly the whole one.
The motive, the "mission," the gun, the mail-order crematorium with the villain's
name on the side, all of it lived in text messages and photographs and a farm
calendar, a premeditated murder helpfully preserved in writing months before its
victim was even chosen. Then the physical world stepped forward to confirm what
the phones had already confessed: cameras caught the truck and the 1:44 flame; the
truck itself gave up Tim's blood, Millard's prints, and the shell casing; and The
Eliminator surrendered the bones and blood the fire couldn't reach. Two men had
planned this crime down to the machine that would dispose of the body, and it
never once occurred to either of them that a truck, a hangar, and a pair of
cellphones are all just witnesses that don't blink. Tim Bosma's family learned
what happened to him for one reason. His killers couldn't stop taking notes.
