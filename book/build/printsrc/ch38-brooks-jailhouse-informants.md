# Chapter 38 - The Word of Men Behind Bars

**Only two adults had access to the child who died in her crib. The physical evidence
pointed hard at one of them. But the words that helped convict him came from two
career informants behind bars, and that is the most dangerous evidence in this
book.**

A nineteen-month-old girl named Samantha Johnings was murdered in her crib. She was
found wrapped in a green comforter, and the injuries on her small body were terrible;
this is not a chapter that will describe them for effect. On the night she died, only
two adults had access to her: her mother, and her mother's partner, Frederick Brooks.
There is no lightness to be found anywhere in this case, and none will be attempted.
It is here because it teaches, more sharply than any other, both the power and the
peril of a killer's own words, and of the men who claim to have heard them.

## What the apartment kept

The physical evidence did not depend on anyone's testimony. Brooks's grey track pants
were recovered from the apartment, and they were stained with three things at once:
semen, blood of the same type as the murdered child, and a juice substance similar to
what was in her bottle. On the way to the hospital the morning after, Brooks leaned
toward the child's mother and whispered, three times, that he was sorry. He made other
statements the Crown treated as admissions of guilt. The forensic picture, the access,
the stained clothing, the whispered apology, all of it pointed in one direction.

That is the backdrop against which the contested evidence has to be understood,
because the physical proof was strong on its own. What made Brooks's case travel all
the way to the Supreme Court of Canada was not the track pants. It was two other men.

## The two men from the cells

The Crown also called two jailhouse informants, Stephen Balogh and Albert King, who
told the jury that Brooks had, in effect, incriminated himself to them behind bars.
The trial judge described them, without much varnish, as men of unsavory character.
And there lies the oldest problem in the criminal law's relationship with
confessions.

A jailhouse informant is a witness with a motive baked into his very situation. He is
already in custody; he often has charges or a sentence of his own to worry about; and
he knows, as everyone in the system knows, that delivering another man's confession to
the Crown is a currency that can buy leniency. That does not make every jailhouse
informant a liar. It does mean that his account is exactly the kind of evidence a jury
must be warned to handle with extreme care, because a man with a reason to invent a
confession is a man who sometimes invents one. Canadian law has a name for that
caution, a Vetrovec warning, a clear, sharp instruction from the judge that the jury
should regard such a witness's testimony with great suspicion and look for
independent confirmation before relying on it.

In Brooks's trial, the judge did not give the crisp, standalone version of that
warning, and that omission became the whole appeal. The Ontario Court of Appeal, by a
majority, ordered a new trial, holding that the jury should have been squarely warned
about the informants. When the case reached the Supreme Court of Canada in 2000, the
Court split, closely and revealingly, on that single question. A majority concluded
that the charge as a whole, taken with the strength of the other evidence, was enough,
that the trial judge's failure to give a textbook Vetrovec warning was not a legal
error requiring a new trial, and it restored Brooks's conviction. A dissent of three
judges disagreed pointedly, and would have upheld the order for a new trial, precisely
because the danger of unwarned jailhouse-informant evidence is too grave to excuse.

That the country's highest court divided on whether the jury was adequately cautioned
tells you everything about how treacherous this evidence is. The conviction stood, in
the end, because the physical proof was powerful and the charge, read fairly, conveyed
the danger. But the closeness of the vote is the lesson.

## How'd he get caught, and what should we take from it?

He was caught, first and most reliably, by what the apartment kept: track pants
carrying the child's blood type, her bottle's juice, and his own semen, and a
whispered apology repeated three times to her grieving mother. That evidence needed no
informant to speak for it. The words of the two men from the cells were added to the
pile, and they are the reason this case is remembered, not because they were the
strongest proof but because they were the most dangerous. Jailhouse informants sit at
the exact fault line of this part of the book: a killer's own confession is the most
compelling evidence there is, and a stranger's claim to have heard that confession,
delivered by someone with every reason to invent it, is the most easily faked. The
Supreme Court restored Brooks's conviction, but only after splitting over whether his
jury had been warned sharply enough about the men who claimed he had talked. Take that
as the caution it is. The word of a killer can hang him. The word of a man who says he
heard a killer must always be weighed with a hand on the scale.
