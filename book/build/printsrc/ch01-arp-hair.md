# Chapter 1 - The Man Who Handed Police the Hair That Hanged Him

**When the police asked Brian Arp for a few hairs to rule him out, he handed
them over. They were the very hairs that would tie him to a murder, and a rule
he thought was clearing him was quietly building his conviction.**

There is a particular kind of confidence that undoes people, the belief that
cooperation looks like innocence. Brian Arp had it, and this book opens with him
because his case is the purest illustration of a truth every chapter after it
will repeat in a different key: your own body keeps the receipts.

It begins in Prince George, British Columbia, a northern city with long winters
and long, dark drives out of town. At about 2:00 a.m. on November 22, 1989,
Marnie Blanchard was last seen standing alone outside a bar. A taxi driver
watched a small grey pickup, a Toyota or a Nissan, pull up beside her, its lone
driver a man with black, shoulder-length hair and some growth on the sides of his
face. Blanchard hesitated. Then she climbed in, and the truck drove off into the
dark.

Almost three weeks later, a cross-country skier came across a human jawbone in a
clearing some ten kilometres from downtown. Police found a skull and scattered
bones nearby; animals had been at the remains, and no cause of death could ever be
fixed. Her clothing lay in the clearing, some of it cut with a sharp blade, and
dental records gave the bones a name: Marnie Blanchard. The forensic pathologist
was blunt about what the scene meant. The cut clothing, the remote dumping ground,
the way the body had been left, none of it was consistent with a woman who had
simply died. This was homicide, dressed up as a disappearance.

## The hairs he gave away

The police had a witness's description and not much else, so they cast a wide net,
and somewhere in that net was a man named Brian Arp. When they asked whether he'd
be willing to help, he obliged, the way an innocent man might. He gave them scalp
and pubic hair samples so they could check whether any of his hair had turned up
where it shouldn't. This was 1990. DNA analysis of the samples was not even on the
table yet, and no one told Arp that the hairs he was handing over might one day be
read like a barcode and shown to a jury.

That is the quiet hinge of the whole case. Arp thought he was submitting to a
formality that would clear him. What he was actually doing was depositing his own
genetic signature into a police evidence locker, where it would wait, patient as a
seed, for the science to catch up.

The science took a few years, and it took a second body to bring it to bloom.

On February 13, 1993, Theresa Umphrey came to Prince George and spent the evening
drinking with friends. In the early hours of the 14th she was seen near a
convenience store, badly intoxicated, asking strangers for a ride home and unable
to tell them where home was. That afternoon her nude, partially frozen body was
found on a snowbank roughly fifty kilometres southwest of the city. Two women,
each last seen alone and vulnerable in the small hours of a Prince George night,
each driven out of town and left in the cold. The pattern was its own kind of
evidence, and the law has a name for it: similar-fact evidence, the principle that
two crimes bearing the same unusual signature are unlikely to be the work of two
different men.

But the pattern only names a type of killer. What named *this* one was the semen
recovered from Theresa Umphrey's body and her sweater. By now Arp had wised up, at
least a little; when police came asking for samples to test his DNA, he refused.
It no longer mattered. They still had the hairs he'd volunteered in 1990. And when
he sat for an interview and was allowed to smoke his own cigarettes, an officer
came back afterward and quietly bagged the butts he left behind, another little
deposit of himself, made without a thought.

## One in thirty-one billion

At the RCMP forensic laboratory, biologist Barbara Fraser laid Arp's contributions
side by side with the killer's: the pubic hairs he had handed over, the DNA off
his cigarette butts, and the semen from Theresa Umphrey. She found what the
scientists call a five-probe match, five separate regions of the DNA aligning
across all of it, an event so vanishingly rare between unrelated strangers that
she put the odds of it occurring by chance in the Canadian population at less than
one in thirty-one billion.

Thirty-one billion is a number with no real human meaning, which is precisely the
danger of it, and the reason Arp's case became a landmark. Because there is a
seductive trap buried in a statistic like that, one lawyers call the prosecutor's
fallacy: the temptation to hear "one in thirty-one billion chance of a random
match" and translate it, wrongly, into "one in thirty-one billion chance he's
innocent." The two are not the same sentence, and the Supreme Court of Canada used
Arp's appeal to explain, carefully, how a jury may and may not reason from a DNA
match and from similar-fact evidence, how much weight the numbers can bear before
they tip over into something misleading.

Arp was convicted of the first-degree murder of Theresa Umphrey, then re-arrested
and convicted of the first-degree murder of Marnie Blanchard, the woman whose case
had first prompted him to be so helpful. In 1998 the Supreme Court dismissed his
appeal, and in doing so gave the country its foundational rules for how DNA odds
are handled in a courtroom.

## How'd he get caught?

He caught himself, and he did it while trying to look innocent. Everything the
police needed, Brian Arp put into their hands personally: the hair he volunteered
to prove he had nothing to hide, and the cigarette butts he left smoldering in an
interview-room ashtray. By the time he grew cautious and started refusing DNA
tests, the caution was theater; his DNA was already on the shelf, waiting for a
second murder and a five-probe match to give it meaning. Two women were driven out
of Prince George into the dark on the assumption that the dark keeps secrets. It
does not, or at least not from the parts of ourselves we shed without noticing. A
man can refuse to give a sample. He cannot, it turns out, stop being made of
evidence.
