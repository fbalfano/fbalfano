# Chapter 21 - He Researched the Wound Before He Made It

**A man walked off a dark Florida beach with his wife shot dead and four bullet
wounds of his own, and a story about a stranger who ambushed them. Months
earlier, his computer had been asking exactly where on a body a gunshot does the
least harm.**

On an August night in 2002, on a dark stretch of Florida beach, April Barber was
shot to death near the water. Her husband, Justin Barber, survived, and he had a
story to tell: an unknown assailant had attacked them both in the dark. His wife
was killed. He himself had been shot four times and lived.

It was, on its face, a random horror, the kind of thing that makes people afraid
of empty beaches. But the state came to see it differently, and so, eventually,
did a jury, because the case against Justin Barber, though it never produced an
eyewitness or a confession, kept circling back to the same suffocating fact: he
had been preparing for this.

## A wholly circumstantial case

Barber's conviction is, in the language of the courts, "wholly circumstantial."
There was no witness who saw him shoot his wife, no confession, no smoking gun in
the literal sense. What there was, instead, was a constellation of facts that
pointed, together, in one direction, and Barber's appeal was essentially an
argument that the constellation was not bright enough to exclude the possibility
that a stranger really had done it.

The Florida appellate court walked through the pieces the state had been allowed
to put before the jury, and they read like a motive assembled in advance. There
were his extramarital affairs. There was a two-million-dollar life insurance
policy on the lives of Barber and his wife. And there was his computer, which
before April Barber died had been searching for a set of terms the court pointedly
allowed into evidence. The trial court had found the murder at least partly
motivated by pecuniary gain, and committed in a cold, calculated, and premeditated
manner. The jury recommended death; the judge imposed life.

The court's own description of the killing quietly dismantled the stranger story.
The evidence, viewed in the state's favor, was that Barber shot his wife at the
water's edge, causing her to fall into the water, and then moved her body to where
the police found it. Not a random ambush by a phantom in the dark. A killing
staged to look like one.

## The search that gave the game away

The detail that made this case infamous, and that gives this chapter its title,
concerns what Justin Barber's computer had been looking up before his wife was
shot. Widely reported from the trial record, the pre-murder searches read like a
man doing homework for exactly the crime that unfolded: queries about gunshot
trauma, about where on the body a bullet wound to the chest is survivable, paired
with searches touching on divorce. In other words, the theory went, before he
staged an attack in which he himself would be conveniently and non-fatally shot
four times, he had researched where a person can take a bullet and live.

*(A sourcing note: the appellate opinion confirms that the trial court admitted
evidence of Barber's affairs, the two-million-dollar insurance policy, and "his
computer search of the terms mentioned," and affirmed those rulings; the specific
content of the searches and the account of Barber's own four wounds come from the
extensive trial record and contemporaneous reporting rather than from the
appellate decision itself.)*

Put the constellation together and the shape is unmistakable. A husband with
affairs and a two-million-dollar reason to become a widower. A computer that
studied gunshot survivability before the shooting. A wife shot dead at the water's
edge and dragged into position. And a survivor whose own wounds, four of them, and
none of them fatal, fit the grim logic of a man who had looked up precisely where
to be shot. The appellate court held that this evidence was flatly inconsistent
with Barber's theory that someone else did it, and that the jury was entitled to
find every reasonable hypothesis of innocence excluded. It affirmed his conviction
for first-degree murder.

## How'd he get caught?

He was caught by his own preparation, preserved on the one device he should never
have used to plan it. Justin Barber built a story that required a stranger, a
dark beach, and a survivor too badly wounded to be a suspect, and he even took
four bullets to sell it. What he could not account for was the browser history
sitting on his computer, a record, made in advance, of a man studying how to be
shot without dying and contemplating the end of his marriage, next to a
two-million-dollar policy that made his wife worth more dead than alive. A staged
crime is a story about the future, carefully told. Barber's mistake was that he
had rehearsed that story in writing, in searches, before the night it was
supposed to come true, and a search bar, unlike a beach, remembers everything it
was ever asked.
