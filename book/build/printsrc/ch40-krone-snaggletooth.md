# Chapter 40 - The Snaggletooth Killer

**The same bite-mark science that caught Ted Bundy sent Ray Krone to death row.
The only difference was that Krone hadn't done it, and it took ten years and a
DNA test to prove what his crooked teeth could not.**

In the last chapter, a bite mark helped convict a genuine monster, and the
experts assured the jury the method was reliable. This chapter is what that
assurance costs when the man in the chair is innocent, and the bite happens to be
his.

Just before dawn on December 29, 1991, the owner of the C.B.S. Lounge in Phoenix
came to open up and found the front door unlocked and the lights still on. Inside,
in the men's bathroom, lay Kim Ancona, a bartender, nude and fatally stabbed.

The killer had left almost nothing. No fingerprints. No semen. All the blood at
the scene was Ancona's own. The saliva on her body came from someone with the
most common blood type on earth, which is to say from almost anyone. There were no
DNA tests, because this was 1991 and the technology was not yet what it would
become. The police had, in the whole of that ugly scene, exactly one piece of
evidence they thought could name a killer. Ancona had been bitten, on the neck and
the left breast, hard enough to leave the impression of teeth.

## A man with the wrong smile

Ray Krone was a suspect almost immediately, and not because of anything
scientific. He was a regular at the bar who had socialized with Ancona, his name
was in her address book, and the police had heard he was supposed to help her
close up the night she died. A witness recalled Ancona saying, hours before her
death, that she didn't need help closing because "Ray" was going to do it. It was
enough to bring him in. It was not, remotely, enough to convict him.

What the state needed was the bite, and Ray Krone had a mouth made for the
theory. His teeth were crooked and misaligned, distinctive enough that the press
would eventually saddle him with a nickname, the "Snaggletooth Killer," as though
his own smile had confessed. He gave the police a styrofoam impression of his
teeth, and it appeared to match the marks on Ancona. Casts were made. A comparison
suggested Krone had left the wounds. On that foundation, and very little else, he
was arrested two days after the murder. As the Arizona Supreme Court would later
put it with devastating economy: without the bite marks, the state arguably had no
case.

The rest of the evidence was the sort of thing that means nothing on its own. Some
material from the bar's shuffleboard table was found in Krone's car, which proves
he had been to a bar he was known to frequent. He was evasive with police about
his relationship with Ancona, which proves he was nervous, a thing innocent people
also are. Everything else could neither include him nor rule him out. The case, in
truth, was a set of crooked teeth and a coincidence of names.

## The tape

The trial turned on a piece of theater. Days before it began, the prosecution's
dental expert, Dr. Raymond Rawson, produced a videotape. It was not a static photo
of a bite next to a photo of teeth. It took Krone's dental casts, styrofoam
impressions, and CAT scans, and overlaid them, moving, onto the actual wounds on
Ancona's body, a slick, persuasive animation of guilt that no still exhibit could
match.

The defense found out about the tape on the last business day before trial. Krone's
lawyer watched it for the first time on a Sunday; the trial started Monday. He
asked the court to keep it out, or failing that, to give him time to prepare a tape
of his own. The court refused. Dr. Rawson played the tape for the jury and built
his testimony around it, and the defense, blindsided, had no answer of equal
wattage. The court-appointed dental expert the defense might have called had no
experience with video and was leaving town.

The jury convicted Ray Krone of first-degree murder and kidnapping. Because of the
"especially heinous or depraved" nature of the crime, and finding that his clean
record and his honorable discharge from the United States Air Force were not enough
to spare him, the court sentenced him to death.

## Reversal, and then the truth

In 1995 the Arizona Supreme Court reversed the convictions and ordered a new
trial. Its reasoning was not, yet, that Ray Krone was innocent; it was that the
last-minute ambush with the videotape had denied him a fair chance to defend
himself, a serious enough violation to undo the verdict. The court noted, pointedly,
how thin the rest of the case was. Strip away the bite marks and there was
almost nothing left.

That should be the end of a tidy story, but the truth took longer and cost more.
Krone was tried a second time. And a second jury, shown the same kind of
bite-mark evidence, convicted him again. He went back to prison, and the years
kept passing, more than a decade of them, a man caged for a murder committed by
someone else on the strength of the shape of his teeth.

What finally freed him was the science that had not existed when Kim Ancona died.
In 2002, DNA testing of the biological evidence on the victim excluded Ray Krone
and identified another man, one already in the state's prison system for an
unrelated sex crime. Krone walked out after ten years, more than three of them on
death row, and became, by the count kept of such things, the hundredth person
exonerated from an American death row. *(The DNA exoneration and the identification
of the true killer come from the case's later history and the record of Krone's
release, not from the 1995 decision anchored here.)*

## How'd he get caught? And why is that the wrong question

For Ray Krone, "how'd he get caught" has a grotesque answer: he was caught for a
murder he did not commit, by a bite mark that pointed at his unusual teeth and a
forensic method dressed in the authority of science. The bite-mark comparison in
his case was not meaningfully different from the one that helped convict Bundy a
few years earlier and a few states away. Same discipline, same confidence, same
persuasive experts. The only variable was guilt, and the technique could not tell
the difference, because it was never really able to tell anyone apart at all. Bundy
was guilty and the bite marks got the right man. Ray Krone was innocent and the
bite marks got him anyway. A science that convicts the guilty and the innocent with
equal certainty has not identified a killer. It has only identified a defendant. And
the next chapter belongs to a man who waited even longer to learn that difference.
